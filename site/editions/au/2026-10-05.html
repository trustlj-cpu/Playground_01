Chalmers warns the interest bill is rising as bond yields climb worldwide. The cash rate is already 4.60%

Speaking in Japan, the Treasurer said Commonwealth bond yields are at 15-year highs and debt interest now costs more than the PBS. The sharemarket finished flat on a thin, holiday Monday.

Treasurer Jim Chalmers used a speech to the Australia–Japan Joint Business Conference in Chiba on Monday to deliver a blunt message about the national accounts. Commonwealth bond yields are at their highest in 15 years, he said, and because cheap debt issued in earlier years is maturing and being refinanced at today's rates, the annual interest bill will keep climbing. He noted that interest payments already cost more than the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme. He placed the problem in a global setting: Australian 10-year yields reached a 15-year high within the past month, American 10-year yields a 24-year high and Japanese 10-year yields their highest in about three decades. Even a country with Japan's asset base, he said, will have to reckon with higher financing costs. He argued that budget settings were not the main driver of prices.

The speech followed an interview on ABC's Insiders on Sunday in which Chalmers said a savings package will feature in the mid-year budget update, due in mid-December, and played down the prospect of more cost-of-living relief. The Reserve Bank lifted the cash rate to 4.60 per cent on September 29, its fourth increase this year and the highest level in about 15 years. Governor Michele Bullock said the board did not take the decision lightly. The next decision falls on November 3, Melbourne Cup Day, and the governor has said the bank is in no rush. Markets have been unsettled. On October 1 the sharemarket shed about $60 billion in value as bond yields jumped, and ABC's The Business described a week of big swings that still ended with the S&P/ASX 200 up 0.2 per cent at 8,682.

Monday was quieter. New South Wales, the ACT and South Australia were on a Labour Day holiday, and the S&P/ASX 200 closed at 8,686.4, up 4.3 points, led by miners and healthcare. BHP gained 1.1 per cent and Cochlear 3.96 per cent. The Australian dollar bought about 69.4 US cents. Bank of America's head of rates strategy, Mark Cabana, met Australian super funds and told them that if United States rate expectations move into the high 4s or mid 5s per cent, tighter financial conditions could weigh on the offshore assets that make up about half of institutional super portfolios. That is an analyst's warning, not a forecast.

The bond market has been the story for weeks. ABC reported on September 1 that the 10-year Commonwealth yield had reached 5.16 per cent, the highest since April 2011, and on September 27 described the largest market correction in generations, with yields at two-decade highs. On Monday the AFR reported that banks are lifting fixed rates faster than the Reserve Bank as their own funding costs deepen. In the same paper, one columnist argued the government has squandered a global budget boom, and another reported that even Labor's think tank believes government spending feeds inflation. Those are opinions, but they show where the argument is heading: whether the pressure on the budget comes from markets or from policy.

The Coalition is set to promise a migration cut of about two-thirds, in a bid to head off One Nation

The Sydney Morning Herald and the Australian Financial Review reported on Monday that Angus Taylor will take a much smaller migration target to the Coalition's next policy release, undercutting Labor's goal of 225,000 a year by 2028. Net overseas migration was 292,100 in the year to March, so a cut of two-thirds would imply a figure near 100,000. That arithmetic is ours, not a published number. The final figures had not been released at press time.

The move comes as One Nation climbs in the polls and Pauline Hanson's party prepares to base itself in a Brisbane office bought by Gina Rinehart, reported by the Guardian and The Age.

Labor's own position was set only weeks ago. On September 17 Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke announced tighter student and working-holiday visa rules, including a ballot for backpackers seeking to extend their stay, in a plan to bring net overseas migration to 225,000 a year by 2028. The Guardian asked on the weekend whether slashing migration would tank the economy or reset living standards, looking to Canada's recent cuts for clues. The Opposition's answer, if the reports are right, is that the economy can cope with far less.

Australia's counter-terrorism team probes the flydubai co-pilot accused of attacking his captain with an axe

Police said on Sunday that Victoria's Joint Counter Terrorism Team, made up of the Australian Federal Police, Victoria Police and ASIO, is investigating the man's links to Australia. The co-pilot of flydubai flight FZ1073, bound from Dubai for Israel last Wednesday, allegedly attacked the captain with an emergency crash axe. The United Arab Emirates prosecutor-general called it a terrorist attack. Passengers and crew subdued the man, a reserve pilot took the controls and the aircraft made an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia.

Reuters, citing sources briefed on the inquiry, said he studied at a Melbourne university around a decade ago. On Monday RMIT confirmed that he had been enrolled but did not finish an engineering qualification. The Financial Times, reported by the AFR, said he had visited radical mosques while in Australia. Police have not confirmed that and have set out no motive.

Joint counter terrorism teams exist in every state and territory and bring together the Australian Federal Police, state police and ASIO, so that intelligence and criminal investigators work from the same file. Their involvement does not mean charges are likely in Australia: the alleged attack happened on a foreign airline, and the Australian inquiry is looking only at what the man did, and whom he met, while he lived here. AP reported that his own country had stopped him flying over concerns about extremist views before the flight.

Roosters win the grand final 19–18 after a 12–0 deficit, with a Daly Cherry-Evans field goal

Sydney beat Newcastle 19–18 at Accor Stadium on Sunday night before 80,814 people, for their first premiership since 2019 and a 16th overall. The Knights led 12–0 early, and 12–6 at the break after a Mark Nawaqanitawase try. The Roosters edged ahead in the second half and Cherry-Evans, 37, kicked a field goal from about 20 metres to make it 19–12. Newcastle scored late, converted, and ran out of time. James Tedesco was reported to have won the Clive Churchill Medal.

Cherry-Evans said he had been chasing that feeling his whole career; his previous title was with Manly in 2011. The Roosters also won the NRLW decider, 30–6 over Brisbane. By Monday the AFR and the Herald were reporting that the AFL's grand final had retaken the television ratings crown from the NRL's.

The game had its drama. The Herald singled out a Kalyn Ponga try that had Newcastle's supporters on their feet, and late in the match Greg Marzhew scored the try that brought the Knights within a point. Knights coach Justin Holbrook said he had never watched a harder game of football. Roosters coach Trent Robinson said the club's slogan is that it plays for premierships, and that the players had felt it. The Herald and The Age both ran reports on celebrations that carried on through the night, including a party in Point Piper.

The card surcharge ban is a week old. Cafes lift menu prices, private schools drop card payments and renters wait to see the fallout

Since October 1 businesses can no longer add a surcharge to payments by EFTPOS, Visa or Mastercard debit, credit and prepaid cards. The Reserve Bank has also cut the cap on domestic credit-card interchange fees, which banks charge merchants, to 0.3 per cent, with further changes to follow on April 1, 2027. Surcharges that apply equally to every payment method, such as weekend and public-holiday loadings under state rules, and discounts for cash remain allowed. Sources differ on American Express, which is outside the rule; some say it is dropping surcharges voluntarily.

The first week of reporting is anecdotal. The AFR says cafe and restaurant prices are rising as the ban hits operators and that private schools are abandoning card payments; the Herald reports that some renters who paid card surcharges on rent will be left in the lurch. Whether the one-off menu reset adds to inflation is contested: one Herald commentary argues the ban 'feeds more inflation'. Nobody has yet measured it, and the next CPI release is not due until later this month.

Behind the change is a long argument about who pays for the card system. Surcharges were meant to signal the true cost of cards, but they had grown past that cost, often to 1 or 2 per cent of the bill. The Reserve Bank's consultation paper warned that cutting the fees banks earn could lead to scaled-back rewards programs, shorter interest-free periods or higher annual card fees. For businesses the choice is now to absorb the fee, raise prices for everyone or encourage cash and debit, which is the course some private schools have taken.
