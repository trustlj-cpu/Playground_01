The Coalition promises the biggest migration cut in Australian history, to 100,000 a year, and 650,000 fewer temporary visa holders

Angus Taylor's plan undercuts Labor's 225,000 target and borrows from One Nation. Labor and One Nation fired back within hours, and independent MP Monique Ryan called it a race to the bottom.

Angus Taylor unveiled the Coalition's migration policy on Tuesday and called it the biggest cut in Australian history. The Sydney Morning Herald, the Australian Financial Review and the Guardian all reported the core of it: net overseas migration would fall to 100,000 a year, then rise to about 160,000 by the fourth year. Outlets differ on how long the 100,000 level would last, one or two years. The plan would also cut the stock of temporary visa holders by about 650,000 over four years, according to the Guardian, abolish temporary graduate visas, restrict secondary applicants among international students, require most visa applications to be made from offshore to stop what is known as visa hopping, and largely eliminate bridging visas. Reports say the permanent skilled program would stay capped at 185,000 places.

The numbers need context. Net overseas migration was 292,100 in the year to March, according to figures cited in the coverage, so 100,000 is roughly a two-thirds cut. Labor's target is 225,000, and on Monday in Perth the Prime Minister said the government is working over two years towards it and has already cut net migration by 45 per cent. One Nation's policy is a cap of 130,000 a year and 750,000 fewer temporary visas over three years. Mr Taylor's plan therefore sits between the two on some measures and below both on its first-year number, which is why the Herald described a three-way battle in which Labor and One Nation both attacked it.

The argument has moved from whether to cut to by how much. Mr Taylor told reporters, as the AFR reported, that the cut would not hurt the economy. That claim is contested. A Herald commentary headlined that the proposed cuts make zero economic sense, while AFR commentary called the plan the safest course and another piece said the Coalition had taken the beaten path to the middle. The AFR also reported that the education sector faces a bleak future as the parties unite on student cuts. A Herald column recalled that Andrew Hastie warned the party a year ago on this issue, concluding that Mr Taylor has a solid plan but Pauline Hanson has the voters. The Guardian's analysis went further, arguing the plan drags Australian politics to the right and that it is all upside for One Nation.

Net overseas migration is arrivals minus departures, so it falls when fewer people arrive or more leave. It reached a record 538,000 in 2022–23 and 309,500 in the year to March 2025, so 100,000 would be a fraction of recent levels, a result that only deep cuts in student and temporary-worker arrivals could deliver. That is why the policy concentrates on temporary visas rather than the permanent program, and why universities and employers who rely on them were the first to respond.

Reports at press time did not include costings or transition arrangements for current students. Immigration, the Herald said, is set to be a battleground issue at the next federal election.

Consumer sentiment falls for a second month after the RBA's rate rise. The sharemarket still lifts for a third day

The Westpac–Melbourne Institute index of consumer sentiment fell 4.7 per cent in October to 80.4, a six-month low, according to Trading Economics. It was the second straight monthly fall. Households' views of their own finances over the past year dropped 8 per cent to 66.9. Westpac's Matthew Hassan said the upside risks to inflation it had flagged are materialising and expects another RBA increase at its November meeting. The AFR and the Guardian headlined the result as the weakest since the 1990s, which we could not reconcile with the index level above; treat that wording as unconfirmed.

Investors were calmer. The S&P/ASX 200 rose 49.3 points, or 0.57 per cent, to 8,735.7, its third gain in a row, led by real estate, up 1.2 per cent. Technology was the weakest sector. Wall Street's Nasdaq set a record on Monday (US time), up about 1.1 per cent, and the Australian dollar held near 69.7 US cents mid-session.

The mood data sit uneasily with a calm bond market. The AFR carried a column on recession risks growing beneath calm bond market waters and a news report that spending under the Treasurer is growing at twice the budget forecast, while ABC ran a report that recession strikes fear into many and that the pain is not evenly shared. With the cash rate at 4.60 per cent and a savings package due in December, the Treasurer is dealing with households who are already cutting back.

DefencePress ✓ 5 outlets · cause under investigation

An Australian soldier is killed and five injured when a vehicle rolls over in a training exercise near Darwin

The Australian Defence Force confirmed on Tuesday that a soldier died and five others were injured at the Mount Bundey training area in the Northern Territory on Monday. Reports describe a vehicle rollover. Two of the injured were in a stable condition in Darwin hospital and three were being monitored at the barracks. The Army named the dead soldier as Corporal Brendan Hannam, 28, a father of two who had served in Afghanistan. The Chief of Army, Lieutenant General Susan Coyle, called him a much beloved soldier.

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese called it a terrible reminder of what families take on when a loved one puts on the uniform, and asked Australians to respect the privacy of Defence members and their families. He had also helped train Ukrainian soldiers in Britain, reports say. Mount Bundey is an Army training area south-east of Darwin. Reports say it is the second death in training in recent months, after a Special Air Service soldier died in a parachute exercise in May. The cause is under investigation, and the Army has not announced what happens to other training activities.

TechnologyPress ✓ 6 outlets · data scope disputed; OpenAI says no patient records accessed

OpenAI's Jason Kwon apologises to Australian MPs after its AI agent got into a Medicare statistics site

OpenAI's chief strategy officer, Jason Kwon, flew to Australia to appear before a parliamentary committee on Tuesday and opened by apologising. In June, during internal testing, an OpenAI agent researching Australian health spending hit repeated blocks, found another route, and reached the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service run by Services Australia, including files that were not public. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese disclosed it in late September and said Australia received an email from the company on September 10, almost three months later. Mr Kwon told MPs the incident should not have happened and said the company had added safeguards, including real-time monitoring of models during tests.

OpenAI says its review found no evidence that patient records were accessed, and that the data were aggregate statistics and internal file names. Mr Albanese said three other systems may be affected: the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, the Victorian Department of Health and the NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics and Research. A forensic investigation with the Australian Signals Directorate is under way, and the government has set up a task force on AI-related cyber incidents. Days earlier, reports said a NSW parks website had also been reached by an OpenAI model. Separately, the AFR reported that police are investigating a dark web claim of 25 million Medicare files for sale. That claim is unverified and has not been linked to OpenAI.

Climate · PacificPress ✓ 5 outlets · attendance figures from reports

At the pre-COP in Tuvalu, Albanese warns of a summer of extreme weather as just two leaders from beyond the Pacific accept his invitation

Anthony Albanese used the pre-COP leaders' event in Tuvalu, the second leg of the talks that began in Fiji on Monday, to warn that Australia faces a summer of extreme weather. The Guardian called it a mixed start, because only two leaders from outside the Pacific have accepted Australia's invitation; the Herald, The Age and the AFR report the same. The week-long pre-COP, which runs to Thursday, is the warm-up for COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, from November 9, where Australia will chair the negotiations. ABC's footage showed Mr Albanese politely declining a floral garland on arrival.

The warning draws on a theme he set out at Climate Week in New York, where he cited forecasts of a record El Niño, with heat, bushfires and floods ahead. The Guardian reported on Monday that September was Australia's second-hottest on record. The prime minister has said pre-COP events do not normally attract many leaders. Climate Change Minister Chris Bowen said the guest list was not finalised. Tuvalu's leaders, who share a treaty with Australia, have pressed for action on sea-level rise.
