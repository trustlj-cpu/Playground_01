IMF puts Australia among the worst in the rich world for rising unemployment and inflation, as the RBA keeps more rate rises on the table

The Fund’s forecasts have the economy still growing next year while more people lose work. The Reserve Bank’s minutes say further increases are possible “if required”, and the Fund’s oil scenario shows how a worse Gulf crisis would reach the bowser.

Australia is on course for one of the sharpest rises in unemployment, and one of the highest inflation rates, in the developed world, according to the International Monetary Fund’s latest forecasts, as reported on Tuesday by the Australian Financial Review, Nine.com.au, the Sydney Morning Herald and The Age. The Herald and The Age went furthest, headlining the “biggest jobless rate spike” among advanced economies. The AFR and Nine.com.au said “among the worst”, so that is the wording we treat as confirmed. All of them described the same shape: the economy keeps growing next year, but more people are out of work. We did not see the forecast figures in two independent sources, so we quote none.

The Fund’s prescription, in The Australian’s headline, is a “renewed focus” on debt and deficits as the key to fighting inflation. The Reserve Bank has been raising rates to fight the same problem, and its board minutes, published on Tuesday, kept the door open. News.com.au, the Daily Telegraph and NT News headlined them “‘If required’: RBA’s grim rates warning”, and CommBank’s summary said they leave the bank open to further rises if required. The Australian said the minutes offered a clue to the November decision, and the AFR reported the bank saying weaker growth must be accepted to defeat inflation. Read beside the IMF’s jobless forecast, that is the trade-off in plain view.

Fuel is the second channel. The Guardian reported that the IMF’s outlook includes a scenario in which global oil prices jump to US$130 a barrel over coming months, which it said could add another 30 cents a litre to Australian petrol prices. That is a stress test, not a forecast; the Guardian framed it as showing how a worsening Middle East crisis could affect growth and inflation. The crisis was in the news on Tuesday. Houthi attacks had closed Riyadh’s airport (see Inside), and both Bloomberg and ABC said oil was rising on supply worries.

Abroad, the Fund’s financial-stability work named risks rather than numbers. Bloomberg quoted a senior IMF official calling a surge in government bond yields the biggest emerging risk to the global financial system. Reuters reported that the Fund also flagged a correction in AI-linked share prices, war and debt, while noting that the AI boom had lifted world trade, to the benefit of China and India. Tuesday’s market moves, described in the story below, were a live test of the first two.

Two cautions. These are projections built on assumptions about oil, interest rates and the AI investment cycle, and they are revised every few months. And the headlines compare increases, not levels: a large rise from a low starting point can top a league table without producing the highest jobless rate in it.

Politics is already attached. Jim Chalmers told progressives not to sneer at One Nation voters because Labor cannot count on the party fading, ahead of what the Herald’s headline called 12 months of pain (SMH, Brisbane Times, ABC, The Australian, The Conversation). We did not see him answer the IMF figures directly, and we did not find the Opposition’s reply.

Where outlets agree: the IMF sees Australia among the weakest rich economies for jobs and prices; the RBA minutes leave more rises possible; and the Fund’s global warnings are about bond yields and AI valuations. Resting on one outlet each: the “biggest” wording (the Nine papers) and the oil-scenario arithmetic (the Guardian).

Albanese calls a livestreamed US execution “abhorrent” as eSafety says it cannot block it in advance

Anthony Albanese said the government is exploring all options to stop Australians seeing a planned livestream of a US execution, calling it “abhorrent”, ABC and the Daily Telegraph reported on Tuesday. The execution is that of Nidal Hasan, the former Army major convicted of the 2009 Fort Hood killings. ABC said Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has said he will make sure the public can watch on 3 December, and The Conversation described the method as a firing squad. CBS News, The Hill and Politico reported the Pentagon’s plan and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s defence of it.

ABC reported that the eSafety Commissioner’s office has no power to block a livestream proactively, although platforms must protect users from harmful material. The Guardian said Julie Inman Grant may be able to limit what Australians see, at the risk of a diplomatic incident with Washington. The Greens called the plan “barbaric”, and ABC said Barnaby Joyce backed a ban. In the items we saw, the government had not said which of its options it prefers.

The debate runs alongside another case. A US inmate, Christa Pike, survived two lethal-injection doses and was taken to hospital; emergency-call recordings obtained by the Associated Press showed confusion among officials (ABC, Guardian, BBC).

Australia win the first Test in Durban as Hazlewood seals it and Renshaw’s comeback takes the headlines

Australia beat South Africa in the first Test in Durban, with Josh Hazlewood’s wicket sealing the win, the Nine papers and the Guardian reported on Tuesday. Australia began the final day at Kingsmead needing six wickets (ABC, SMH, The Age). The Guardian credited Hazlewood with figures of 6-49 as South Africa were seen off; that is one outlet, and the winning margin was not in the headlines we saw. Australia had set a target of 421 on day four, after Pat Cummins’ big hitting, as ABC and CODE Sports reported then.

The individual story was Matt Renshaw, back in the side for what the Nine papers called his comeback Test. Keshav Maharaj’s fingertip catch ended his second-innings innings just short of a century (ABC, Nine papers). Renshaw then took his first Test wicket, South Africa’s captain Temba Bavuma, and the Nine papers reported Cummins saying he could open in the next Test.

The cloud is Steve Smith. He was hit on the helmet while batting, and Australia are testing him for delayed concussion (ABC, SMH), with the Herald saying the team must wait to learn whether he misses the next Test or even the series. ABC said the blow adds to the questions about the side for the second Test. The Nine papers also said the criticism of the selectors after Nic Maddinson’s duck has eased. We did not see a date for the second Test.

Competition watchdog sues Sunny Queen and 11 other egg producers, alleging a cartel that held back competition for years

The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission has started court action against Sunny Queen and 11 other egg businesses, alleging they coordinated on production and supply decisions, ABC, the Sydney Morning Herald, The Age, the Guardian, Bloomberg and The West Australian reported on Tuesday. ABC said the regulator alleges the conduct likely reduced competition substantially for many years. The Herald and The Age said the producers are alleged to have manipulated the market for at least six years, potentially driving up what consumers pay, and their headline called Sunny Queen the country’s biggest egg supplier.

These are allegations; the ACCC must prove them in court, and the companies’ replies were not in the items we saw. The summaries did not give the court, the penalties sought or any figure for what households paid. In competition law, cartel conduct means rivals agreeing among themselves instead of competing on things like price, output or who supplies whom. As background from our own knowledge, not from Tuesday’s reports, it carries civil penalties for companies and can be a crime for individuals.

For shoppers, the practical effect today is limited: nothing in the coverage says egg prices will change as a result. The case matters more as a test of how the regulator treats coordination among farm producers, and we did not see how the companies will defend it.

Chip shares keep sliding: Seoul’s Kospi sinks more than 5%, the ASX ends about flat, and Wall Street’s banks report into a bond rout

Technology and memory-chip shares extended a global sell-off on Tuesday. South Korea’s Kospi index sank more than 5% on a semiconductor sell-off driven by worries about the AI boom, AP reported. Bloomberg said Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix fell further on concerns about Chinese rivals and a stretched AI rally, and Reuters headlined “Asian stocks dive on AI jitters ahead of US bank earnings”. The cue came from Wall Street: Monday, the last completed US session in our window, was mixed near a record, with the Dow higher and the S&P 500 struggling (AP, AFR), while Bloomberg said a chipmaker sell-off was weighing on sentiment. Closing levels were not in our sources.

In Sydney the S&P/ASX 200 recovered to end roughly flat, with banks lifting and AI and lithium stocks tumbling, Market Index reported; Business Recorder had earlier described shares slipping on miners and tech with the RBA minutes in focus. That is a single-source wrap and we have not confirmed a closing level, so the ear above shows a dash.

Later on Tuesday, Reuters reported US chip stocks extending their slide on a China memory report and AI-spending jitters, as the first big US banks reported: JPMorgan with a rare profit miss, Goldman Sachs beating estimates, Citigroup topping them, and Wells Fargo reporting a rise in profit. Those were intraday events, not a close; the US session was still under way at our cut-off.

Underneath sits the bond market. The Financial Times said investors were piling into government bond funds at record rates after yields hit their highest in decades. Bloomberg said big US bank shares were in correction territory as yields surged, and, as in the lead story, the IMF named a surge in bond yields as the biggest emerging risk to the financial system. A FinancialJuice relay of Bank of America’s fund-manager survey, one aggregator, put a disorderly rise in yields as the biggest tail risk, followed by AI.

A chip rout and a bond rout are different forces that happen to coincide, and both feed back to Australian shares through the banks, miners and tech names. Headlines alone cannot say which will dominate.
