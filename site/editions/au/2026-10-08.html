Firmus scrambles to save Australia's second-biggest share float as investors balk. The ASX falls 0.8%

The AI data-centre company has reportedly cut its price from $11 to $8.25 as the book closes early, and some reports say the float could be pulled. The sharemarket lost 66.8 points as oil jumped on Iran tensions.

Firmus, the Nvidia-backed AI data-centre company, spent Thursday trying to rescue what would be the second-largest share float in Australian history behind Telstra's in 1997. The ABC reported that the nation's second-largest ASX float was in doubt as investors got cold feet, and that the company was trying to save the offer as interest underwhelmed. The Australian Financial Review said the banking syndicate began price talks on Wednesday after overseas demand came in weaker than expected, and that the bankers were trying to shrink an offer first reported at $7.9 billion. The Sydney Morning Herald wrote that investors did not buy the hype, and the Guardian said the high-flying valuation may be coming back down to earth.

The numbers are moving, and the reports differ. Reuters had earlier reported an $11 price and a raise near $7.1 billion, valuing Firmus at about $44 billion. By Thursday several outlets, including the New Zealand Herald, reported that the price had been cut to $8.25 and that the book had closed a day early, which would cut the valuation by roughly 30 per cent to about $30 billion. The New Zealand Herald said the float could be pulled altogether. We saw no announcement of a withdrawal or a revised timetable by evening, and the ABC noted the company did not appear on the exchange's list of upcoming floats. We therefore treat the revised price as reported, not confirmed.

The AFR kept up a stream of reports under headlines that included a hedge fund finding 30 red flags, the company pulling down its deal documents, bankers' emails that raise serious questions, and a 36-hour spiral of doom. The paper also reported that Maas Group, which has about $1.2 billion of AI contracts linked to the float, was exposed, and its shares fell. Investors were said to be worried that about half the book was going to existing holders, that more than half the stock would be freely tradable at listing, and that 42.4 per cent of the register would sit in escrow.

The wider market was weak for other reasons. The S&P/ASX 200 closed down 66.8 points, or 0.77 per cent, at 8,660.9, according to ABC and Bloomberg, with miners hit as oil rose. Brent crude was up about 2 per cent near $102 a barrel on worries about supply as attacks on shipping in the Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz increased. The index is more than 6 per cent below its early-August peak. Lovisa and Weebit Nano were among the worst performers, down 8.7 and 11.0 per cent.

Bond markets added to the nerves. The AFR reported that France had set the bond market on fire again, warned that artificial-intelligence debt raisings increase the risk, and said a sudden wobble in AI debt had arrived at the wrong time for Firmus. A company that needs cheap capital to build data centres is sensitive to exactly that.

Labor ministers split over the ATO's credit-card ban, and the Opposition calls the government 'all over the shop'

The government's own ministers took opposite positions on Thursday on the tax office's decision to stop accepting credit cards for tax payments after November 30. Housing Minister Clare O'Neil told Nine's Today program that the ATO should reconsider and work with business on a fix, and Small Business Minister Anne Aly joined her. About 25 minutes later Assistant Minister Andrew Charlton said he supported the ban, arguing the ATO offers flexible payment plans for cash shortfalls and that businesses losing card income would have to lift prices. The Guardian, the Herald, the AFR, SBS and the ABC all reported the contradiction, and the ABC said the government was under pressure to intervene.

The ATO has stood firm after meeting business groups. It says only about 2.3 per cent of tax payments were made by credit card last year and that it is not appropriate to pass merchant fees on to the public. Opposition leader Angus Taylor called the stance hypocritical given the Reserve Bank's ban on business surcharges, which Treasurer Jim Chalmers championed as a cost-of-living measure. Shadow Treasurer Tim Wilson mocked the confusion. The Herald and The Age carried small business owners calling the position un-Australian. The Guardian reported ministers urging the ATO to reverse course amid a growing backlash from small business, and SBS reported that card surcharges face scrutiny as costs rise.

Law · ResourcesPress ✓ 7 outlets · political reaction as reported; effect on other approvals untested

Governments and miners react to the High Court coal ruling. NSW downplays it, WA seeks advice and lawyers see a wave of cases

A day after the High Court sent the Mount Pleasant extension back to the NSW Independent Planning Commission, governments tried to work out what it meant. The ABC reported that Australia's biggest coal-producing state fears the impact of the emissions decision, and that Western Australia's treasurer was surprised by the outcome and is seeking advice. NSW Premier Chris Minns told the Guardian activists should not get overexcited about what he called a technical decision. The AFR reported that miners are bracing for an environmental legal wave, and The Age urged the Albanese government to resist as miners dig in.

One report put the number of coal proposals in the NSW pipeline that could be affected at more than 18, and the ABC said the ruling says the quiet part out loud. Commentators were split on whether the case is narrow or sweeping. The majority held only that the commission failed to consider conditions on downstream scope 3 emissions; the court left open whether climate change is an impact in the locality of a development. The Guardian profiled a retired couple who took on a coal giant, which it compared with the film The Castle. The Hunter residents behind the case, SBS reported, now face a re-assessment rather than a final victory. An AFR commentary on Wednesday urged calm, arguing that the judges are no climate activists.

NSW Police launch an armed response command to put heavily armed officers on Sydney's streets after the Bondi attack

NSW Police unveiled a new Armed Response Command on Thursday, and the Herald, The Age, the ABC and SBS reported that heavily armed officers will begin patrolling Sydney streets. The unit is a response to the Bondi Beach attack of December 14, 2025, in which 15 people were shot dead at a Hanukkah celebration. In May Deputy Commissioner David Hudson told a royal commission that officers had faced long-arm weapons armed only with 9 mm Glock pistols, and that the force planned a command equipped with semi-automatic rifles. An interim operation, Operation Shelter, was to continue until the new squad was fully operational in about 18 months to two years.

The Herald and The Age reported that an expansion of the remit of Jewish community security groups is still being considered. Premier Chris Minns has said police will carry long-arm weapons on Sydney's streets for a long time to come. The announcement comes the day after the Jewish community's October 7 commemorations at Bondi, and SBS described it in its Easy English bulletin as a police response team launched after the Bondi shooting. The Guardian and the Herald reported this week that the police commissioner moved to hold closed hearings, or to block public hearings, into police violence at a Sydney protest.

Health · WorldPress ✓ 6 outlets · case details as reported by Kenyan authorities

Kenya reports its first Ebola case, a man back from Congo who died in Nairobi, and Australian outlets ask whether the world is watching the wrong outbreak

Kenya confirmed its first-ever Ebola case this week. Health Minister Aden Duale said a Kenyan man who had been living in Congo had been isolated at Nairobi Hospital, tested positive and died on Monday. He fell ill about a month earlier, was treated in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, travelled overland to Kampala and flew to Nairobi on October 3. Authorities began contact tracing, listing 28 contacts, of whom one had tested positive, and are tracing 23 passengers and crew from the flight. Contacts remain in isolation for 21 days. The Africa CDC is sending a team, and the World Health Organization advised against travel restrictions.

The outbreak is the second-largest on record, with more than 8,000 cases and more than 4,000 deaths in eastern Congo, caused by the Bundibugyo strain, and Kenya is the fourth country with a linked case. The Herald ran a commentary asking why the world is watching reports of plague in Russia while Ebola crosses borders, and SBS called the first Kenyan case a wake-up call for East Africa. SBS also reported on a telltale sign that Russia's plague scare could be more serious. We saw no report of an Ebola case in Australia.
