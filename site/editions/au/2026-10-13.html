A 13-year-old on bail is charged with murder after a Sydney father is stabbed to death on his way home from work

Police allege the boy was already on bail. The man was reportedly helping a group of children get home when he was attacked in the city’s west; the number of people charged is not yet clear.

A 13-year-old boy who was on bail has been charged with murder after a Sydney father was stabbed to death in the city’s west, the Sydney Morning Herald, ABC and News.com.au reported on Monday. The Herald said the man was on his way home from work and was allegedly attacked while helping a separate group of children get home. Both the Herald and ABC used the phrase “absolutely senseless” in their headlines. The reports we saw do not give the man’s name or the time of the attack, so we have not repeated any. ABC’s web address points to Mount Druitt, but we have not seen the suburb confirmed in its text.

Police have since described the circumstances that led to the death, the Herald said, although the summary we saw did not set them out. News.com.au reported that CCTV footage is part of the case. Headlines in the Herald group also spoke of “teens” and “teenage boys” being charged, which suggests more than one young person is involved. We have not confirmed how many people were charged, or with what.

The boy’s age shapes what follows. In New South Wales a child can be charged from the age of 10, but between 10 and 13 the prosecution must also show the child knew the act was seriously wrong, a safeguard known as doli incapax. Courts also restrict the publication of the names of children accused of crimes. That is background from NSW law, not something Monday’s reports set out.

That the boy was on bail will draw attention. Bail lets an accused person stay out of custody until trial, usually under conditions such as a curfew. A separate Herald-group headline on Monday said the teenager who allegedly hit a woman with a car near the Melbourne Marathon was also on bail; we take that to mean the Flinders Street Station incident, but the summary did not say so, and it comes from one publisher. Whether bail conditions were breached in either case was not stated in the items we saw.

Youth crime and bail are politically sensitive in both states, and each new case tends to be read through that lens. We did not find a response from the NSW Government or the Opposition in Monday’s coverage, and we have not repeated any claim about what rules should change.

To summarise what two or more outlets agree on: a man died after being stabbed in Sydney’s west, a 13-year-old has been charged with his murder, and the boy was on bail. What only one outlet reported, or remains open, includes the CCTV detail (News.com.au), the plural “teens” in the Herald group’s headlines, and almost every detail of the attack itself. Words such as “senseless” are description, not evidence, and we treat them that way.

Beyond the politics, a family has lost a father. Anyone affected by what they have read can call Lifeline on 13 11 14, or Kids Helpline on 1800 55 1800. A date for the boy’s next court appearance was not in our sources.

Australian engineer Robert Pether leaves Iraq after more than five years in detention

Robert Pether, an Australian engineer jailed in Iraq since 2021, has left the country after more than five years in detention, the Sydney Morning Herald, The Age, ABC, the Guardian and News.com.au reported on Monday. The Herald said his release followed a protracted campaign. It reported that the Iraqi government had been insisting that Pether or his employer pay a US$12 million fine. We did not see whether any money was paid, or how the dispute was settled. ABC reported that his wife was relieved the “nightmare” was over.

The Herald’s headline called the detention “arbitrary”, a term for imprisonment without a proper legal basis. Earlier reporting that we have not re-checked said he had been freed on bail but barred from leaving Iraq because of financial claims tied to a contract dispute involving his employer. Monday’s items did not say when he flew out, where he is now, or whether the travel ban was lifted by a court or by agreement.

For his family the focus now turns to getting him home and to his health after years in custody. We did not find a statement from the Foreign Minister, Penny Wong, in the items we saw.

National petrol prices pass $2.40 a litre, the highest since the first weeks of the Iran war, with little relief in sight

The national average price of petrol went past $2.40 a litre, the Sydney Morning Herald and The Age reported on Monday. They said drivers had not faced pump prices this high since the opening weeks of the Iran war, and that prices are unlikely to ease until the conflict is resolved. We did not see the exact average, the date it was measured or the price survey behind it, and the figure comes from one publisher.

Crude oil, the raw material, was more mixed. WTI, the US benchmark, closed on Friday at US$91.85 a barrel, up 0.39 per cent. Reuters reported that oil slipped in Asia on Monday and the FT that prices fell after Donald Trump’s “energy ceasefire” announcement (see the Ukraine story), while Reuters said the dollar stayed near an 18-month high. ABC’s headline said Mr Trump’s diesel deal is not expected to lower prices. Reuters also said top oil executives were dropped from an event schedule after the Houthi attacks on Riyadh’s airport, and that skies over the Saudi capital were largely empty.

Pump prices usually follow crude with a lag of a week or two, so a fall or rise in oil does not show at the bowser at once. The Herald’s separate commentary said Mr Trump’s diesel deal with Russia was driven by petrol prices ahead of the US midterm elections, a sign of how far the oil price now steers politics in Washington as well as in Canberra.

Trump’s “energy ceasefire” falters as Kyiv says Russia keeps striking and the Kremlin rejects a 60-day truce

Donald Trump’s announcement of an “energy ceasefire” between Russia and Ukraine faltered within hours, the Financial Times, AP, ABC and the Guardian reported. Volodymyr Zelenskiy said Russia carried out strikes on power plants and fuel reserves after the announcement, the FT said. ABC quoted him saying that if Moscow wants Kyiv to stop hitting Russian refineries, as Mr Trump demanded, it should stop hitting Ukraine’s key infrastructure. Reuters reported that the Kremlin rejected the Ukrainian leader’s idea of a 60-day ceasefire and wants an overall peace deal instead.

A headline from the FinancialJuice news feed quoted the Kremlin as saying contacts on the energy truce were ongoing and it would say more “in due course”; that comes from one aggregator. Reuters also reported that the European Union adopted nearly 1,600 new sanctions listings against Russia, and that a prominent Republican senator said Mr Trump’s Russian diesel deal may be illegal. Bloomberg quoted Amrita Sen of Energy Aspects calling the diesel and ceasefire moves “extremely desperate measures”.

The Herald wrote that Mr Trump made the announcement with petrol prices biting ahead of the US midterm elections. The FT also reported that Washington had threatened to block arms for Ukraine over its refinery attacks; we have not seen the US side confirm it. The Kremlin’s line, as Reuters framed it, is that Moscow wants lasting peace rather than a pause.

World · EconomicsPress ✓ 5 outlets · citation wording as reported by AP and Bloomberg

Robert Hall and Michael Woodford win the Nobel economics prize for work on how economies are steadied

Robert E. Hall of Stanford University and Michael Woodford of Columbia University share the 2026 Nobel memorial prize in economics, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced in Stockholm on Monday, AP, Reuters, ABC, Bloomberg and the Financial Times reported. Bloomberg, citing AP, gave the citation as work on the “microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization”; Reuters summarised it as work on stabilisation. Both men are American.

In plain terms, stabilisation means the policies and institutions that smooth booms and busts, chiefly central banks setting interest rates and governments adjusting spending and tax. As background from our own knowledge, not from Monday’s reports, Woodford is best known for theory on how central banks set rates, and Hall for work on unemployment and the business cycle. The link to Australia is direct: the Reserve Bank is trying to bring inflation down without causing a recession, and News.com.au has reported “dire” inflation figures while CommBank noted that wage growth slowed in September after an August bounce.

The prize is formally the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel, funded by Sweden’s central bank rather than by Alfred Nobel’s will, and first awarded in 1969. We did not see the prize amount in Monday’s items. The announcement followed the physics prize, which ABC reported honoured work on “ghost messengers from space”.
