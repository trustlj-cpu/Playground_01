Police charge a person with murder over the shooting death of a 12-year-old boy in a Sydney apartment last month

Early reports said a man had been charged; later ones, from the Herald and the BBC, call the accused a teenager. None of the headlines we hold gives a motive or an age.

Police have charged a person with murder over the death of a 12-year-old boy who was shot in a Sydney apartment last month, the ABC, the Guardian, the BBC, Nine’s news site and the Sydney Morning Herald reported on Wednesday. The Guardian placed the shooting in an apartment “last month”. Nine’s headline named Norwest, a business and residential area in Sydney’s north-west (the location description is our background). The Herald said the boy was shot in the face, the BBC in the face and neck. The boy’s name and the date of the shooting were not in the items we saw, so we have not repeated them.

How the accused is described changed during the day. The ABC, Nine, the Guardian and the Herald’s first reports said a man had been charged. Later on Wednesday the Herald’s updated headline and the BBC called the accused a teenager. We did not see an age, a name or a police statement explaining the difference, so we use neutral wording. “Man” may simply have been loose language in the earlier headlines, but that is our guess, not something any outlet said.

The question of age matters for what follows. As background, not from Wednesday’s reports: in New South Wales a child aged 10 to 13 is presumed not to know that serious wrongdoing is wrong unless the prosecution proves otherwise, a safeguard known as doli incapax, and the law restricts the publication of the names of children accused of crimes. A child charged with a serious offence is also dealt with through the Children’s Court system. Whether any of that applies here depends on an age that has not been published.

The charge became public in reports published early on Wednesday, Sydney time. News.com.au’s headline, “huge update after boy shot dead”, fits the charge but does not spell it out. We did not see how police identified the accused, whether anyone else is being sought, or where the accused is now. In a case like this the first court appearance is usually brief: the charge is read, bail is addressed and the matter is adjourned so police can prepare the evidence. That is general practice, not a report of what happened here.

To summarise what the outlets agree on: the boy was 12, he was shot, he died, the shooting happened last month in Sydney, and someone has been charged with his murder. What is open: the accused’s age, the motive, what happened inside the apartment, and when the accused next appears in court. Until those are on the public record, anything further is speculation.

A child has died and a family is grieving. Anyone affected by this can call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or Kids Helpline on 1800 55 1800. Anyone with information about the case can report it anonymously to Crime Stoppers on 1800 333 000.

Labor’s trust tax overhaul is set to raise $40 billion, as businesses warn of accounting turmoil and the Greens warn of a school-fees loophole

Labor’s plan to change how discretionary trusts are taxed is set to raise $40 billion, the Sydney Morning Herald and its sister mastheads reported on Wednesday, adding that businesses are warning of “accounting turmoil”. The Courier Mail said Treasurer Jim Chalmers faces fury over changes that “could cost Aussies” $13.3 billion, and the Guardian said changes are likely and explained why some Australians are upset. The headlines do not say what period either figure covers or whether they measure the same thing, so we do not add them up or treat one as correcting the other.

A discretionary trust is a legal structure, common among family businesses and farms, in which a trustee decides each year how income is shared among its beneficiaries. That flexibility has long let families spread income across several people (background, not from the reports). The Greens said Labor’s tax bill contains a loophole for private school fees, the ABC and the Herald reported; we have not seen Labor’s reply or the bill’s wording. Start dates and exactly who is affected were not in the headlines we saw.

The size of the figure explains the heat: $40 billion is enough to move a budget. What the headlines do not settle is who ends up paying, which is the question the fury is really about.

World · Middle EastPress ✓ 5 outlets · damage at Aramco and the ship sinking from Reuters sources only

Saudi-led strikes hit Sanaa, the Houthis vow a “painful response”, and Reuters says Aramco’s headquarters was badly damaged

A Saudi-led coalition ordered an oil facility in Sanaa, the Yemeni capital, to be evacuated ahead of an operation, then struck a fuel depot near the city, Reuters and the BBC reported. The ABC reported blasts in Sanaa and a Houthi vow of a “painful response” inside Saudi Arabia. Reuters later reported, citing sources, that loud booms were heard near Saudi Aramco’s headquarters in Dhahran and that the headquarters suffered extensive damage in a Houthi attack. AP said residents of the Saudi capital, Riyadh, reported new explosions. Reuters added that the Houthis vowed more attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities.

The damage claims rest on Reuters’ unnamed sources; we did not see Saudi Arabia or Aramco confirm them. Reuters also reported that a container ship sank near the Strait of Hormuz after being hit by a projectile, again citing sources and without saying who fired, and that Iran is shifting tactics to step up attacks on shipping before the US vote, citing officials. Donald Trump said the Houthis are trying to drive up oil prices before the US midterm elections, Reuters reported. Its headlines this week describe the wider backdrop as an Iran war with a US blockade of Iran.

For oil, Reuters said prices climbed as traders weighed Saudi supply risk and Russian diesel flows, and that the International Energy Agency says diesel shortages could last well into 2027. We have no confirmed oil price inside our time window, so we quote none. Pump prices follow crude to the bowser with a lag, and Reuters headlines also note fuel shortages in Malawi. Separately, the Herald group reported that Bali is facing fuel shortages, without giving a cause in the headline, and ran a travel piece saying cheaper fares are beating safety concerns for people flying to Europe via the Middle East.

World · ReligionPress ✓ 5 outlets · hospital, stage of the cancer and treatment plan not in our sources

Pope Leo XIV has part of his right lung removed, and the Vatican says doctors found cancer

Pope Leo XIV has been diagnosed with lung cancer after surgery to remove part of his right lung, AP reported. The ABC said the Vatican reported that a lobe of the lung was removed and that doctors found cancer, and the Sydney Morning Herald reported the same on Wednesday morning, Sydney time. A Reuters web address from Tuesday points to a planned hospital stay for surgery on a lung nodule, and Reuters said on Wednesday that he had a peaceful night in hospital after the operation.

Beyond that, our sources are thin. We did not see which hospital he is in, the stage of the cancer, whether further treatment is planned, or how long he is expected to be away from his duties. A lung nodule is a small spot on the lung found on a scan; it is not always cancer, but in this case the Vatican’s statement, as the ABC and AP reported it, says it was. A lobe is one of the sections of a lung, and the right lung has three.

As background, Pope Leo was elected in May 2025 and is the first pope from the United States. About one in five Australians said they were Catholic in the 2021 census, which is why news from the Vatican reaches many Australian households. The reports we saw are about his health and recovery, not about any change to the Church’s governance.

Chalmers tells progressives to stop sneering at One Nation voters as the party’s rise unsettles both Labor and the Coalition

Treasurer Jim Chalmers urged progressives not to mock, cancel or talk down to One Nation supporters, The Australian, the Courier Mail, the Herald Sun, News.com.au and the Guardian reported on Wednesday; the Herald group’s live blog said he told progressives to take the party’s rise seriously. The ABC reported that Labor loyalists are jumping ship as voters look for alternatives, and the Herald group reported a long list of former Coalition MPs seeking a new home on One Nation’s crowded NSW ticket. Its headline on Pauline Hanson’s party said it looms large at Parliament, crashing question time and backing anti-vaccine bills. We saw only headlines on a separate report that One Nation’s NSW leader has drawn intrigue over his parliamentary escorts.

A second thread was less comfortable for Labor. The ABC reported that the prime minister confirmed he intervened to delay an ATO credit-card ban. News Corp mastheads and The Nightly framed it as Mr Albanese taking credit and leaving Mr Chalmers exposed, while Crikey said the two men were trying to calm Labor nerves. We did not see what the ATO rule is or its new start date. The Liberals face their own pressure: the Herald group reported an outgoing Liberal warning the party not to copy One Nation, and Mr Albanese meeting “Taylor” more often (we take that to be the Liberal leader Angus Taylor, but the summary did not say).

No poll figures were in the headlines we saw, so we cannot say how large One Nation’s rise is. What the coverage does show is that both major parties are spending public energy on how to talk about its voters. That is a story about nerves as much as numbers, and Labor and the Coalition are still working out whether to confront One Nation or to copy it.
