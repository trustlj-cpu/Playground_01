U.S. Loses Measles Elimination Status After 26 Years, a Regional Health Body Says, and Democrats Blame Kennedy

The Pan American Health Organization says measles has spread without a break for more than a year, ending a status the country had held since 2000.

The Pan American Health Organization, the World Health Organization's regional arm for the Americas, announced Tuesday that the United States has lost its measles elimination status, a designation the country had held for 26 years, since 2000. The body said measles has circulated in the United States without interruption for more than a year. That is the test: a country keeps the status only if the same chain of infection does not persist for 12 months or longer. According to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention figures cited in coverage, 5,412 cases and six deaths had been confirmed as of Nov. 12. Mexico and Bolivia lost the designation in the same announcement. CDC figures also show that coverage with the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine among kindergartners fell from 95.2% in the 2019-20 school year to 92.4% in 2025-26, and about 95% is the level generally treated as enough to keep an outbreak from spreading through a community.

Democrats blamed Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. for the loss; the department's response has not been confirmed. The designation marks a reversal for a country once held up as a model for stopping the virus. To get it back, health officials said, the United States must submit a corrective action plan and then go 12 months without continuous spread. The loss follows Monday's report that a medical journal released data on measles in pregnancy after the CDC reportedly declined to alert doctors, a development covered in this morning's edition. Whether federal officials will file a corrective plan, and when, has not been announced.

Jensen Huang Says He Would Be 'Honored' to Return as Nvidia CEO; a Report Says the Board Is Not Considering It

Jensen Huang, the Nvidia co-founder who resigned as chief executive last week, said Tuesday that he would be honored to return if the board decides it is in the best interest of shareholders, employees, the company and the country. It was his first public statement since stepping down. He said he owns his mistakes, is cooperating with the board's independent investigation, and has heard an outpouring of calls from employees, investors, partners and customers to come back. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO while the company looks for a permanent one. President Trump has said he would not mind if Nvidia reinstated Huang. A petition urging his return has reportedly gathered more than 190,000 signatures, a count from a single report that has not been independently checked. One report says the board is not considering his return as of Tuesday, and another says the board has formed a national security panel, a group of directors set up to oversee matters such as export rules for chips. Nvidia has not announced a successor or a timetable. The next test is the company's quarterly earnings report, the first since Huang left, and its date has not been confirmed.

Administration Moves to Close a Loophole That Lets China Rent Advanced AI Chips Remotely, a Report Says It Is Rattling Southeast Asia

The Trump administration is moving to close a loophole that lets Chinese companies use advanced American AI chips without owning them, by renting computing power from data centers abroad. The new restrictions target that remote access, and a report says they could jeopardize billions of dollars in investment in Southeast Asia and force governments there to decide how closely to align with Washington. The step comes just ahead of a planned meeting between the U.S. and Chinese presidents, and a Chinese government spokesman reportedly said China will protect the rights and interests of its firms. Details of the rules beyond that outline have not been confirmed.

The Commerce Department said it is investigating Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data center company that buys Nvidia chips. U.S. agencies said the inquiry began in 2024, and the Justice Department is reported to be running a criminal investigation. Nvidia has said it has seen no evidence that Megaspeed diverted chips to China, and Megaspeed denies ties to smuggling. Separately, unnamed sources say Nvidia has warned buyers about legal risks tied to China access and will not sell to customers that rent out more than half their chips to China; Nvidia has not confirmed that policy.

Ukraine Strikes the Kerch Bridge to Crimea, Leaving It Critically Damaged, Reports Say

Ukraine struck the Kerch Bridge, the road and rail link between Russia and occupied Crimea, in an overnight attack into Tuesday, and reports describe a section of the roadway and an arch of the span falling into the water. Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelensky, called the operation unprecedented, and the crossing was shut to traffic. One report says Russian officials described it as the second day of strikes on the bridge and the largest drone attack of the war. Russia was also reported to be hitting crossings in Kyiv at the same time.

The bridge carries symbolic weight for Moscow: it was built after Russia annexed Crimea in 2014 and is a main supply route for forces in the south. The attack came ahead of a planned meeting involving Vladimir Putin, Xi Jinping and Trump, though no link between the two has been established. How badly the bridge is damaged, how long it will be closed and whether anyone was killed have not been confirmed, and the accounts of what was used in the attack differ.

Transportation Secretary Asks Congress for $50 Billion for Air Traffic Control, and Senate Republicans Are Cool to It

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy laid out a request for about $50 billion to modernize the air traffic control system when he met Republican lawmakers on Capitol Hill on Tuesday, days after a nationwide ground stop halted flights. The department's letter urges $50.1 billion, including $10 billion to build five new consolidated en route traffic control centers, the facilities that guide planes between airports. A one-page explanation of the request was thin on specifics, and the figure is well above an earlier ask of roughly $30 billion.

Senate Republicans were skeptical. Sen. John Kennedy said Duffy made a mistake by raising the number, Sen. John Cornyn said Congress is unlikely to simply write a check, and Majority Leader John Thune said Republicans have questions and will see what is achievable. Sen. Ted Cruz floated using reconciliation, a budget process that can pass on a simple majority, but said the administration must show it can spend the $12.5 billion Congress approved last year. House Speaker Mike Johnson said the request would be taken up in the lame-duck session, the stretch between the election and the new Congress, and the president has urged lawmakers to pay for it. Duffy said he will take what he can get.
