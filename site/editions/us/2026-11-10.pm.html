Russia and Ukraine Open Two Days of U.S.-Mediated Talks in Abu Dhabi; the First Session Lasted Nearly Four Hours

The first direct negotiations since February began Tuesday; Zelenskyy called day one “constructive and quite substantive,” and no agreement has been announced.

Delegations from Russia, Ukraine and the United States opened a two-day round of negotiations in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday, the first direct talks between the two warring sides since February. The first session ran for nearly four hours. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy described the day as “constructive and quite substantive,” and Secretary of State Marco Rubio called it “very productive” while saying much work remains. The discussion centered largely on humanitarian questions such as prisoner exchanges, and the agenda also includes a broad conversation about European security, a subject Moscow has long wanted on the table. Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov said security issues would come first on Russia’s list. President Trump said the talks present a chance of a possible breakthrough. No agreement, ceasefire or timetable has been announced, and some European officials have said they expect progress to be limited.

Tuesday’s session follows two earlier U.S.-brokered rounds in Abu Dhabi, on Jan. 23-24 and Feb. 4, when territory was the central sticking point: Russia demanded concessions in the Donbas region and Ukraine refused. A “trilateral” format means all three parties sit in the same process rather than the United States passing messages between two delegations. Zelenskyy wrote Tuesday that the fastest way to end the war is to hold positions and move to negotiations. The Kremlin declined to say whether the resumption of Black Sea shipping is on the agenda, saying it would not answer such questions while talks are under way. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has said he does not have high expectations, and Rubio said Europeans should eventually be at the table. Day two is Wednesday. What remains unknown is whether either side will move on territory, and whether a ceasefire or another round will be announced.

House Faces a Tuesday-Evening Vote on Rep. Massie’s Eight Articles of Impeachment Against Hegseth; the Outcome Is Not Yet Known

Rep. Thomas Massie, a Kentucky Republican, filed eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on Monday, Nov. 9, most of them tied to the war with Iran. Three accuse Hegseth of waging war against Iran in violation of the War Powers Resolution, the 1973 law that limits a president’s ability to keep forces in hostilities without Congress, and another says he ignored rules meant to minimize civilian casualties. Because Massie filed the resolution as privileged, a procedural status that obliges the House to act on it, leaders had to schedule a floor vote within two legislative days. That vote was set for Tuesday evening, and the first step was expected to be a motion to table, or shelve, the resolution. If that motion fails, Massie has said he will ask for separate votes on each article. Hegseth called the effort a “political spectacle” at a Pentagon briefing Tuesday, and Trump has said he would stand by him even if the House impeaches him. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries has said he is a “hard yes.” It is not known how many Republicans will cross over. Impeachment by the House takes only a simple majority, but removing a cabinet officer requires a two-thirds vote in the Senate, which Republicans control. At the time of writing, no result had been reported.

Supreme Court Lets Border Wall Construction Resume in Texas’s Big Bend Region While the Lawsuit Continues

The Supreme Court on Monday, Nov. 9, allowed the Trump administration to resume building border barriers in the Big Bend region of West Texas, including areas in and around Big Bend National Park. The justices set aside an order by U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone in El Paso that had halted the work while the challengers’ lawsuit goes forward. The order was unsigned, and Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson was reported as the only dissent. The majority said federal law lets the Homeland Security secretary waive environmental statutes to speed border construction, and that the government’s interest “outweighs any aesthetic, recreational, and environmental harm alleged by respondents.” The Justice Department had asked the Court to step in on Oct. 21. Cardone had found the challengers likely to succeed on their argument under the major questions doctrine, the idea that Congress must speak clearly before an agency claims sweeping power.

The ruling is temporary: it holds until the Supreme Court decides whether to hear the case or the lower court resolves it. Environmental groups including the Sierra Club and Earthjustice condemned the decision. Because the order is interim, the underlying question, whether the three waivers issued this year were lawful, has not been decided.

Fed Governor Waller Says a December Rate Hike Looks Likely and Opens the Door to a Half-Point Move

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said Tuesday that he plans to support at least a quarter-point increase in the Fed’s benchmark interest rate at its December meeting and is open to a larger half-point increase. He said the Fed needs to raise rates to put downward pressure on inflation, and described the labor market as solid, with employment close to the Fed’s goal. A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so a quarter-point move is 25 basis points and a half-point move is 50. Waller said he is not super hawkish, meaning he is not pushing for the most aggressive path. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan separately said the Fed needs to raise rates at least two more times.

A single governor’s preference is not a decision: the rate-setting committee votes as a group at its December meeting, and the data between now and then can change the debate. The S&P 500 closed Tuesday down 0.38% at 7,520.93 and the Nasdaq Composite closed down 0.74% at 25,065.88. Still unknown is whether other members of the committee will back a half-point move rather than a quarter-point one.

Trump Casts Doubt on the Midterm Results After Saying He Was “Absolutely Willing” to Accept Them, Offering No Specifics

President Trump wrote on Truth Social on Monday that he doubts the integrity of the Nov. 3 midterm elections, questioning whether the election was honest and saying the voting machines matter more than the voters. He had earlier said he was “absolutely willing” to accept the results. His post pointed to early-voting totals and suggested the machines favored Democrats, but it did not identify which machines or any ballots that were counted improperly, and no evidence of irregularities has been presented in the reporting. Election officials around the country reported largely trouble-free voting.

Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer called the post a “pathetic return to election denialism.” Former Republican Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene rejected Trump’s fraud claims on X, saying the outcome is “100% on him.” Results of an election are not final until each state certifies them, and certified results, not statements about them, are what settle who won. What remains unknown is whether Trump will take any formal step beyond the post.
