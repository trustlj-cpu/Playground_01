Asian Tech Stocks Slide Friday After Wall Street's Sharp Thursday Drop, With the S&P 500 Down 2.02% and SoftBank Down More Than 16%, a Report Says

The three major U.S. indexes each fell more than 2% on the day Anthropic began trading; reports offer several explanations and analysts quoted said no single event accounts for it.

U.S. stocks ended Thursday sharply lower in a broad selloff on the day Anthropic began trading on the Nasdaq. The S&P 500 fell 2.02% to 7,175.83, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 2.25% to 23,879.20, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average lost 2.19%, about 1,101 points, to 49,097.06. The S&P 500 tracks 500 large companies and is the benchmark many index funds follow; the Nasdaq Composite leans toward technology; the Dow tracks 30 industrial and blue-chip names. In plain terms, every $100 invested in the S&P 500 at Wednesday's close was worth about $98 at Thursday's. A report says it was the index's sixth straight losing session, its longest losing streak since 2024. Anthropic priced its record IPO at $83 a share and opened at $111.50 before giving back much of that gain; one report says the shares finished about 17% above the offering price, a figure that so far comes from a single report.

The offering raised more than $100 billion, a report says, the largest IPO on record, and a report says the company's seven co-founders sold about $2 billion of their shares in it. Chipmakers including Micron and Broadcom gave up earlier gains on Thursday, a report says. Reports link the drop to worries that the boom in artificial intelligence stocks may be ending, to scrutiny of Nvidia's sales of chips to China, and to the possibility of tighter export controls on advanced chips. The CME Group's FedWatch tool, which tracks futures prices, put the odds of a quarter-point Federal Reserve rate increase in December at about 94%, a report says. Analysts quoted said no single event explained the day. The slide reached Asia on Friday: a report says SoftBank shares plunged more than 16% in Tokyo, and another says stocks in Taiwan fell more than 4%. Still unknown is whether U.S. futures follow Asia lower at Friday's open, whether the S&P 500's losing streak reaches a seventh session, and how Anthropic's shares trade in their second day.

White House Cyber Director Sean Cairncross Is Leaving, Reports Differ on Whether He Was Pushed Out, and the Pentagon's Kirsten Davies Is Named Acting Director

Sean Cairncross, the White House national cyber director, is leaving the administration, a White House official said Thursday, and Kirsten Davies, the Pentagon's chief information officer, will take over as acting director. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced her appointment on X. An acting director is a temporary stand-in who can run the office without a Senate vote; a permanent pick needs Senate confirmation, and Democrats take control of the Senate in January. Accounts of why he is going differ. One report, citing multiple unnamed sources, says he was fired; another describes an amicable exit, with Cairncross unwilling to serve two more years. The departure follows about a week of technology outages that reports say hit Microsoft services, air traffic control systems, banks and a National Security Agency internal network. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told lawmakers this week that investigators have seen nothing indicating the outages are related, and no named official has said they led to Cairncross's exit. Still unknown are the stated reason, who the White House will nominate, and whether the outages share a cause.

Justice Department Gives Up Its Court Fight to Delay Alaska's Senate Result, Saying the Dispute Is Moot Now That Ballots Are Counted, While Peltola Remains Projected Ahead

The Justice Department told a federal appeals court Thursday that it is dropping its appeal and asked to dismiss its lawsuit over Alaska's U.S. Senate race. The department sued Oct. 22 to block certification, the state's formal sign-off on the final count, if Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan lost, citing a second candidate named Dan Sullivan on the ballot. A federal judge dismissed the suit Nov. 1 and the department said it would appeal. It now calls the dispute moot, a legal term for an issue that no longer needs a court's decision, because the ballots have been counted and the investigation it sought can no longer be carried out.

Democrat Mary Peltola is projected ahead: news organizations projected her ahead Tuesday, with a count showing about 50.1% of first-choice votes to Sullivan's 45.8%, according to one report. Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, but a candidate above 50% of first-choice votes does not need the extra rounds. Those figures are projections, not a result. The state has not certified the race, and one report says Sullivan's campaign said it would not concede until it does. A report says a state investigation into allegations tied to the election continues. Still to come are the certification date and any further challenge.

JPMorgan Chase, Visa and Mastercard Say Third-Party Vendors Were Hit by Cyberattacks, and Each Says Payment Card Data Was Not Affected

JPMorgan Chase, Visa and Mastercard each said Thursday that a third-party vendor, an outside company that handles services or data for them, was hit by a cyberattack. Chase said attackers reached certain marketing data held by vendors that may include some customer names and email addresses. Visa tied the incident to a provider in the Asia-Pacific region, and Mastercard said a limited number of email addresses from a small group of cardholders enrolled in one benefit program were exposed. All three said payment card data, such as card numbers, was not affected. Chase said the incident is unrelated to the service disruption its customers had on Tuesday.

A ransomware group calling itself n0n has claimed the attacks, a report says, giving the companies 48 hours to pay before it publishes data; ransomware is malicious software that locks or steals data and demands payment. The companies did not name the vendors or say how many people are affected, and the group's claims have not been verified. Still unknown are the number of customers involved, which vendors were breached, and whether any stolen data has been released.

Trump Says He Has Issued a 'Complete and Total Pardon' to the Late Pete Rose, Baseball's All-Time Hits Leader

President Donald Trump said Thursday that he had issued a "complete and total pardon" to Pete Rose, the baseball great who died in 2024, announcing it in a post on Truth Social as he returned from his trip to Asia. A pardon is a president's act of forgiving a federal conviction, and a posthumous pardon, granted after death, cannot change Rose's life but formally forgives the conviction. Reports tie it to Rose's 1990 guilty plea to two counts of filing false tax returns.

Rose holds baseball's career record of 4,256 hits and was banned from the sport for betting on games, a ban the commissioner of Major League Baseball lifted for him last year. Trump said in March 2025 that he planned to pardon Rose. Hall of Fame eligibility is a separate question handled by baseball, and one report says the earliest Rose could be inducted is 2028. Still unclear is what Rose's family has said and whether the pardon reaches beyond the tax convictions.
