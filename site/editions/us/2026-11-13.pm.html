City Investigators Examine the Excavation Next Door to the Brooklyn Collapse That Killed Eight

The Fire Department has ended its search; the city ordered work stopped at the neighboring site, and no cause has been announced.

Eight people have died since a three-story apartment building on Flatbush Avenue in Brooklyn collapsed, officials said. Two of the dead were children, ages 9 and 10. Reports conflict on the number of injured, putting it at 18 in some and at least 19 in others, so that figure is not settled. Mayor Zohran Mamdani said rescue crews were working in “extremely dangerous conditions” as they searched the rubble in the rain, and the Fire Department later said the search for victims was over. The dead included construction workers, a food deliveryman and two school-age children, and many had come to New York looking for a better life. Video verified by one newsroom shows walls caving in from inside a third-floor apartment as a family scrambled to get out. That family survived.

What caused the collapse is not known. An excavation, meaning digging into the ground to prepare a foundation, was under way on the lot next door, and city officials issued a stop-work order there after the building came down onto the site. City investigators have begun examining the excavation. That is an inquiry, not a finding: no official has said the digging caused the collapse. One report says the cause is under criminal investigation, and another says records show hazards and cracks were noted before the collapse; neither has been confirmed by the city. Digging beside an occupied building is regulated because it can affect the ground that supports its neighbors, and whether that happened here is what investigators must determine. Still open: the cause, any charges, and the final casualty figures.

Iran Says It Hit a U.S. Radar Site in Bahrain; U.S. Military Says the Radar Was Not Destroyed and Two Drones Were Intercepted

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said on Friday that they targeted a U.S. early-warning radar site in Bahrain, calling it a response to U.S. attacks on an Iranian telecommunications tower in the southern province of Hormozgan. The U.S. military’s Central Command denied that the radar was destroyed and said it intercepted two one-way attack drones that the Guards launched at Bahrain. A one-way attack drone is an explosive drone that is flown into its target rather than returned. The two accounts conflict on what, if anything, was hit: Iran describes a strike on the radar, while the U.S. says the drones were shot down and the radar stood. An early-warning radar is a system that detects incoming missiles and aircraft so forces can react in time. The United States and Iran are at war. Still open: whether independent evidence shows damage at the site, whether the U.S. carried out the tower strike Iran cites, and whether either side escalates further.

Bessent Reportedly Faults Commerce for Not Blacklisting Megaspeed

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent reportedly blamed the Commerce Department on Friday for failing to blacklist Megaspeed, the Singapore-based company at the center of the Nvidia affair. A blacklist of this kind is the Entity List, Commerce’s roster of foreign firms that U.S. companies may not supply without a license, and Commerce runs the export controls, the rules on which technology may be sold abroad. Reports say he faulted the department for not sanctioning the firm. According to one account, he added that Nvidia employees should be held accountable if they sidestepped the law. Commerce is led by Howard Lutnick, whose reportedly close relationship with former Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang has drawn growing scrutiny.

Megaspeed has said it will cooperate with the U.S. investigation, according to one report. A separate account describes the president’s top economic advisers as clashing over Lutnick’s handling of Nvidia; that wider claim of a rift is not confirmed by the officials involved. Lutnick’s reply to Bessent was not available. On Thursday the attorney general said the Justice Department has not concluded whether Alice Huang was a Chinese spy, and that remains unresolved. Still open: whether Commerce lists Megaspeed, whether Lutnick testifies before the Senate Banking Committee, and what Nvidia says.

Grand Jury Adds an Attempted Terrorism Charge Against Man Accused in White House Correspondents’ Dinner Attack

A federal grand jury has added an attempted terrorism charge, a D.C. terrorism count, to the case against Cole Tomas Allen, according to court filings that became public Thursday. Allen is accused of trying to assassinate President Trump at the White House Correspondents’ Association dinner in April, where he was arrested. A grand jury is a panel of citizens that decides whether prosecutors have enough evidence to bring formal charges, and the indictment is the document that lists them. The new count is an accusation, not a conviction, and Allen is presumed innocent.

An earlier indictment, returned in May, charged Allen with attempting to assassinate the president, assaulting a federal officer with a deadly weapon and firearm offenses, and prosecutors said the top count carries up to life in prison. Still open: the penalty the new count carries, when Allen will answer it in court, and when a trial might begin.

Florida City Cancels Its St. Patrick’s Day Parade to Comply With New State Anti-DEI Law

Delray Beach, Florida, has canceled its popular St. Patrick’s Day parade, saying it must do so to comply with a new state law that Gov. Ron DeSantis signed. The measure is widely called an anti-DEI law, a reference to diversity, equity and inclusion programs, and it bars municipalities, meaning cities and towns, from hosting events that fall under its terms. The city announced the cancellation of the Irish celebration this week, and the decision has drawn objections.

A Florida mayor is working to salvage the parade, according to one report. The episode shows how a state law can reach a local event: when state law overrides what a city may decide for itself, lawyers call it preemption. Still open: how the parade could go ahead under the law, which other events the law covers, and whether other cities will cancel events. The parade is normally held in March.
