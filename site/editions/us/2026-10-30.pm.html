Binance says about $6.3 billion in assets was affected in what would be the largest crypto hack on record, and analytics firms point to North Korea

The world's biggest exchange froze deposits and withdrawals, stablecoin issuers locked up suspect funds, and two blockchain analytics firms tied the theft to a North Korean hacking unit.

Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, said late Thursday that a security breach affected an estimated $6.3 billion of assets, which would make it the biggest crypto theft on record. The figure is Binance's own estimate of the assets involved, not a tally of money lost: Binance said roughly half of it, about $3 billion, had been frozen. The company first said Thursday that it was investigating a potential security issue, and its founder, Changpeng Zhao, said the exchange appeared to have been hacked. Binance suspended deposits and withdrawals while its teams worked on the problem, and bitcoin fell to a six-week low near $76,000. Tether, the issuer of the largest stablecoin, a digital token designed to stay worth one dollar, said it froze more than $1.5 billion of its tokens tied to the incident, and Circle, which issues another dollar stablecoin, froze funds too. Freezing matters because it keeps stolen tokens from being moved or cashed out. Early estimates ranged from about $2.5 billion to more than $5 billion before Binance settled on the $6.3 billion figure. Binance has said customer funds are safe.

On Friday, two blockchain analytics firms, TRM Labs and Chainalysis, said the theft was likely the work of TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking unit that has stolen from crypto companies before. Binance's preliminary investigation points to a compromised laptop belonging to one of its engineers as the way in. Neither analytics firm is a government agency, and no government attribution has been reported. It also remains unclear how much of the money can be recovered, who ends up absorbing the loss, and when Binance will let customers move funds again. The previous record for a single crypto heist was the roughly $1.5 billion taken from the exchange Bybit in February 2025, which the FBI also pinned on North Korea.

Nasdaq falls 2.9% and the S&P 500 drops 1.8% as the AI spending selloff spreads across the technology sector

What began as a reaction to two earnings reports became a broad Friday selloff. The Nasdaq Composite closed at 25,437.00, down 2.9%, on top of Thursday's 1.4% drop. The S&P 500 finished at 7,580.07, down 1.8%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at 51,770.31, down 1.1%. Before the opening bell, the Nasdaq 100 had been on course to lose more than $1 trillion in market value. Intel, which had cut its guidance, the outlook a company gives for coming quarters, on weak personal-computer demand, was among the heaviest losers. It followed Thursday's record $500 billion one-day wipeout at Alphabet, the Google parent, after its results disappointed. The common thread is capital spending: the giant sums that large technology companies are putting into data centers and chips for artificial intelligence, which investors now want to see turn into profit sooner. Strategists also warn that earnings growth across the market has probably peaked, and the market has punished companies that miss forecasts about as harshly as it ever has.

Appeals court again rejects Justice Department bid to set aside more than 20,000 California ballots

A federal appeals court on Friday turned down the Trump administration's latest attempt to force California to set aside ballots from more than 20,000 people the Justice Department says might be noncitizens. The panel voted 2-1 and declined to order the state to segregate the mail-in ballots, which are ballots sent and returned by post, in the middle of an election. It is the second time the court has refused the request. The ruling does not decide whether any of those voters are eligible. It only means the ballots can be counted in the normal way while the underlying lawsuit goes on. The Justice Department has also opened a criminal inquiry in California and sent grand jury subpoenas to local election officials, which was reported this week. California officials dispute the department's list, and the majority called the idea of setting the ballots aside plainly unconstitutional. Election Day is Tuesday, so any further step by the department would have to come within days.

Houston grand jury indicts ICE officer Mario Alberto Morales for striking a fleeing man with his vehicle

A Harris County grand jury on Friday indicted Mario Alberto Morales, a 33-year-old officer with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, on a charge of aggravated assault with a deadly weapon. Prosecutors say Morales was driving an unmarked black vehicle on Oct. 10 when it struck Miguel Parra Hernandez, 48, who was running away during a pursuit. Parra Hernandez was injured. The charge is a second-degree felony in Texas, which can bring a prison sentence of two to 20 years. Prosecutors argue the officer deliberately used the vehicle as a weapon. A state prosecution of a federal immigration officer is unusual, and it is not yet known whether the case will stay in state court or whether Morales has entered a plea.

Meta settles a Mississippi woman's social media addiction suit, avoiding the next bellwether trial

Meta reached a settlement on Friday with an 18-year-old Mississippi woman, identified in court papers as K.D.B., who said the company's apps were addictive and damaged her mental health when she used them as a child. Jury selection had begun this week, and the trial was set to start next week. It was the fourth of nine bellwether cases, a small set of test lawsuits chosen so that verdicts give both sides a sense of how the many similar claims might fare. Another plaintiff had also settled with Meta during the same stretch of trials. The terms have not been reported.

A settlement ends Meta's part of the case without a verdict, so no jury has ruled on whether the design of the apps caused harm. That leaves the central legal question open for the remaining bellwether cases, which are the closest thing so far to a preview of how juries may treat the many similar claims.
