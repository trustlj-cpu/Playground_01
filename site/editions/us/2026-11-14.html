FAA Orders Rare Nationwide Ground Stop; Officials Cite a Telecommunications Outage and Say They Have No Sign of an Attack

Departures were halted at airports across the country for hours on Friday; the transportation secretary later said the airspace was open again.

The Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop on Friday, halting departures at airports across the United States in a rare move it said was taken for “extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety.” A ground stop keeps planes at the gate or on the ground until air traffic control clears them to leave, and its effects spread far beyond the airports with trouble: flights that had not yet left were held, passengers waited on runways, and some professional and college teams bound for weekend games were stuck or switched to buses. Early counts put delayed flights above 10,000 and cancellations near 1,000, and final totals were not yet available; many travelers spent hours without information, and several international departures were allowed to leave while domestic flights stayed grounded. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said the cause was a telecommunications outage, a failure in the lines and equipment that carry voice and data between controllers, pilots and FAA systems. He said there was no indication of a “nefarious attack,” though he did not rule one out, and that the root cause was still being investigated.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said controllers could still reach pilots during the intermittent outage. Late Friday he said the airspace was open again and the problem had been identified, and he blamed overloaded routers, the devices that direct data across a network. Duffy and Mullin also called for about $50 billion to overhaul air travel systems. The last nationwide ground stop came in 2023, after a pilot-warning system went down, and lasted about 90 minutes; this one lasted for hours. Because airlines schedule aircraft and crews in chains, a stoppage of this size can disrupt flights for a day or more after the order is lifted. Still unanswered are what exactly failed, why a single outage could halt flights across the country, and how quickly airlines can restore normal schedules after crews and aircraft ended the night out of position.

Trump Administration Delays Enforcement of Medicaid Work Requirements by Six Months

The Trump administration has delayed enforcement of Medicaid work requirements by six months. The requirements were set to take effect on Jan. 1 for many able-bodied adults who receive Medicaid, the public health insurance program for low-income people. A work requirement means an adult covered through the program must show proof that he or she is working to keep coverage, and people who cannot show it can lose their insurance. The delay moves back the date on which people could begin losing coverage. A federal rule issued in June, an interim final rule, meaning a rule that takes effect before the public comment process is finished, set out how states are to carry out the requirement. The administration’s stated reason for the delay, the exact new start date and whether it applies to every state have not been confirmed. Several state officials and advocacy groups had asked for more time to prepare.

Trump Is Set to Meet Putin and Xi in China Next Week, U.S. Officials Say, With Nuclear Arms Control on the Agenda

President Trump will meet Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping in China next week, U.S. officials said, in what would be the first trilateral meeting of the three leaders, meaning a meeting among three parties at once. The main item is nuclear arms control, meaning negotiated limits on nuclear weapons: Trump wants to raise reductions in nuclear arsenals with the leaders of three of the world’s largest nuclear powers.

He is also reported to plan to discuss the war against Iran and is not expected to bring up Russia’s war in Ukraine. China has previously rejected three-way arms talks, and its government has been expanding its arsenal, so whether Beijing will agree to anything beyond a conversation is the open question. Also open are the exact date and venue, whether any joint statement or agreement is planned, and what, if anything, the three leaders will sign.

Tanker Strikes a Docked River Cruise Ship in New Orleans; All Aboard Are Accounted For, With No One Killed or Critically Hurt

A tanker, a ship that carries liquid cargo such as oil or fuel, struck a river cruise ship on the Mississippi River in New Orleans on Friday, and rescue crews were sent to the scene. The cruise ship was docked when it was hit. All passengers and crew were accounted for, and no one was killed or critically hurt.

Officials cited in one account put the number of injured at more than 30, and President Trump said in a post that nobody was killed and thanked the Coast Guard, law enforcement and other first responders. What caused the collision, how many people were hurt in all and what happens next for river traffic have not been confirmed. Tankers and passenger vessels share the lower Mississippi with barges and cargo ships, which makes the stretch through New Orleans one of the busiest in the country, and investigators typically examine steering, equipment and crew decisions in sequence.

Measles Cases Top 5,400 and Deaths Reach 10; One Report Says the U.S. Has Lost Its Measles-Elimination Status

Measles cases across the United States have topped 5,400 and deaths have risen to 10 after new reports in Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. Measles is a highly contagious viral disease that can cause pneumonia and brain swelling, and vaccination is the main protection against it.

One report says the country has also lost its measles-elimination status, the formal designation that a disease is no longer spreading continuously within a country’s borders. That report says the finding follows a resurgence that began with a large outbreak in West Texas in January 2025. The loss of status is not yet confirmed by a health-agency announcement, and the 5,400 case count comes from a single tally. Measles spreads through the air when an infected person coughs or breathes, and it is among the most contagious diseases known, which is why high vaccination rates are needed to keep outbreaks from taking hold.
