Storms disrupt Election Day voting in Florida and the Southwest, and election officials move some polling places

Flooding changed where some people vote. The full list of moved sites has not been confirmed, and voting is continuing.

Storms and flooding disrupted voting in parts of the country on Election Day, and local election officials moved polling places so people could keep voting. In Florida, flash flooding forced county election offices to relocate some voters, and a count of the moved sites has not been confirmed.

A move does not change the clock. Unless a judge or an election official orders otherwise, polls close at their normal hour, and anyone in line at closing time may still vote. Whether any county will extend voting hours was not confirmed as of 5 p.m. Eastern. A voter who is sent to a different site, or whose name is missing from the list at the site they reach, can ask for a provisional ballot, which is set aside and counted once election workers confirm the voter is eligible. County election offices post moved locations on their websites, and state election offices run lookup tools. The earliest polls close at 6 p.m. Eastern, in parts of Indiana and Kentucky, and most states close between 7 and 9 p.m. No results exist yet; the first will come after those closing times.

Politics · Turnout✓ 3 outlets · final turnout; totals differ by tracker

Early voting ran ahead of 2022 in Texas before Election Day, state figures show; no state has released results

In Texas, 23.4% of registered voters had voted in person or by mail in the first seven days of early voting, according to the Texas secretary of state, compared with 24.3% at the same point in 2018. That pace was ahead of the 2022 midterms but slightly behind 2018. National trackers publish early-vote totals too, but they count mail ballots and in-person votes differently, so their figures do not match. Early-vote counts show how many people have voted, not how they voted, and no state has released results. Some states count early and mail ballots quickly after polls close, while others, including California, accept mail ballots that arrive days later, so the order in which results appear is not the order of the final count.

Law · Supreme Court✓ 3 outlets · how the justices lean; when a ruling comes

Supreme Court hears Catholic parish's challenge to Colorado's universal preschool rules

The Supreme Court heard oral argument today in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, No. 25-581, a case over whether Colorado can bar a Catholic preschool from its universal preschool program, which pays for preschool for the state's four-year-olds, because the school will not follow the program's nondiscrimination rules. Those rules include admitting children of same-sex couples. The parish says the requirement forces it to act against its beliefs and asks for a religious exemption under the First Amendment's guarantee of free exercise of religion. Colorado says the rule applies to every preschool in the program equally.

The Tenth Circuit sided with Colorado, citing Employment Division v. Smith, a 1990 decision that generally lets states enforce a neutral law of general applicability even when it burdens a religious practice. The Court agreed on April 20 to hear the case, and the solicitor general, the administration's lawyer before the Court, has urged the justices to rule for the parish. A decision is expected in the months ahead.

Law · Courts✓ 3 outlets · whether the administration refiles

Federal judge dismisses Trump administration lawsuit demanding Harvard admissions data

A federal judge on Monday dismissed a lawsuit in which the Trump administration demanded admissions data from Harvard University, including student application essays. The ruling leaves the door open to a future challenge, so the administration could try again with a different filing or argument. Harvard has been in a dispute with the administration on several fronts, and the admissions data was one of the demands the university resisted.

Transport · Aviation safety✓ 2 outlets · cause of the crash

Investigators say a Learjet may have been breaking up before it crashed on a Florida road, killing all nine aboard

The National Transportation Safety Board said Monday that a Learjet that crashed onto a Florida roadway on Sunday may have been coming apart before it hit the ground. All nine people aboard died. Investigators later said they had found pieces of the plane's tail, which they will examine for signs of how the aircraft failed. Among the dead was the jet's 20-year-old co-pilot, whom family remembered for her early love of flying.

The board has not given a cause. Investigators typically study the wreckage, the flight data and voice recordings, maintenance records and air-traffic control tapes, and a final finding takes many months. The board's early statements describe what the wreckage suggests, not why it happened.
