Fed holds rates steady on a 10-2 vote as two officials push for a hike

The central bank's last big decision before the midterm elections leaves borrowing costs where they were, even as inflation climbs.

The Federal Reserve left its benchmark interest rate unchanged on Wednesday, but the decision was not unanimous. The Federal Open Market Committee, the panel of 12 voting officials that sets U.S. interest rates, voted 10-2 to hold the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans and the anchor for most mortgage, car and credit card costs. The two officials who voted no, Cook and Hammack, both wanted to raise rates instead. A formal vote against the majority is called a dissent, and two of them on one decision is a clear sign that part of the committee thinks the fight against rising inflation needs more force. The target range for the rate starts at 3.75%. It was the Fed's final major decision before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh told reporters afterward that the central bank will deliver price stability, one half of the Fed's dual mandate, which also includes keeping unemployment low.

Wall Street took the news calmly. The S&P 500 closed up 0.28% at 7,743.98, the Nasdaq rose 0.27% to 26,549.85, and the Dow gained 0.19% to 52,475.10. The 10-year Treasury yield, which is what the government pays to borrow for ten years and which feeds into long-term loan rates, ended the day at 5.045%, up 0.052 percentage point. Outside voices were more pointed. Former Fed Vice Chair Richard Clarida said he expects the Fed to raise rates in December. Economist Claudia Sahm said there is a strong economic argument for raising by a quarter point right away and that the Fed's communication has become confusing. The Fed is not alone in leaning that way: the Bank of Canada raised its overnight rate by 25 basis points, a quarter of a percentage point, to 2.5% on Wednesday, its first hike since 2023. The Fed itself has not said it will raise rates in December, and the committee's next meeting will show whether the two dissenters are joined by others.

Dolores Huerta, farmworker organizer and civil rights icon, dies at 96

Dolores Huerta, who co-founded the United Farm Workers with César Chávez and spent more than six decades fighting for people who pick the nation's fruit and vegetables, died Wednesday in California at age 96. The union she helped launch was the first enduring labor union for poor farmworkers in the United States, and it set out to improve pay, safety and living conditions for a workforce that was mostly Latino. For decades she served as the emotional compass of the movement, negotiating contracts, leading boycotts and lobbying lawmakers. Huerta was also a feminist and a civil rights figure whose name reached far beyond the fields. Her organizing slogan, 'Sí, se puede,' became a rallying cry for later campaigns on immigration, wages and voting. Tributes poured in from labor groups and political leaders on Wednesday. Funeral and memorial plans have not been announced.

Judge rejects Justice Department bid to set aside about 20,000 California ballots

A federal judge refused on Tuesday night to order California to set aside roughly 20,000 ballots, rejecting an unprecedented Justice Department demand days before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. The department had asked the court to make the state segregate, meaning physically separate from the rest, ballots cast by people it claimed were not U.S. citizens, and to stop processing them. The judge denied that request, so California counties can keep counting. The ruling does not end the case, which remains pending.

The fight is spilling beyond the courtroom. California election official Keaton Denlay said federal agents handed him a poorly formatted Excel spreadsheet and threatened to prosecute him if he did not act on its claims of voter fraud. The Justice Department has not publicly detailed the evidence behind its citizenship claims, and it has not said whether it will appeal or file a new request before Election Day.

Kennedy lets pharmacies make six unapproved peptides despite FDA scientist objections

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced Wednesday that the Department of Health and Human Services will let small compounding pharmacies, which mix customized medicines outside factory production lines, make six peptides that the Food and Drug Administration has not approved. Peptides are short chains of amino acids that online influencers promote for muscle growth, recovery and weight loss. The permission is a temporary shortcut that bypasses the usual approval process, and it is a unilateral declaration by the secretary.

The move drew resistance inside the government. FDA scientists argued against loosening access to unproven peptide drugs, and their objections were overridden. Supporters say consumers already buy these products and that regulated pharmacies are safer than gray-market sellers. Critics note there is little safety data for the six compounds. It is not yet clear how long the temporary permission lasts or how soon pharmacies will start making the injections.

Brewers beat Rays 4-1 in Game 5 for first World Series title in franchise history

The Milwaukee Brewers beat the Tampa Bay Rays 4-1 in Game 5 on Wednesday night to win the World Series, the first championship in the franchise's history. The clincher closed out the series four games to one and ended a long wait for a club that had never won baseball's final round.

Catcher William Contreras was named World Series MVP after hitting .381 in the series. He also finished with 10 ABS overturns, calls reversed after a player challenged a ball or strike through the automated ball-strike system, which uses cameras and software to judge pitches. The Brewers have not yet announced details of a victory parade.
