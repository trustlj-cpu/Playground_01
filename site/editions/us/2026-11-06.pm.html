Newsom Commutes the Sentences of All 548 People on California's Death Row to Life Without Parole

The governor says state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values. The order does not repeal the death penalty, and prosecutors can still seek it in new cases.

Gov. Gavin Newsom announced on Thursday, Nov. 5, that he is commuting the death sentences of all 548 people on California's death row to life in prison without the possibility of parole. A commutation is a governor's order that lowers a punishment without erasing the conviction, so none of the 548 is cleared of the crime; each is to remain in prison for life. Newsom said that "state-sanctioned killing" is incompatible with the values of California, the most populous state. The 548 sentences amount to roughly a quarter of all death sentences in the country, so a single order changed the status of a very large share of the nation's condemned prisoners. The decision comes in the closing weeks of his time in office, which ends in January 2027, and it means a future governor cannot carry out these executions.

The order does not abolish the death penalty in California, and prosecutors can still ask for death sentences in future cases. Executions in the state have been on hold under a moratorium, a formal pause, that Newsom ordered earlier in his term, so the practical change is that the pause becomes permanent for these 548 prisoners. Life without parole means there is no hearing at which release could be considered. Defense attorney Mark Geragos, speaking on a cable news program, said the move is "impossible to view outside a political lens." It is not yet known how prosecutors, victims' families or the courts will respond, whether anyone will file a legal challenge, or when and how the prisoners will be moved out of death row housing.

Employers Added 165,000 Jobs in October, Far Above Forecasts, and Unemployment Slipped to 4.1%

U.S. employers added 165,000 jobs in October, the government's monthly jobs report showed on Friday, Nov. 6, well above forecasts. One forecast had put the gain at 59,000, so the actual number was nearly three times that figure. The unemployment rate, the share of people who want work but cannot find it, slipped to 4.1% from 4.2%. Payroll figures for the previous two months were also revised upward, meaning the earlier readings turned out to be somewhat stronger than first reported. Payrolls count the jobs at employers outside of farms, so the number is a measure of hiring across most of the economy. Stocks ended the day mostly higher after Thursday's rout. The S&P 500 closed at 7,376.86, up 0.31%, and the Nasdaq Composite closed at 24,579.42, up 1.09%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 0.46% to 51,285.73. Thursday's drops of 4.36% for the S&P 500 and 5.52% for the Nasdaq were far larger, so Friday's gains recovered only a small part of them; the S&P 500 would need to rise more than 4% to return to its Wednesday close. No official or company has said what drove Friday's rebound.

Two Top Justice Department Officials Resign Friday, a Day After Civil Rights Chief Harmeet Dhillon Quit

Attorney General Todd Blanche announced on Friday, Nov. 6, that two senior Justice Department officials have resigned: Jason Reding Quiñones, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Florida, and John Eisenberg, an assistant attorney general. Quiñones's office has been running the so-called grand conspiracy investigation, an inquiry into federal law enforcement officials who took part in earlier investigations of President Trump. The announcement followed the Thursday resignation of Harmeet Dhillon, who has headed the department's Civil Rights Division since her confirmation last year and who sharply changed the division's direction.

A U.S. attorney is the chief federal prosecutor for a region, and an assistant attorney general leads one of the department's divisions; the president appoints both. The reasons for the three resignations have not been confirmed. Who will lead the Florida office and the Civil Rights Division, and whether the grand conspiracy investigation continues unchanged, are not yet known.

Jay Rockefeller, Former West Virginia Governor and U.S. Senator, Dies at 89

Jay Rockefeller, who served as governor of West Virginia and then for decades as a U.S. senator from the state, has died at 89, according to reports on Friday, Nov. 6. He was the last member of his family's political dynasty to hold elected office. He came from one of the richest families in the country and spent about half a century in public service in West Virginia, one of the poorest states. In the Senate he was a Democrat and an advocate on health care, and he left the chamber in January 2015. A governor leads a state's executive branch, while senators serve six-year terms, so a long Senate career spans many presidencies. The date, place and cause of death, and funeral arrangements, have not been announced.

Paraguay's President Resigns Over the Collapse of a Digital Bank That Froze Customers' Savings

Paraguay's president, Santiago Peña, resigned on Thursday, Nov. 5, after the collapse of Ueno Bank, a digital lender whose failure froze its customers' savings. Peña apologized for failing to prevent the crisis. Regulators had placed Ueno into resolution, a procedure in which authorities take control of a failing bank, after its solvency ratio, a measure of the capital a bank holds against its risks, fell below half the legal minimum. The central bank's chief, Carlos Carvallo, resigned on Nov. 3 at Peña's request. Peña had been a business partner of the group that owns Ueno until March 2025; the bank says he was never a shareholder or investor.

One outlet reported that lawmakers voted to impeach Peña minutes after he resigned; that has not been confirmed elsewhere. A prosecutor has said charges will be sought against him. Before resigning, Peña had called the failure "a particular case of one entity, not of the national financial system" and announced a plan with congressional leaders to use a national guarantee fund to repay depositors, the customers whose money is held at the bank. Who is running the country now, whether the impeachment vote took place and what it would mean once a president has resigned, and whether depositors will be repaid in full have not been confirmed.
