Trump Says He ‘Would Not Mind’ Seeing Jensen Huang Back at Nvidia Days After the Chief Executive Resigned

Huang resigned as Nvidia’s chief executive on Nov. 12; no return has been announced, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is reported to have urged the company to look outside.

President Donald Trump said Monday, Nov. 16, that he “would not mind” seeing Jensen Huang return as chief executive of Nvidia, days after Huang resigned amid reports about his relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence agencies are reported to suspect of spying for China. Speaking to reporters before leaving for Asia, Trump said national security was not put at risk and that nobody has proven the woman, Alice Huang, was a spy. He added that he may raise the matter with Chinese President Xi Jinping, whom he is due to meet on the Asia trip, and that Huang probably should not have done what he did on a personal basis. Alice Huang is a former executive at Megaspeed, a company that says she left in August 2025; the allegation that she spied for China has not been proven publicly, and Huang has not commented publicly, according to reports. Huang quit as Nvidia’s president, chief executive and a board member on Thursday, Nov. 12, according to a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is serving as interim chief executive while the board searches for a permanent successor.

A petition calling for Huang’s return has drawn well over 100,000 signatures, with counts differing by report; one report says Elon Musk signed it. Neither Huang nor Nvidia has commented on a return. Bessent is reported to have urged Nvidia to bring in an outside chief executive, which rests on one report. Neither the president’s remarks nor the petition decides the question; that rests with Nvidia’s board, which has so far said only that it is searching for a permanent successor.

Commerce Moves to Restrict China’s Remote Access to U.S. AI Chips as Trump Heads to Asia

The Commerce Department issued an interim final rule on Monday, Nov. 16, that requires companies to get licenses before shipping advanced AI chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world if those facilities might supply computing power to companies in China or other restricted countries. An interim final rule takes effect without the usual comment period first. The rule targets a loophole that let Chinese companies use American chips by renting computing power, a practice known as remote access, without the hardware ever being shipped to China. One report says the rule also adds license requirements for shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, and exempts some commercial recommendation systems, such as those used by online retailers. The rule arrives as the administration faces questions over Nvidia’s China business after the scandal around Jensen Huang. Trump told reporters Monday that the scandal did not put national security at risk and defended Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, while one report says Bessent questioned whether suspect chip sales to China boosted Nvidia’s earnings. Trump is due to meet Xi on his Asia trip, with one report putting the meeting at Nov. 19. How China will respond has not been announced.

Supreme Court Turns Away Trump Administration Appeal in University of California Research-Grant Case

The Supreme Court on Monday, Nov. 16, declined to take up the Trump administration’s appeal in a case over research grants cut at the University of California under the administration’s anti-diversity orders. The order leaves in place a Ninth Circuit ruling that partly upheld a lower-court order blocking the terminations, which that court found likely violate the First Amendment. The case, known as Thakur v. Trump, was brought by UC researchers whose grants from federal agencies, including the National Science Foundation, were ended. Reports differ on the money involved: one puts it at about $59 million, another at about $150 million.

The order is not a nationwide ruling, because declining an appeal sets no rule for other courts. In August 2025 the justices let the National Institutes of Health cancel about $783 million in similar diversity-related grants in a 5-4 emergency order, saying such claims likely belong in the Court of Federal Claims rather than district courts. Whether other universities can use the Ninth Circuit’s reasoning to protect their own grants is open, and no next step from the administration has been announced.

Iran Sends Drones at Bahrain Twice in Three Days, Claiming Retaliation Against the U.S.; Bahrain Says It Intercepted Them

Bahrain said its defenses intercepted Iranian drones on Sunday, Nov. 15, the second Iranian attack on the small Gulf kingdom in three days. Bahrain hosts the headquarters of the U.S. Navy’s Fifth Fleet and a large American military presence. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said they fired at a U.S. air-defense radar at a base in Bahrain and described the strikes as retaliation against the United States; one report puts the number of drones at six. One report says no casualties or damage were reported.

The strikes came after months of relative quiet. U.S. Central Command reportedly said it shot down two Iranian drones in the earlier attack on Friday. No U.S. confirmation of Iran’s account of what it targeted on Sunday has been reported, and Iran’s claim about the radar is unverified. Neither Washington nor Tehran has said what comes next.

Trump Blames ‘Old Equipment’ for Friday’s Nationwide Ground Stop as Investigators Examine Two Routers and See No Sign of a Foreign Cyberattack

President Trump said Monday that the nationwide FAA ground stop on Friday, Nov. 13, was caused by old equipment, and he denied responsibility. Air traffic was halted across the country for more than four hours, and delays and cancellations were still rippling through the system on Sunday. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has said investigators are examining two routers and see no sign of a foreign cyberattack. He has also said the routers’ ability to broadcast data has been turned off while the investigation continues.

Duffy has said work by a contractor, L3Harris, replacing a copper line with fiber at a remote site in El Dorado, Arkansas, may have caused a router to flood the FAA’s network with data; this rests on one report. Whether the cause was human error, an installation mistake or faulty equipment has not been determined, and the contractor’s responsibility has not been established. A ground stop is an order that keeps planes on the ground until a problem is fixed.
