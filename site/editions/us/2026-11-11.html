Republicans pick Jim Jordan to lead the House GOP and keep John Thune as Senate leader

Closed-door votes on Tuesday put the Freedom Caucus co-founder ahead of Steve Scalise and kept the Senate leadership team in place as Congress returns for a short lame-duck session.

House Republicans chose Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio on Tuesday as their leader for the next Congress, which convenes in January. The vote was a closed-door secret ballot, and Jordan beat Majority Leader Steve Scalise by a reported 106 to 73. Jordan is a co-founder of the Freedom Caucus, the hard-right bloc that has pushed House leaders to the right for a decade, and he takes over a job that Speaker Mike Johnson said last week he would not seek. The contest was a rematch of the 2023 fight between the two men over who would become speaker after Kevin McCarthy was removed from that post. The title attached to the job is minority leader: the party’s chief strategist and spokesman in the chamber. Some House races from the November 3 vote are still uncalled, and certification of final results is continuing state by state, so the exact size of each party’s margin is not yet settled.

In the Senate, Republicans kept John Thune of South Dakota as their leader after a long closed-door meeting. A group led by Sen. Ron Johnson of Wisconsin, with Sen. Josh Hawley of Missouri joining the calls, had pushed to postpone the leadership vote, arguing that reelecting the same leaders without a discussion would send the wrong message. Senators rejected the delay, spent several hours talking through the election, and then reinstated Thune and the rest of the top team. Thune told colleagues his priorities were a plan for operating as the minority and getting as much done as possible in the last two months of the current Congress. Those two months are the lame-duck session, the stretch after an election when the outgoing Congress can still pass bills. Democrats are expected to hold their own leadership elections on November 18 and 19.

Average U.S. gasoline price falls below $4 a gallon for the first time since July

The national average price of a gallon of regular gasoline dropped under $4 on Tuesday, November 10, for the first time since July. AAA’s daily figure was about $3.98, down from roughly $4.02 on Sunday and from $4.10 in its November 5 weekly report, which had already recorded a 12-cent fall in a single week. For a driver who fills a 15-gallon tank, that is about $1.80 less than on November 5. The price is still high for the season. Before this year the national average had never topped $4 in any November, and it remains more than a dollar above a year ago. Prices differ widely by state: on Monday, 26 states were already under $4, and North Carolina was lowest at $3.26. Before the war with Iran began, gasoline averaged $2.98 a gallon. Oil analyst Andrew Lipow said prices could stay under $4 for a few months even without a lasting truce in that war. AAA’s next weekly update is expected Thursday.

Vehicle crashes into crowd outside North Hollywood Metro station, killing three

A vehicle plowed into people on the sidewalk outside the North Hollywood Metro station in Los Angeles at about 5:55 p.m. Tuesday, killing three and leaving others critically hurt. It struck near Lankershim and Chandler boulevards during the evening rush. Mayor Karen Bass said the victims included street vendors and pedestrians, and witnesses said at least two of the dead were food vendors who had worked outside the station for years. Reports differ on how many people were seriously injured, giving figures from two to three or more, and on whether the vehicle was an SUV or a minivan.

The driver, a man in his early 60s, had to be freed from the vehicle, was conscious and appeared to be in shock, and was taken to a hospital. LAPD Capt. Warner Castillo said, “We don’t know if it was done intentionally, if it was done by accident,” and investigators are checking whether the driver was impaired. Police Chief Jim McDonnell said officers will review video from the area in what he called a “prolonged but very deliberate investigation.” It is not known whether the driver suffered a medical emergency before the crash, and no charges have been announced.

Appeals court says Justice Department demand for reporters’ LinkedIn records is too broad

A federal appeals court ruled Tuesday that a Justice Department demand for reporters’ LinkedIn records went too far. The government wanted metadata covering 18 months of the journalists’ activity on the platform, which would have named every person they communicated with, though not the content of messages. The demand came in a leak investigation. The Fourth Circuit panel, in a unanimous opinion by Judge J. Harvie Wilkinson joined by Judges Andrew Wynn and Nicole Berner, found the request overbroad and said it could reveal unrelated confidential sources. A related order that barred LinkedIn from telling its users about the disclosure for a year was also found too broad.

The court did not strike the demand down entirely. It denied LinkedIn’s request to quash, meaning cancel, the order outright, rejected the government’s claim that the company had shown no injury, and sent the case back to a lower court to narrow what must be handed over. The Fourth Circuit covers Maryland, Virginia, West Virginia and the Carolinas, so the ruling binds courts there and not across the country, and federal law still has no broad shield law protecting reporters from such demands. What the narrower order will look like, and whether the government appeals, are not known.

United States sends no official delegation to the COP31 climate summit in Turkey

The U.S. government is not sending a delegation to COP31, the United Nations climate summit that opened Monday in Antalya, Turkey, and runs through November 20. The State Department told a news outlet by email that no delegation will go. It is the second year in a row that Washington has stayed away from the annual talks, after the administration formally withdrew from the Paris Agreement, the 2015 deal to limit global warming, in January.

The American presence will be unofficial. Rep. Sean Casten, an Illinois Democrat, said a 19-member group of lawmakers organized by the nonpartisan SEEC Institute will attend, and governors, mayors and other local leaders are expected under the America Is All In coalition. Of the leaders of the Group of Seven rich democracies, only Britain’s prime minister is reported to be scheduled to appear. Two weeks of negotiations lie ahead, and what an unofficial U.S. group can achieve without a seat at the table is not known.
