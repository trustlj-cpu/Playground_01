Alaska's top federal prosecutor confirms criminal investigation of Senate ballot scheme as Justice Department asks court to separate ballots

U.S. Attorney Michael Heyman declined to name targets or charges, and Alaska says setting ballots apart would break ballot secrecy.

The top federal prosecutor in Alaska confirmed Friday that a criminal investigation is under way into an alleged scheme to put a second Dan Sullivan on the state's Senate ballot. U.S. Attorney Michael Heyman said, "There is both a civil investigation and a criminal investigation that is ongoing," and that the criminal inquiry has run for months. The allegation is that Democrats recruited an independent named Daniel J. Sullivan, whose name is nearly identical to that of the Republican incumbent, Sen. Dan Sullivan, to confuse voters and pull votes away from him. Heyman declined to say who the targets are, what charges could follow or which law is at issue, and said he is not assuming a crime was committed. Mary Peltola, the Democratic nominee, has said she knew nothing about any candidate on the ballot other than herself and the incumbent.

The Justice Department also asked a federal judge Friday for a preliminary injunction, an early court order issued before a case is decided, directing Alaska to preserve and segregate ballots on which voters rank Daniel J. Sullivan. It asked for a decision on the separation request by Monday, one day before Election Day. Alaska opposes the request and calls the lawsuit meritless. The state says ballots are mixed together on Election Day, so pulling some aside would break ballot secrecy, and that it already keeps ballots for 22 months. Gov. Mike Dunleavy has ordered a state investigation. Democrats have condemned the civil suit and the criminal investigation as an effort to influence the outcome. No ruling on the request has been announced.

Judge dismisses Trump's Epstein-letter defamation suit against Dow Jones and Murdoch with prejudice

A federal judge in Miami on Saturday threw out President Trump's defamation lawsuit over a newspaper report on a birthday letter to Jeffrey Epstein, and this time the dismissal is with prejudice, which means Trump cannot file the case again in that court. Judge Darrin Gayles ruled that Trump did not plausibly allege actual malice, the legal standard that requires a public figure to show a publisher knew a statement was false or recklessly ignored whether it was true. Because it was Trump's second attempt to state his claims, Gayles wrote, dismissal with prejudice was appropriate. He had dismissed the first version of the suit in April without prejudice, which let Trump file an amended complaint. The defendants include Dow Jones, which publishes the newspaper, and Rupert Murdoch. The report described a letter bearing Trump's signature in a 2003 album compiled for Epstein's 50th birthday. Trump has denied writing it, and the suit originally sought $10 billion. In April the judge noted that who wrote the letter is a question of fact that could not be settled at that stage, and Saturday's order does not decide it. A spokesman for Trump said he "will continue to press forward." A dismissal with prejudice can be taken to a higher court on appeal, but no appeal has been confirmed.

Virginia and D.C. sue to stop Trump's proposed 250-foot arch as the administration aims to start construction Nov. 5

The District of Columbia and Virginia sued Saturday in federal court to stop President Trump's proposed 250-foot "Triumphal Arch" at Memorial Circle, the traffic circle on the Virginia side of the Potomac River. D.C. Attorney General Brian Schwalb and Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones filed the complaint against the National Park Service and the Interior Department. They argue that Congress never authorized the monument, that it would nearly double the 130-foot height limit that applies to buildings in the capital, and that the administration skipped required federal reviews. The complaint says construction would take two to three years, close parts of the circle and cut Memorial Bridge to two westbound lanes. The administration has said it plans to begin construction Nov. 5 after final plans are approved. The new suit joins others. Arlington County sued Oct. 8, saying the government skipped the review required by the National Environmental Policy Act, and Vietnam War veterans and an architectural historian sued in February, arguing the Commemorative Works Act requires congressional approval. U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan is weighing whether to halt the project while that case proceeds.

Mangione's lawyers press judge to dismiss New York murder charges, arguing double jeopardy after his federal plea

Lawyers for Luigi Mangione are again asking Judge Gregory Carro of Manhattan to dismiss the state murder and weapons charges over the killing of UnitedHealthcare chief executive Brian Thompson, arguing that a second prosecution would violate the Constitution's ban on double jeopardy, being tried twice for the same conduct. Mangione has pleaded guilty to federal stalking charges and awaits federal sentencing in December. The defense argues that the conduct behind the stalking offense is "coterminous with," meaning identical to, the conduct in the murder case, and says there "is and was no other gun" involved in either one.

The Manhattan District Attorney's office disagrees. It argues that stalking and murder are separate offenses, so double jeopardy does not apply. The fake-ID charge is not part of the dismissal request. Carro earlier called the argument premature while the federal case was pending, and in September he dismissed the state terrorism count. He will hear the motion Dec. 10, and his decision can be appealed. The state murder trial has been delayed indefinitely until the question is settled.

Binance says it has restored backing for 90% of the $6.3 billion hack and plans to resume withdrawals

Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, said Saturday that it has restored backing for 90% of the roughly $6.3 billion in assets affected by the hack that began Thursday, when money started moving out of its wallets and the exchange suspended deposits and withdrawals. The company says it is covering the losses directly and plans to make up the remaining 10%, about $630 million. It also plans to resume withdrawals, subject to security checks, as soon as Sunday. In practice, for every $10 customers had at risk, about $9 has been put back behind their accounts, and customers can reach it again only once the exchange reopens.

The $6.3 billion is Binance's own estimate of assets affected, not a confirmed final loss, and about $3 billion of it was frozen by other crypto firms earlier in the week. Private analytics firms have tied the attack to a North Korean hacking unit, but no government has confirmed that link. Still unknown are how the last 10% will be covered, whether withdrawals resume on schedule and who carried out the attack.
