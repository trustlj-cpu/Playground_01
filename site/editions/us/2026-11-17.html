United Airlines Says a Widespread System Outage Delayed Departures Monday, Days After a Nationwide Ground Stop

The airline says the problem is resolved and that it saw no indication of a cybersecurity cause, but the disruption lands while travelers are still unsettled by last week's stoppage.

United Airlines said Monday that a widespread system outage had delayed flight departures, and later in the day it said the problem was resolved. The airline called it a technology issue that delayed departures and said there was no indication the problem was related to cybersecurity. Flights resumed once its systems came back. The disruption was brief, but it came only days after a nationwide ground stop, an order that tells airports to hold planes at the gate, shut down flights across the country last week, and it added to concerns about the reliability of air travel.

A ground stop and an airline's own computer outage are different things. The ground stop is a federal order that applies to every airline; Monday's outage was inside one carrier's systems, and nothing made public connects the two. United has not said how many flights were delayed, which system failed or how long the worst of it lasted, and none of those details has been confirmed. Another large U.S. company, JPMorgan Chase, reported a separate software problem the same day, and no link between the two has been established. The open questions are whether United will publish a cause and whether the delays carry into Tuesday's schedule.

Wall Street Ends Monday Sharply Lower: Nasdaq Composite Falls 2.49%, S&P 500 Drops 1.92%

U.S. stocks closed sharply lower on Monday. The S&P 500 fell 145 points, or 1.92%, to 7,416.59. The Nasdaq Composite lost about 627 points, or 2.49%, to 24,509.76, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average gave up about 739 points, or 1.41%, to 51,608.17. In household terms, $10,000 invested in an S&P 500 index fund at Friday's close would have been worth about $9,808 at Monday's close. Bonds moved the other way. The 10-year Treasury yield, the interest rate the government pays to borrow for ten years, slipped to 5.121% from 5.154%, a drop of about 3 basis points; a basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point. The closing figures come from market data feeds, and no single cause for the day's decline has been established here. Market coverage flagged Nvidia's upcoming earnings report as the next major company event, though the date has not been confirmed. Tuesday's session will show whether Monday's losses extend or reverse.

CDC Reportedly Declined to Alert Doctors to Measles Risks in Pregnancy; a Journal Has Now Released the Data

Doctors across the United States are reportedly seeing more pregnant women and newborns with serious measles complications as the virus spreads again. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reportedly declined to send an alert about the risk in pregnancy, and a medical journal has now released the underlying data. According to people familiar with the matter in an earlier report, CDC scientists tried to warn clinicians through the agency's Health Alert Network, the system it uses to push urgent notices to doctors, but the alert needed sign-off from the director's office and was reportedly not approved, with no reason given. The Department of Health and Human Services declined to confirm or comment on that earlier report.

The medical stakes are well established. Measles during pregnancy raises the chance of pneumonia, hospitalization, pregnancy loss and preterm birth, meaning birth before 37 weeks, and the virus can pass to a baby before birth as congenital measles. Pregnant women cannot get the MMR vaccine because it contains live virus; they can receive immunoglobulin, a shot of ready-made antibodies. Pennsylvania health officials have issued a statewide alert on measles in pregnancy. What the journal's figures show, and whether the CDC will now issue an alert of its own, have not been confirmed.

Court Filing Reportedly Shows the Administration Sought LinkedIn Data to Identify a Newspaper's Sources

A court filing reportedly confirms that the Trump administration sought data from LinkedIn in an effort to identify the confidential sources of reporters at a national newspaper. The filing is said to show two parallel efforts: the LinkedIn request and earlier subpoenas, which are legal orders compelling someone to hand over records. Those earlier subpoenas, disclosed in July, sought phone records of several of the paper's reporters and some of their relatives, in an effort to identify sources for stories about a jet gifted by Qatar for use as Air Force One.

The Justice Department said at the time that its subpoenas complied with federal law and internal policy, and a judge froze enforcement of the phone-records subpoenas while the newspaper's motion to quash, a request asking a court to cancel a subpoena, is decided. Whether LinkedIn handed over anything, what data was sought, and whether the Justice Department will comment on the LinkedIn request have not been confirmed, and the LinkedIn details so far come from a single report.

Tesla and SpaceX Already Share Engineers and Projects as Musk Weighs a Merger, a Report Says

Elon Musk's two best-known companies already overlap heavily, a report says: for years engineers have moved between Tesla and SpaceX, and the two have worked together on major projects. The overlap matters because Musk said on SpaceX's Oct. 20 earnings webcast that both companies had formed special committees, board-level groups set up to study a possible deal, to explore a merger. He added that calling a merger a certainty would be overstating it.

A separate report says prediction markets, where traders bet real money on outcomes, put the odds of a Tesla-SpaceX merger by April 2027 above 60%. Those odds measure traders' bets, not any decision by either company. Neither company has announced a merger agreement, no price or exchange ratio has been disclosed, and the committees' timetable has not been made public. Any deal would likely face scrutiny from shareholders and regulators.
