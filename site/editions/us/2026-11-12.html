Brooklyn Building Collapse Kills at Least 2 and Hurts at Least 17, Officials Say

Part of a three-story building in Flatbush gave way Wednesday; four of the injured are in critical condition, and fire officials warn the toll could rise.

Part of a three-story building at 2625 Bedford Avenue in Flatbush, Brooklyn, collapsed on Wednesday, Nov. 11, and the New York City Fire Department says at least two people died and at least 17 were hurt, seven of them children. Four of the injured are in critical condition and more than 10 others have injuries. The count moved through the evening: early reports said one person had died, and the figure rose to two as crews worked through the rubble. The department has warned that the toll could climb, because it was not yet clear how many people were inside or whether everyone had been accounted for. It had not released the names or ages of those who died. Seven children among 17 injured means roughly two in five of the known casualties are minors.

The building stood beside an open construction site, and what brought it down has not been established. Brooklyn’s district attorney said his office is working with the city Department of Buildings and law enforcement to determine whether any criminal conduct contributed to the collapse. That is an inquiry, not a finding. Officials, including the mayor, gave the casualty figures to reporters overnight, and the numbers may change again as searchers finish clearing the wreckage. The open questions are who the two victims were, how many people were in the building when it gave way, how the structure failed, and whether work next door played any part. Officials have not said when a cause might be known.

Reports Say Nvidia Board Is Preparing to Remove CEO Huang; Company Has Announced No Decision

Late Wednesday, a business newspaper reported that Nvidia’s board is preparing to remove chief executive Jensen Huang, citing people familiar with the matter. A separate newspaper report, also built on unnamed sources, said the board was meeting to discuss his future, and other outlets repeated the accounts. Reports add that directors are considering an interim chief executive while they look for a permanent one. All of this comes from anonymous sources: Nvidia has announced no decision and no change in Huang’s role. Nvidia shares fell more than 8% in after-hours trading, the stretch of trading after the regular session closes, when prices can swing on thin volume. The reports come after Huang acknowledged an affair in an email to employees earlier this week, and after the board opened an independent review run by independent directors, the board members who are not company executives. The newspaper account on Wednesday also alleged a second relationship, with an executive who reports to Huang; Nvidia had not publicly responded to that claim as of early Thursday. Wall Street analyst Dan Ives estimated that losing Huang could cut up to 30% of Nvidia’s value, roughly $1.2 trillion, a forecast, not a measurement. Still unknown: whether the board has voted, what the review has found, and when the company will speak.

Hyde-Smith Concedes Mississippi Senate Race to Colom; Returns Remain Unofficial Until Certified

Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith conceded Mississippi’s Senate race to Democrat Scott Colom on Wednesday, saying in a statement on X, “I respect the decision of Mississippi voters.” Colom’s campaign said she phoned him and that he told her, “God bless you and thank you, senator.” In a tally with about 99% of the vote counted, Colom had 473,336 votes to her 468,667, a margin of 4,669; independent Ty Pinkins had 20,234. That is 49.2% to 48.7%, a gap of about half a percentage point. On the morning after Election Day the two were separated by fewer than 100 votes, so the lead grew as the count went on. Several news organizations have declared the race for Colom, but those calls rest on unofficial returns. Certification, the formal sign-off by state officials that makes results final, has not been announced, and a concession is a candidate’s statement, not a legal step.

If the count holds through certification, Colom, a prosecutor, would become Mississippi’s third Black senator, after two who served during Reconstruction, the years after the Civil War when Southern states were readmitted to the Union. In North Carolina, Republican Rep. Gregory Murphy also conceded his House race Wednesday, to Democrat Raymond Smith. What remains: certification dates, any challenge to the count, and the timing of swearing-in, which depends on Congress seating its members in January.

Trump Uses Veterans Day Speech at Arlington to Defend His Iran War Policy

President Trump spent much of his Veterans Day remarks at Arlington National Cemetery on Wednesday defending the Iran operation. Standing near the graves of Americans killed in Korea, Vietnam, Iraq and Afghanistan, he listed how long each of those wars lasted and how many Americans died, and said the country “got very little from any of these wars.” By his count Vietnam lasted 21 years and cost 60,000 lives; he said each of those conflicts ran far longer than the Iran fight has. He said the United States did not start the war with Iran, that Tehran “has been waging war on America since 1979,” and that he wants “total victory,” not “93 percent victory.” Critics dispute who started the conflict, and the figures he cited were his own, not an official tally.

The speech came eight days after Election Day. In an Election Day survey of voters, about six in 10 called the decision to use military force against Iran the wrong one. Trump also praised Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who attended, saying he had “done a fantastic job,” and again raised the 250-foot arch he wants built just outside the cemetery. Still unsettled: how long the operation will last, what outcome would count as the “total victory” he described, and whether Congress will take up the war.

White House Letter Says Democrats’ Threatened Investigations Could Be Criminal; Jeffries Calls It ‘Outrageous’

The White House sent a letter Wednesday to House Democrats’ top investigator saying it is “monitoring” what it called abusive and capricious threats to open congressional probes, and that such threats could be criminal and could justify investigations by the executive branch. The letter points to Supreme Court rulings holding that congressional inquiries must serve a legislative purpose and cannot be used to punish political opponents. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries called the letter “outrageous” and said it was “factually inaccurate.” The power of committees to demand documents and testimony from the administration, known as congressional oversight, is broad, and courts have seldom stepped in to stop it.

On the same day, a U.S. attorney sent Sen. Richard Blumenthal a letter demanding that he preserve documents from his inquiry into the White House ballroom project, according to one report; Blumenthal’s response was not available. Such a demand is a routine step before a legal dispute. Still unknown: whether the administration will take any action against a congressional investigation, how Democrats will respond in writing, and which inquiries come first.
