Trump Signs Proclamation Declaring the Oval Office and Cabinet Room a National Monument

Trump says the step will keep later presidents from changing his redesign of the rooms. He says Congress would have to ratify it, and its legal reach is not yet settled.

President Trump signed a proclamation on Thursday, Nov. 5, that declares the Oval Office and the Cabinet Room a national monument, a step he says will stop future administrations from altering the way he has redecorated them. A proclamation is a formal order a president signs on his own authority, and a national monument is a federal designation that normally protects a historic place or object. The White House fact sheet says the order exists "to ensure its preservation, proper care, and management," and the text calls the Oval Office one of the most recognizable and historically significant spaces in the country, tracing its present form to Franklin D. Roosevelt's administration. Trump said the point is to keep his designs from being "ripped down by stupid people." He also said Congress would have to ratify the proclamation, meaning approve it, and that "no major modifications" could be made to the rooms without Congress's approval. In plain terms, the order is meant to freeze the rooms in their present look.

Since returning to the White House, Trump has overseen many design changes to its rooms. The Oval Office now carries portraits, a copy of the Declaration of Independence and gold accents across its surfaces, and the Cabinet Room is the long meeting room where the president sits with the heads of the departments. Presidents have long used the 1906 Antiquities Act to declare monuments on federal land, but it has not been confirmed that this proclamation rests on that law. The proclamation arrived on the same day a federal planning commission was scheduled to consider his proposed 250-foot triumphal arch near Washington, and the outcome of that vote has not been confirmed. Several points are unsettled. The White House fact sheet is titled for the Oval Office alone, while Trump described the order as covering both rooms. It is not known what Congress would be asked to vote on, or whether a later president could cancel the order with a proclamation of his own. No lawsuit has been reported.

Flash Floods Force Dozens of Rescues on the Georgia-Tennessee Line as Georgia Declares an Emergency in at Least Four Counties

Heavy rain and flash flooding hit communities along the Georgia-Tennessee border late Wednesday and into Thursday, forcing dozens of rescues from homes and roads. Six to nine inches of rain fell overnight, and two cities across the two states were closed because of what was described as catastrophic flooding. The National Weather Service issued a flood warning telling residents to seek higher ground now. Georgia's governor declared a state of emergency in at least four counties on Thursday, and McCaysville, a Georgia town on the state line, was among the places flooded overnight. Flash flooding happens when rain falls faster than the ground and drains can absorb it, so streams and streets fill within minutes, which is why rescues often come before any damage count. Which two cities were closed, how many homes were damaged and whether anyone was hurt or killed have not been confirmed. A forecast for more rain in the region has not been confirmed either.

Wisconsin Reports an Infant's Death From Measles, the State's First Since 1990 and the Third in the U.S. This Week

Wisconsin health officials said on Thursday that an infant died in association with a measles infection, the state's first measles-related death since 1990. The state's Division of Public Health announced it in a statement, and the death is the third measles death reported in the United States this week. Measles is among the most contagious diseases known: the virus spreads through the air and can linger in a room for up to two hours after an infected person has left. Wisconsin has been dealing with a growing outbreak, meaning a cluster of cases linked to the same spread, and the state health department had confirmed 267 cases in 2026 as of Oct. 13.

Infants are the most exposed group because the first dose of the measles vaccine is normally given at 12 to 15 months, which leaves younger babies dependent on the people around them being immune, a protection known as herd immunity. The infant's age, home county and the vaccination status of those around the child were not stated in the first announcements, and the places where the other two deaths this week occurred have not been confirmed.

Raman Leads Bass in the Los Angeles Mayor's Count; the County Has Not Yet Certified the Result

City Councilmember Nithya Raman leads Mayor Karen Bass in the unofficial count in the Los Angeles mayor's race released through Thursday, Nov. 5. One report on Thursday said Bass called Raman to concede; Bass had not confirmed a concession in a public statement at the time of writing. Los Angeles County must still complete its canvass, the official tally of every valid ballot, and certify the result before anyone is formally elected. Raman, a Democratic socialist who once endorsed Bass, would become the leader of the second-largest city in the country.

Ballots are still being counted after Election Day, so the final margin can change, and the date on which the county will certify has not been announced. A concession, if Bass makes one publicly, is a political act rather than a legal one: it does not change the count or the certification timeline.

Trump's Plan to Allow Russian Diesel Imports Appears to Falter as Two Tankers Change Course and Officials Warn of Legal Risk

President Trump's plan to allow imports of Russian diesel into the United States ran into trouble this week. State and federal officials warned that anyone handling such shipments could face legal consequences, and two tankers that had been heading for the United States changed course. Asked about the cargoes, Trump said, "We're looking at a law that forbids it." The warnings center on sanctions, the penalties the United States and its allies have placed on Russian energy exports since the invasion of Ukraine. Diesel fuels trucks, farm machinery and trains, so its price reaches well beyond drivers.

Moscow disputes that anything has changed. Putin's envoy Dmitriev called the reports that the tankers had altered course "fake," and the Kremlin said the diesel "will find its buyers" while declining to discuss commercial matters. Where the two tankers are now, who owns the cargoes and which law Trump meant have not been confirmed.
