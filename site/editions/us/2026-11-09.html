Nvidia’s Board Opens an Independent Investigation After CEO Jensen Huang Tells Staff He Had an Affair With an Executive

Huang told employees he was unfaithful and apologized; the board says it has no reason to believe the company’s secrets were compromised, and the House wants a briefing Nov. 18.

Nvidia’s board of directors said Sunday that it is “initiating an independent investigation in light of recent reporting that founder and CEO Jensen Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort.” The same day, Huang sent employees a letter admitting that he had been unfaithful to his wife, apologizing to the company and its customers and writing, “I have fallen short.” The relationship at the center of the inquiry is with Alice Huang, a technology executive whom U.S. officials, according to reporting last week, suspect of passing information to Chinese intelligence. Those officials have not been named, and no criminal charge against either person has been reported. Nvidia has said Huang’s meetings with her were routine business and that no national-security information was disclosed; China’s government has called the spying allegations “groundless speculation.”

The board said it has no reason to believe the company’s secrets or national security were compromised, and the company said Huang “is committed to continuing to lead NVIDIA” and is cooperating. The inquiry follows pressure from Washington that began last week: three House committee chairmen have asked Huang for a closed-door briefing on Nov. 18, the day after Nvidia is due to report third-quarter results. President Trump defended Huang on Sunday night, saying he is a good person and that he has not discussed the allegations with him. One market-news service reported Nvidia shares down about 3.5% in after-hours trading on a single alternative trading venue; regular trading opens this morning, the first full test of how investors weigh the news. The scope of the board’s review and its timetable have not been disclosed, and the claim that Alice Huang worked for Chinese intelligence rests on unnamed U.S. officials, not on a public finding.

Trump Says His Deportation Policy “Might Have Been Too Tough” and He Wants an Immigration Deal With Democrats, in His First Network Interview Since the Midterms

President Trump said in a television interview broadcast Sunday evening that his deportation policy “might have been too tough” and that he would like to work out a deal with congressional Democrats on immigration. Interviewer Ross Douthat asked whether the administration’s approach cost Republicans the midterm elections. Trump acknowledged that the crackdown had hurt him and said enforcement had gone too far in pursuing immigrants who have “good jobs.” It was his first network television interview since the Republican losses on Nov. 3. He has voiced interest in a bipartisan immigration deal before; this time he tied it to the election result. The remarks did not come with any announced change: no new order, rule or funding decision was described. In other answers, asked whether Marco Rubio will remain secretary of state, Trump said, “I think so!”, and on artificial intelligence he said the risks can be managed but “I don’t mind guardrails.” Republicans still run the current House until the new Congress is sworn in, so any immigration bill would have to move through that chamber first. Whether Democratic leaders will negotiate, and on what terms, was not part of the interview.

Pope Leo Tells Argentina’s Milei That People Should Come Before the Economy and Urges Him to Acknowledge the “Limits” of Power

Pope Leo XIV arrived in Argentina and, in a speech met with long applause, told President Javier Milei that economic growth must benefit everyone. Many listeners read the remarks as a rebuke of Milei’s harsh austerity program, the deep spending cuts at the center of his government. The pope also urged the libertarian president, an ally of Donald Trump, to “safeguard democracy” and to acknowledge the “limits” of power. Argentina is the second stop of a 12-day Latin America tour that began in Uruguay, where the pope called for universal health care.

The visit is the most politically delicate leg of the three-nation South American tour. Milei’s government has been at odds with an Argentine Catholic Church that is deeply committed to social justice, and the trip brings a pope back to the homeland of Francis, who never returned during his 12-year pontificate. The pope is due to meet a church that opposes the president’s cuts. How Milei will answer the criticism, and what else the pope says before leaving Argentina, are still to come.

Biden Says His Aggressive Prostate Cancer “Slows Me Down” and Will Limit Promotion of His Memoir, Due Nov. 17

Former President Joe Biden said his prostate cancer, which he described as aggressive, will limit how much he can promote his memoir. He said the illness “slows me down” and that he “won’t be able to do as many conversations” as he had hoped. The book, “Promise Me, America,” covers his presidency and his decision to leave the 2024 race, and goes on sale Nov. 17, two weeks after the midterm elections. Its publisher had said Biden planned to promote it with a tour and media interviews.

Which events will be cut has not been announced. Biden’s office has not made public details of his treatment or prognosis, and he has said earlier that the treatment is going “really well.” The scaled-back plan means the book will reach readers with fewer of the television and stage appearances that usually drive first-week sales.

Peltola Leads Sullivan by About 600 Votes in Alaska’s Unofficial Senate Count as Trump Keeps Claiming a Republican Win

The U.S. Senate race in Alaska remains undecided in the unofficial count. Democrat Mary Peltola leads Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan by about 600 votes, with roughly a quarter of the ballots still to be counted; a separate report put her lead at 637 votes. The two figures come from different tallies at different moments, and neither is a final or certified result. President Trump has continued to say Republicans won the seat, a claim that cannot be confirmed until counting ends and officials certify the outcome.

Republican Gov. Mike Dunleavy called the state’s ranked-choice voting system “very confusing” as the race sat a few hundred votes apart. In ranked-choice voting, voters list candidates in order of preference; if no one clears 50 percent, the last-place candidate is dropped and those ballots go to voters’ next choices, which can move the totals after the first count. Certification comes only after the count ends, so until then the margin can still change in either direction.
