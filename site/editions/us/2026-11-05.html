Democrats Win the House and Senate, Ending Republican Control of Washington

Voter anger over high prices and the war in Iran carried the party into both chambers, and President Trump says he will accept the result.

Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in Tuesday’s midterm elections, ending a Republican trifecta, the stretch in which one party held the House, the Senate and the White House at the same time. Network projections showed the party flipping Senate seats in states including Texas, Kansas and Ohio. In Texas, James Talarico claimed victory in the Senate race, and Gina Hinojosa defeated Gov. Greg Abbott. In Maine, Troy Jackson defeated Sen. Susan Collins, a Republican who had survived hard election years before. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed beat Republican Mike Rogers in the Senate race. Republicans did hold one closely watched seat: Sen. Pete Ricketts fended off independent Dan Osborn in Nebraska. House results ran in the same direction. William Lawrence unseated Rep. Tom Barrett in a Michigan swing district, and Randy Villegas beat an incumbent Republican in a California seat redrawn through redistricting under Proposition 50.

The wave rode voter discontent over high prices and the war in Iran, and it reached governors’ races too. Aaron Ford defeated Nevada Gov. Joe Lombardo and is set to become the state’s first Black governor. Trump said he is “willing to accept those results.” Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called the outcome a “generational remake” of the Senate. Democrats have vowed to rein in the president, and a majority gives them real tools to do it: committee chairs, the power to issue a subpoena that compels documents or testimony, and control of the hearings that make up congressional oversight. In his first public appearance after the losses, Trump showed off the White House ballroom. The new Congress takes office in January, and any bill still needs the president’s signature to become law.

Vance Blames Iran War for GOP Rout as Trump Asks Advisers About Hegseth

Vice President JD Vance blamed the war in Iran for the Republican rout in Tuesday’s midterm elections, breaking with President Trump in a social media post on Wednesday. Vance offered a critique of the administration’s agenda and of its messaging in the campaign. The post came as Republicans absorbed losses that reached deep red states and cost the party both chambers of Congress. Trump has also been asking advisers whether Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth should stay in his job, according to sources. Hegseth had Trump’s public support before the vote, and no change at the Pentagon has been announced. Rep. Thomas Massie, a Republican, has filed eight articles of impeachment against Hegseth. Articles of impeachment are the formal charges that start the House process for removing an official. Democrats take over the House in January, which changes who controls what reaches the floor. Whether Trump acts on the question he is putting to his advisers is not yet known.

Voters Approve Nearly Every Datacenter Moratorium on the Ballot, Dozens in All

Voters approved nearly every datacenter moratorium that appeared on the ballot in Tuesday’s elections, dozens in total. A moratorium is a temporary halt, here on new projects, and a datacenter is a large building packed with computers that store and process data, including the work behind artificial intelligence and cloud services.

Technology companies have been announcing large datacenter projects, and these ballot questions put the buildings to a public vote. The result was lopsided: nearly all of the dozens of measures passed. A moratorium does not ban datacenters permanently. It pauses new approvals for a set period, so builders and officials have to wait, and the specific terms differ from measure to measure.

Brent Crude Falls Below $100 for the First Time in Nearly a Month

Brent crude, the international oil benchmark, fell below $100 a barrel on Wednesday for the first time in nearly a month. A barrel holds 42 U.S. gallons, so a drop of even a few dollars changes the cost of fuel across the economy. The decline came a day after the election handed Democrats control of Congress.

A new Democratic majority could pressure the president to end the war in Iran, but lowering gasoline and diesel prices quickly will be hard. Wall Street closed higher on Wednesday: the S&P 500 rose 1.58% to 7,688.62, and the Nasdaq Composite gained 1.44% to 25,736.51.

Voters Back Abortion Rights and Photo ID Rules Across 146 Statewide Ballot Measures

Voters in dozens of states considered 146 statewide ballot measures in this midterm cycle, on topics spanning LGBTQ rights, abortion and guns. A ballot measure is a question put straight to voters rather than decided by a legislature, so the result takes effect without a vote in the statehouse.

Voters mostly expressed support for abortion rights. They also affirmed photo ID requirements and citizenship requirements at the polls. Photo ID rules require voters to show identification with a picture before voting, and citizenship requirements spell out that only citizens may vote. The results do not line up neatly with the party results: the same electorate that gave Democrats Congress backed voting-rule measures that Republicans have long favored.
