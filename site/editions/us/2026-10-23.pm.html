Two former FBI officials are charged with lying to Congress about the Hunter Biden laptop investigation

The charges were announced Friday against two ex-agents who worked on foreign election interference before the 2020 vote; attorneys for one call them "nonsense charges" filed just before the midterms.

Two former FBI intelligence officials who specialized in foreign election interference were charged Friday with making false statements about their roles in investigating the origins of Hunter Biden's laptop in the run-up to the 2020 presidential election, PBS NewsHour reported. CBS News reported that each was charged with one count of lying to Congress. The Hill identified them as Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides, who worked in the FBI's Foreign Influence Task Force in 2019 and 2020, and said both were taken into custody Friday. NBC News described the pair as officials who have come under withering criticism from allies of President Donald Trump over their intelligence work during the 2020 election.

CNBC reported that attorneys for one of the former officials criticized the Trump administration for filing what they called "nonsense charges" just before the midterm election. NPR noted that Republicans have long accused the FBI of mishandling the laptop and the investigations into the younger Biden. The Hill reported that the charges concern what the two told Congress about their involvement in the agency's investigation. Midterm Election Day is Nov. 3, eleven days away.

Elections · Alaska Senate race✓ 5 outlets · no court ruling reported; how Alaska officials will respond is unconfirmed

Justice Department sues to block certification of Alaska's Senate result if Sen. Dan Sullivan loses

The Justice Department on Thursday filed a pre-emptive lawsuit in federal court to stop Alaska election officials from certifying U.S. Senate results if the Republican incumbent, Sen. Dan Sullivan, loses next month, NBC News and the Guardian reported. The Hill said the suit was filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Alaska. The department argues that the presence of a second candidate with a similar name could confuse voters and taint the result, and The New York Times called the filing highly unusual. CBS News reported that the department is asking the court to block certification if Sullivan loses but would have won had the votes cast for the other Sullivan been counted for him. President Trump said Friday that he directed the Justice Department to file the suit, The Hill reported, quoting him as saying, "I'm the one that said, 'do it.'" PBS NewsHour reported that the move has sparked concerns about a lengthy battle between the federal government and states over election certification. CNBC noted that Alaska is one of several contests that could determine whether Republicans keep their slim Senate majority. Axios reported that the race has been marked by controversy.

Oil climbs to its highest level in more than five months as fears of renewed U.S. strikes on Iran grow

Oil prices hit their highest level in more than five months on Friday as fears of renewed escalation in the Iran war gripped energy markets, the Guardian, The Hill and The New York Times reported. The Guardian put Brent crude at $114.72 a barrel after Tehran threatened retaliatory attacks on Gulf oil and tech facilities. The Hill said Brent futures traded at about $116 on Friday morning and U.S. benchmark WTI at about $102, and noted that the war is in its eighth month. The Times reported global oil at $117 a barrel, which it called the highest since April, and said investors are increasingly worried that the war could escalate again and put energy supplies at greater risk.

CNBC reported that U.S. crude held above $100 and Brent reached a five-month high as Iran said it is prepared for warfare, and that President Trump accused Iran on Thursday of trying to manipulate the oil market. Bloomberg reported that Iran-backed Houthi militants claimed recent attacks on the Saudi Red Sea port of Yanbu, as well as deadly strikes on an oil field and airport in the kingdom, a flare-up it said threatens to widen hostilities around the Strait of Hormuz. Bloomberg also reported that the CFTC's Selig, the top U.S. derivatives regulator, said the agency's enforcement division has been looking at oil trading during the Iran war.

Technology · Ukraine war✓ 6 outlets · who ordered the cap is disputed; current speeds are Kyiv's account

Trump says Musk restored Ukraine's Starshield access, but Kyiv says the speed caps remain

Ukrainian forces continue to report speed restrictions on Starshield, the military version of SpaceX's Starlink, which the Guardian said was still operating at a cap of 161 km/h (about 100 mph) despite President Trump's claim that Elon Musk had resumed full service; the paper described a war of words between the Trump administration, officials in Kyiv and Musk over who is to blame. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy told ABC News on Friday that the network is still not fully working, despite assurances from Trump. In a Telegram post Friday, Zelenskyy wrote that the speed limits for Starlink and Starshield are still in effect, that speed has not been restored, and that deliveries of interceptor missiles to defend against Russian ballistic missiles are still delayed. CNBC reported that SpaceX is still restricting Starlink in Ukraine and that the toll is rising.

CNBC, citing The Wall Street Journal, reported Thursday that Trump called Musk and asked him to lift the restrictions. The Hill reported Friday that Trump told reporters on the White House South Lawn, "I have not spoken to him," and warned he might have to if access is not restored. PBS NewsHour reported that Trump administration officials and their allies have offered mixed messages about what led to the sudden restrictions. CNBC also reported that Silicon Valley Democrats sent a letter demanding an investigation into the Starlink restrictions, which it said signals Democrats would make the issue a priority if they win the House.

Trade · U.S.-Canada tariffs✓ 6 outlets · the full list of exempted goods beyond Collins' nine categories is unconfirmed

Trump administration exempts some Canadian goods from tariffs, Sen. Susan Collins announces

Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine) announced Friday that nine categories of Canadian products "crucial for Maine's agricultural industries" will be exempted from U.S. tariffs, The Hill reported; the list includes paperboard cartons, plastic boxes, refrigeration equipment and agricultural cleaning and sorting products. CNBC reported that the administration will issue exemptions on several categories of Canadian imports, and NBC News reported that the administration canceled some tariffs on Canada. The New York Times described the move as coming ahead of a tight race in which Collins faces voters worried about rising prices, and the Guardian reported that she is in a tough re-election battle in a state where Trump's trade war with Canada has stirred outrage.

The White House said in a fact sheet that Trump signed a proclamation Friday under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 to further refine the scope of Canadian products subject to the additional duties. The proclamation text refers back to Proclamation 11048 of July 20, 2026, which imposed duties to offset what the administration calls Canadian discrimination against U.S. commerce with respect to motor vehicles. The White House fact sheet describes the step as part of continuing to address Canada's discriminatory treatment of American exports.
