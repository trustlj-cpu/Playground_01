Binance estimates $6.3 billion in assets affected by hack; about $3 billion frozen, North Korea link unconfirmed

Binance's own estimate of affected assets is not a confirmed loss, and the North Korea link rests on analysis by private firms, not any government.

Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, said Thursday that about $6.3 billion in assets were affected by a security breach, a figure the company itself estimated and has not confirmed as a final loss. The exchange suspended deposits and withdrawals while it investigated, and other crypto firms froze roughly $3 billion of the affected assets, about half the total, to keep them from being moved out. Binance says customer funds are safe and that it will cover what was taken; it has set aside more than $6.3 billion for that purpose and has said it aims to reopen within a couple of days. If the $6.3 billion becomes the final count, it would be the largest theft in cryptocurrency history, several times the roughly $1.5 billion taken from the exchange Bybit in February 2025. The company's early findings point to an attacker who got in through a compromised laptop belonging to a DevOps engineer, an employee who manages the systems that run the exchange.

Two blockchain analytics firms, TRM Labs and Chainalysis, have tied the attack to TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking unit that has stolen crypto before. That link comes only from those private firms. No government has confirmed it, and President Trump, asked Friday whether North Korea was responsible, said, "We don't know." He said his administration is looking into the hack. Markets reacted quickly: Bitcoin was down about 4% by Friday afternoon, and crypto-related stocks tumbled. Investigators are now racing to trace the rest of the stolen coins before they are laundered, meaning swapped and shuffled through many wallets until they can be turned into cash. Still unknown are the final loss once frozen assets are counted, how much can be recovered, who carried out the attack, and when withdrawals will resume.

Judge orders Justice Department to stop threatening election officials with prosecution days before the midterms

A federal judge in California ordered the Justice Department late Friday to stop threatening election officials with criminal prosecution over how they handle ballots, four days before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. U.S. District Judge Trina Thompson issued a temporary restraining order, a short-term court order that freezes the situation until a fuller hearing. It bars the department from threatening or coercing election workers to stop processing ballots, and from demanding that local officials separate out or stop counting ballots the government says belong to ineligible voters. The department has been pressing California in particular over claims that more than 20,000 noncitizens are on its voter rolls; a federal appeals court rejected its latest bid Friday to force the state to set those ballots aside, and grand jury subpoenas have gone to local election workers there. Separately, the department has warned officials in several states they could face jail time if they fail to preserve election records. Not yet clear is how far the order reaches outside California, whether the department will appeal, and what it means for the subpoenas already issued.

Nasdaq falls 2.86% and Intel plunges 29% as the tech selloff over AI spending deepens

Wall Street's slide in technology stocks deepened Friday as worries about how much the biggest companies are spending on artificial intelligence spread well beyond the firms that reported Thursday. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.86% to 25,437.00, the S&P 500 lost 1.82% to 7,580.07, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 1.09% to 51,770.31. Intel led the damage, plunging 29% in its worst day in decades after it forecast weaker sales, blaming soft demand for personal-computer chips and supply problems with data-center parts. The fall cut about $12 billion from the value of the U.S. government's stake in the company. The Nasdaq 100 fell more than 3%, putting it in its third correction of 2026, meaning a drop of at least 10% from a recent high. The cost of insuring big tech companies' debt against default hit record highs, a sign lenders are also growing wary of the borrowing that funds AI buildouts, and chipmaker AMD slid in sympathy. Apple and AMD report earnings in the week ahead.

U.S. approves $2.1 billion sale of 500 Patriot missiles to Ukraine, but deliveries could take years

The State Department said Friday it has approved a possible $2.1 billion sale of 500 Patriot interceptor missiles to Ukraine. The PAC-3 interceptors are the air-defense weapon Ukraine relies on to bring down Russia's ballistic missiles, which fly too fast and high for most other systems. At $2.1 billion for 500 missiles, each interceptor works out to about $4.2 million. Approval is a first step: the sale is described as potential and still has to be turned into contracts and production. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called the decision strong but warned that delivery of the missiles could take years and that stockpiles are limited. Delivery dates and the order in which units will arrive have not been announced.

Texas grand jury indicts ICE officer Mario Alberto Morales for striking a man with his vehicle in Houston

A Harris County, Texas, grand jury on Friday indicted Mario Alberto Morales, a 33-year-old officer with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, on a charge of second-degree aggravated assault with a deadly weapon, a felony. Prosecutors say that on Oct. 10 Morales was driving an unmarked black SUV when it struck Miguel Parra Hernandez, 48, who was running away after a botched traffic stop. Parra Hernandez was seriously injured. The Harris County District Attorney's office announced the charge.

An indictment is a formal accusation, not a finding of guilt. Cases like this can turn on a question of authority: federal officers charged by a state often ask to move the case to a federal court and argue that they were doing their jobs and are immune from state prosecution. Whether Morales has entered a plea, has a lawyer or remains on duty is not yet known.
