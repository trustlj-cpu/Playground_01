Trump Names Stephen Miller White House Chief of Staff, Replacing Susie Wiles Three Days After the Midterms

Miller, the architect of Trump's mass-deportation policy, takes over Monday. Susie Wiles, the first woman to hold the job, says Friday was her last day.

President Trump announced on Friday, Nov. 6, that Stephen Miller, his deputy chief of staff and the architect of his mass-deportation policy, will become White House chief of staff on Monday, Nov. 9, replacing Susie Wiles. The chief of staff is the official who runs the White House day to day, deciding who reaches the president and which decisions land on his desk. Trump called Miller “one of my most trusted advisors,” and the White House release names him the 33rd person to hold the job. Trump wrote that Wiles, who has led the White House staff since January 2025 and is the first woman to hold the post, will move to the Trump Political Operation. Wiles posted on X, “Today is my last day as White House Chief of Staff.” A White House official has said she submitted her resignation on Thursday night. The change comes three days after Tuesday’s midterm elections, in which Republicans lost ground. Why Wiles resigned has not been confirmed by anyone involved.

Miller has driven the administration’s most contested immigration policies since the first term, and his promotion drew a sharp reaction within hours. House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries called him “a dangerous xenophobe and reckless extremist who has American blood on his hands.” Oversight Committee Democrats said they will keep investigating Miller and will fight his policies in Congress and the courts. Leaders of industries that rely on immigrant labor have voiced anger and fear about what the promotion means for their workers. In plain terms, the aide who designed the deportation campaign will now control the flow of decisions to the president. Several points are unsettled: who will replace Miller as deputy chief of staff, whether he keeps his other titles, including homeland security adviser, who will run the Trump political operation day to day, and whether more changes to the White House staff or the Cabinet are coming.

Secret Service Arrests an Armed Man Near the White House; DHS Says He Told Officers Voices Urged Him to Kill Trump

Secret Service officers arrested a 26-year-old Venezuelan man, Ronny Guanipa-Chrinos, on Thursday night after finding him in a parked pickup truck near the White House with firearms inside. The Department of Homeland Security, or DHS, says officers recovered a rifle, two handguns and dozens of rounds of ammunition, and that the man told them he heard voices in his head telling him to come to Washington and kill President Trump. A report on the court affidavit, the sworn statement officers file to support charges, says it does not repeat those remarks. He is charged with unauthorized use of a vehicle, receiving stolen property, and carrying a pistol and a rifle without a license, among other firearms counts. The affidavit says he told investigators the truck belonged to his boss, who lent it to him, so whether the vehicle was stolen is disputed. DHS says he entered the country illegally and that Immigration and Customs Enforcement, known as ICE, has lodged a detainer, a request that he be held for immigration officials if he is released. A federal law enforcement source says he is due in federal court on Monday. These are charges, not findings, and neither his plea nor his lawyer’s account has been reported.

Hegseth Vows to Fight an Impeachment Push as Republican Leaders Voice Doubts After the Midterms

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is fighting a growing impeachment effort after the midterm elections, and the White House says the president “has full confidence” in him. Rep. Thomas Massie, a Kentucky Republican, plans to reintroduce articles of impeachment, the formal charges that start the process, when Congress returns, and has said he will not do so if Hegseth resigns. Impeachment is a House vote to bring charges; removal would then take a Senate trial, so a House vote alone does not end a tenure. Democrats, including House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, have said they want Hegseth out. Hegseth answered in a video on X, calling the effort “sham articles of impeachment” and saying “it’s all about the politics of personal destruction.”

Republican doubts have surfaced too. House Oversight Committee Chairman James Comer said “there’s a lot of questions” about Hegseth, that “no one’s happy about Iran,” and that the president must decide his future. Watchdog groups have said Hegseth probably broke federal law by using government resources for a self-promotional video; no agency finding has been reported. Not yet known: whether Trump will keep him, whether enough Republicans would join an impeachment vote, and when the House will take up the charges.

Cornell Faculty Senate Votes No Confidence in University Leaders Over a Fraternity Gang-Rape Case; Trustees Stand by the President

Cornell University’s Faculty Senate approved a resolution on Friday, Nov. 6, declaring no confidence in the university’s administration and President Michael Kotlikoff over how it handled an allegation by a former student that she was gang-raped at a fraternity house in 2024. The vote is symbolic: it is a no-confidence vote, a formal statement of lost trust, and only the board of trustees can remove a president. The board answered the same day. Board chair Anne Smalling said the trustees “fully support” Kotlikoff and his administration.

The case has drawn national attention. Gov. Kathy Hochul had the state attorney general, Letitia James, take over the criminal investigation, and Kotlikoff has said he welcomes an independent review of the university’s handling of the case, which the board commissioned from former acting Attorney General Sally Yates. Not yet known: whether faculty will press further, whether Kotlikoff stays, and when the criminal investigation will reach a decision.

Justice Department Moves to Drop Its Suit Over 20,000 California Ballots After the Supreme Court Refused to Step In

The Justice Department asked a federal court in Los Angeles on Friday, Nov. 6, to end its lawsuit seeking to force California to set aside about 20,000 mail ballots the department alleged came from people who were not citizens. The three-page filing is a voluntary dismissal, a plaintiff’s request to drop its own case. The Supreme Court had already rejected the department’s emergency appeal in a one-line unsigned order, and District Judge David Carter and a three-judge appeals panel had also turned down the request. In its filing, the department said California would have mixed the challenged ballots with other ballots before a hearing set for Nov. 13, when a special master, a court-appointed reviewer, was to evaluate the accuracy of the list.

A department spokesperson called the case moot, meaning no longer live because the ballots can no longer be separated, and said the administration “is not backing down” from efforts to target alleged noncitizen voters. California is still counting: about 65% of its votes had been tallied as of Thursday. Not yet known: whether the court grants the dismissal and whether any ballot in the group is shown to have been cast by a noncitizen; no such finding has been reported.
