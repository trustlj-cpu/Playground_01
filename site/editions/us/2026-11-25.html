Kilmar Abrego García Is Leaving the U.S. for Spain, Ending a 20-Month Legal Fight, Reports Say

A federal judge had ordered him to leave by Tuesday, a U.S. official told one report, and sources say he was due to depart that day.

Kilmar Abrego García, the Salvadoran migrant whose March 2025 removal to El Salvador became a national test of presidential power over immigration, is leaving the United States for Spain, according to reports published Tuesday. Several reports describe the departure as voluntary, and one calls it self-deporting, meaning he is going on his own rather than being put on a plane by immigration agents. Sources told one report he was due to leave Tuesday, and a U.S. official told another that a federal judge had ordered him to be gone by that day. Sen. Chris Van Hollen, the Maryland Democrat who traveled to El Salvador last year to press for his return, said Abrego García is leaving as a "free man." The reports describe the move as the end of a legal fight that has run for 20 months, counting from the removal in March 2025.

The background is well documented. Government agents sent Abrego García to a prison in El Salvador in March 2025 even though an immigration judge had ruled in 2019 that he could not be removed to that country, a protection known as withholding of removal. Government lawyers called the removal an administrative error, and after court fights he was returned to the United States on June 6, 2025, to face federal charges of transporting people in the country illegally. In December 2025 a federal judge ordered him freed from immigration detention. A voluntary departure lets a person leave on their own rather than under a formal removal order, but the reports reviewed do not say whether his departure is formally that, why Spain was chosen, whether he has already left, or what happens next in the Tennessee criminal case.

Fort Hood Shooter Nidal Hasan Asks a Federal Judge to Halt His Firing-Squad Execution, Reports Say

Nidal Hasan, the former Army officer sentenced to death for the 2009 Fort Hood shooting that killed 13 people, has asked a federal judge to halt his scheduled execution by firing squad, according to reports published Tuesday. One report says he wants the execution delayed so he can keep fighting his sentence, a request that, if granted, is known as a stay of execution. The Army announced in October that the execution would take place Dec. 3 at Fort Hood, Texas. The reports reviewed do not say which court received the request, what legal grounds Hasan cites, or when a judge might rule. Separately, the Army has told three people wounded in the 2009 attack that they cannot attend the execution because they were not direct victims of Hasan's crimes, one report says. Hasan was convicted at a court-martial, the military's own trial system, and the military appeals courts upheld the death sentence. The Supreme Court declined to review his conviction in March 2025.

A U.S. Jury Finds Huawei Guilty of Conspiracy to Steal Trade Secrets but Acquits It of Racketeering, Reports Say

A U.S. jury has found the Chinese technology company Huawei guilty of conspiracy to steal trade secrets, according to reports published Tuesday. One report says the jury acquitted the company of racketeering, a charge that accuses a business of operating as a criminal enterprise. Prosecutors had accused Huawei of stealing technology from other companies and of sanctions evasion, and one report says the trial exposed corporate espionage and sanctions evasion. An earlier report said the jury began deliberating after a trial of about two months.

The reports reviewed do not give the jury's decision on every count, such as bank fraud or obstruction of justice, which were among the charges in the case. They also do not say what penalties Huawei faces, when sentencing will take place, or whether the company will appeal. Huawei's own response was not in the reports reviewed.

Blanche and the FBI's Patel Sidestep Questions on Whether Nvidia Is Under Investigation, and One Report Says Investigators Are Examining Its Customers

Attorney General Todd Blanche and Patel of the FBI declined on Tuesday to say directly whether Nvidia or its chief executive, Jensen Huang, is being investigated, according to reports, a day after the chipmaker's board reinstated Huang. Blanche played down the questions. One headline report quotes him saying that if Nvidia were the target of a criminal investigation, "that wouldn't be the message."

A separate report says Justice Department investigators have gathered evidence that Nvidia's high-powered computer chips were handled illegally and are examining the company's customers, a line of inquiry tied to export controls, the rules that limit which advanced chips can be sold abroad. The reports reviewed do not say what the evidence shows, who the customers are, or whether anyone has been charged.

Wisconsin Health Officials Report a Third Measles Death as Illnesses Spread to the General Public, One Report Says

Wisconsin health officials reported Tuesday that a third person has died of measles in the state, and one report says illnesses have spread to the general public. The report summary reviewed does not give the person's age, vaccination status or the current case count, and no other report confirming the death was found.

Measles is among the most contagious viruses known. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says two doses of the MMR vaccine are about 97 percent effective, and health officials aim for roughly 95 percent of children to be vaccinated, the level at which herd immunity, the shielding effect that protects people who cannot be vaccinated, tends to hold.
