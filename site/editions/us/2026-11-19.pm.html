China and Russia Say the Three Leaders Agreed on United Nations Reforms, Fuel Supplies and Nuclear Dialogue; the White House Has Said Little

Moscow and Beijing describe a four-point peace declaration from the Shenzhen talks; Washington has not yet given its own account.

Hours after the first three-way meeting of the leaders of the United States, China and Russia, Beijing and Moscow have described what came out of it. Russia's Foreign Ministry said the three heads of state adopted a joint declaration on peace at the end of the Shenzhen talks, and it posted a text it described as the full declaration. Chinese and Russian officials said the three sides agreed on reforms to the United Nations, on boosting fuel supplies and on a dialogue about nuclear weapons, and one report says the four-point text also touches the International Criminal Court. Those descriptions come from the Chinese and Russian side. The United States has not publicly confirmed the terms, and the declaration's wording has not been checked against an American account.

The White House was largely silent in the hours after the trilateral meeting. President Trump is set to give a news conference on the trip, according to one report, so the American version of what was agreed is still to come. A report says a reporter was barred from the talks after asking about Nvidia. Before the meeting, one report said Moscow and Beijing had shown little interest in the three-way nuclear deal Trump wanted, which makes the wording of any nuclear dialogue the main thing to read closely. The open questions are what the declaration commits each government to, whether Washington describes it the same way, and what a dialogue on nuclear weapons means in practice.

Senate Rejects Iran War Powers Resolution 48-51 in the First Vote on the Conflict Since the Midterm Elections

The Senate on Thursday rejected a war powers resolution, 48-51, that would have forced an end to U.S. fighting in Iran. A war powers resolution is a measure under which Congress can require a president to stop military action unless lawmakers approve it. It was the first Senate vote on the conflict since the midterm elections, and the second or later attempt, since earlier tries also fell short. Most Republicans banded together against it, while three Republicans reportedly joined nearly the entire Democratic caucus in voting yes. The tally leaves the administration free to continue the military action without a new congressional authorization. Democrats, who will control both chambers in January, have signaled that the fight over the war is not finished, and one report says a bigger battle looms. Which Republicans voted yes, and whether a later vote can draw the handful of additional votes the measure needed, are not yet clear. The next things to watch are any new vote scheduled before the new Congress is seated and any White House statement on the result.

Anthropic Shares Open at $111.50 in Their Nasdaq Debut, Above the $83 Offering Price, While Major Stock Indexes Fall

Anthropic began trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday at $111.50 a share, according to reports, against the $83 at which it priced its initial public offering the night before. Reports differ on the size of the raise: two put it at about $81 billion, while one broadcast said $107 billion; either would be the largest such sale on record. One report says the shares rose over the first day of trading; another says they climbed above the offering price and then fell back from their high. The closing price for the day has not been confirmed. An offering price is what investors paid in the sale itself, while the trading price is set minute by minute by buyers and sellers afterward.

Reports differ on what the company is worth. Coverage at the pricing put its valuation, the total value the market places on all its shares, near $1.5 trillion, while two reports on the debut cite $2 trillion. The figure moves with every trade, so no single number is settled. The S&P 500, an index of 500 large U.S. companies, closed at 7,175.83, down 2.02% for the day, and the Nasdaq Composite closed down 2.25%. No single cause for the decline has been established. What remains to be seen is how the stock trades in the days ahead and what company filings show for the final size of the raise.

Jensen Huang Has Agreed to Appear Before the Senate Banking Committee, Its Chairman Says, as a Lawmaker Cites 879,000 Chip Approvals for China

Jensen Huang has agreed to appear before the Senate Banking Committee, committee Chairman Tim Scott said Wednesday. No date for the appearance has been reported. Huang resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, and the company is now led by interim chief executive Colette Kress, who told employees at her first all-hands meeting since his exit that it was time to move forward. One report describes the scandal as involving an affair with an alleged Chinese spy; that account has not been independently confirmed. Elon Musk said on Wednesday that Huang is irreplaceable.

Separately, Rep. Mast said the Commerce Department approved sales of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies. Commerce issues the export licenses, the government permissions required to ship sensitive technology abroad, and the figure comes from Mast; no Commerce tally was cited. Members of Congress are pressing for legislation to tighten export controls, the rules that limit which technology can be sold to which countries. One analysis says the scandal could prove a tipping point toward tougher enforcement of those rules, and another report examines how Nvidia chips ended up within reach of China's military. The open questions are when Huang testifies, what lawmakers ask him, and whether any bill advances.

Danish Lawmakers Approve a Security Deal With Greenland and the United States, Hoping to End Months of Tension

Danish lawmakers on Thursday approved a security deal involving Denmark, Greenland and the United States, a step known as ratification: the formal approval by a legislature that makes an agreement binding. The agreement was struck with the Trump administration, and Danish officials said they hope it will finally end the tensions that have strained relations over the island for months. One report ties the deal to U.S. military bases.

Denmark, a NATO ally of the United States, had been at odds with Washington over Greenland's future, and the vote is meant to settle that dispute. The reports do not spell out every term of the agreement, and they do not include a full response from Greenland's own government. What remains to be seen is whether Washington issues its own statement on the vote, how the bases or other security arrangements will work in practice, and whether the ratification brings the tensions to a close.
