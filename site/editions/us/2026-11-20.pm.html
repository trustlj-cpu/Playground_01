Mayor Mamdani Proposes Closing Rikers Island by the End of 2032, Five Years Past the Legal Deadline, and the City Council Must Approve It

The city's first comprehensive plan would move the legal deadline from August 2027 to the end of 2032 and cut the jail population to a little over 4,000; it cannot take effect unless the City Council changes the law.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani on Friday unveiled a plan to close the Rikers Island jail complex by the end of 2032, five years later than the legal deadline of Aug. 31, 2027. His administration has said the city will not be ready by 2027, and Mamdani cannot move the deadline on his own: the City Council would have to change the law, so nothing about the timeline has changed yet. Under the plan, new borough-based jails, smaller facilities meant to replace the island complex, would open in Brooklyn in 2029, in the Bronx and Queens in 2031 and in Manhattan in 2032, according to City Hall.

The plan also depends on shrinking the number of people in custody. It sets a target of a little over 4,000 people, down from about 6,700 now, and includes $290 million through fiscal year 2030 for efforts to reduce the jail population. In plain terms, that is roughly 2,700 fewer people, or about 4 in every 10 now held. Mamdani said the plan was developed in coordination with the federal monitor, the outside official appointed to oversee conditions at the jails. The Legal Aid Society welcomed the renewed commitment but said New Yorkers deserve a credible plan with concrete benchmarks. Still unknown are whether the Council will approve the extension, how the plan will be paid for, and how the city will reach the population target while the jails remain open.

A Second Federal Judge Says She Will Halt Trump's 250-Foot Arch Near Arlington Cemetery, Calling It a 'Rushed Job' Without Congressional Approval

U.S. District Judge Leonie Brinkema said Friday from the bench in Alexandria, Virginia, that she will issue a preliminary injunction halting construction of the 250-foot triumphal arch President Trump wants built at Memorial Circle, at the Virginia end of the Arlington Memorial Bridge. A preliminary injunction is a court order that pauses a project while the lawsuit is decided; hers would last while Arlington County's suit goes forward. She called the project a "rushed job" and said it "lacks congressional approval," and she said the government's argument that a 1925 law had already authorized it made no sense. She also appeared to find the government's review of the arch's environmental and economic effects inadequate, in particular the effect of construction cranes on air traffic, because the site is about 3,000 feet from Reagan National Airport and inside its main approach and departure corridor. The ruling follows a separate case in Washington, where reports say Judge Tanya Chutkan extended a temporary restraining order, a short-term freeze, through Dec. 3 after the National Capital Planning Commission approved the arch by an 8-4 vote on Nov. 5. White House spokesman Davis Ingle said, "The judiciary should not be abused to politicize a structure designed to unite our nation." Kate Talmor of Democracy Forward, a lawyer for Arlington County, welcomed the ruling. Still unknown are the exact terms of the written order, whether the administration will appeal, and what the Washington judge will decide when her order runs out.

Anthropic Shares Fall to Within 5 Cents of the $83 IPO Price on Their Second Day, Then Recover, and Reports Differ on the Close

Anthropic's stock, a day after a debut that a report says closed about 17% above the $83 offering price, fell as low as $83.05 on Friday, just 5 cents above where it was sold to investors, before bouncing about 6% into the close, reports say. Reports differ on where it finished, giving $88.30 in one case and $88.91 in another, so neither figure is treated here as settled. One report puts Thursday's close at $97.17, which would make the first of those figures a drop of roughly 9% on the day. The offering price is what the first buyers paid in the IPO, the company's first sale of shares to the public, so a stock trading near it means most of the first-day gain has disappeared.

The slide came as technology stocks kept falling. Reports said the Nasdaq Composite briefly turned negative for 2026 during Friday's session and that the S&P 500 was headed for correction territory, a drop of 10% or more from a recent high. One report said both indexes were on course for their worst weekly performances in more than a year. Final index closes for Friday were not available at press time, so the figures here are limited to what reports described during the session. Reports do not agree on why stocks are falling, and no single cause is confirmed. Still unknown are Friday's final closing levels and whether Anthropic's shares hold above the offering price when trading resumes Monday.

Texas Execution of Reinaldo Dennes Took More Than an Hour as Staff Struggled to Find a Vein, Witnesses Say, and the State Denies It Was Botched

Texas executed Reinaldo Dennes, 70, on Thursday evening in a lethal injection that took more than an hour, witnesses said, because staff struggled to find a vein that could hold the IV line, the tube that delivers the drugs. The needles ended up in his left foot and the left side of his neck. Amanda Hernandez, a spokesperson for the Texas Department of Criminal Justice, acknowledged several unsuccessful attempts before the lines were placed. A reporter who witnessed the execution said Dennes moaned for several minutes before he began to snore, and said that, having seen nearly 500 lethal injections, this one was "dramatically different."

Dennes's spiritual adviser, the Rev. Jeff Hood, who was in the chamber, called it a botched execution. Hernandez said, "as far as this agency is concerned, it is 100% not a botched execution," and said the pain Dennes described referred to places where no IV had been placed, an account that is disputed. Dennes was convicted of the January 1996 killing of Janos Szucs, a diamond dealer, in his Houston office, and the prison system's record says he also shot an unarmed security guard, who survived. One report says the case has prompted calls for a moratorium, a pause on carrying out lethal injections. Still unknown are which drugs were used, whether anyone will seek an inquiry, and whether the state will review its procedures.

Nikhil Gupta Is Sentenced to 235 Months, About 19 Years and 7 Months, in the Failed Plot to Kill Sikh Separatist Gurpatwant Singh Pannun in New York

A federal judge in Manhattan on Friday sentenced Nikhil Gupta, 55, an Indian national, to 235 months in prison, about 19 years and 7 months, for his role in a failed plot to kill Gurpatwant Singh Pannun, a Sikh separatist leader and U.S.-Canadian citizen, in New York. Judge Victor Marrero chose the low end of the sentencing guidelines range of 235 to 293 months, the range the court's formula recommends, and rejected defense arguments over how the range was calculated. Prosecutors had asked for 293 months, about 24 years, and the defense had asked for 144 months, or 12 years.

Gupta pleaded guilty in February to three counts: murder-for-hire, conspiracy to commit murder-for-hire and conspiracy to commit money laundering. Murder-for-hire means paying someone to carry out a killing. A report says he admitted conspiring with an Indian government official. He apologized in court and faces deportation to India after he finishes his sentence. Pannun attended the hearing and said the Indian government wanted him dead because he leads a campaign openly. India's government has denied involvement, saying such a plot is against its policy. Some reports round the term to 19 years or 20 years; 235 months is the exact figure reported. Still unknown are whether either side will appeal and whether any Indian official will face charges.
