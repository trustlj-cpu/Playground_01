Prop. 40, California’s Billionaire Wealth Tax, Trails in the Unofficial Count; the Result Is Not Yet Certified

The one-time 5% tax on billionaires’ wealth is trailing in the unofficial count after the most expensive ballot campaign in state history, with Democrats split over it.

In California’s unofficial count, Proposition 40 is trailing. The ballot measure would have charged residents worth more than $1 billion a one-time 5% tax on most of their wealth, including their ownership stakes in companies. News organizations projected its defeat Monday evening, six days after Election Day, while counties were still counting ballots; the result is not certified. A wealth tax is charged on what a person owns, such as shares, private companies and property, rather than on income earned in a year. The money was meant to go mostly to health care. In dollars, 5% of a $10 billion fortune is $500 million, though the measure reached most, not all, of a person’s wealth, so the actual bill would have been somewhat smaller. The campaign was the most expensive ballot campaign in state history, with Silicon Valley donors supplying much of the money. Opponents were far better funded than supporters, and reported spending totals vary by tally. The fight also split Democrats in California and across the country.

Opponents, who included politicians, a teachers union and other powerful groups, argued that squeezing California’s ultra-rich would hurt the state’s economy. The latest tallies put the no vote at about 53%, though the totals differ slightly from one count to the next, and early returns on Nov. 4 had it nearer 56% before the gap narrowed as more ballots were counted. Those figures are unofficial: a result becomes final only when county elections officials finish counting and certify it, and that has not happened. Two companion measures, Propositions 41 and 42, were written to undercut the tax, and final results for them were not available. Until certification, the margin can still move a little, but the projection of a loss is not in doubt in the tallies now public.

Trump Says Nvidia’s Jensen Huang Told Him He Had “No Idea” the Woman Was a Spy; China Calls the Allegation Unsubstantiated

President Trump said Monday that Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang told him he had “no idea” that Alice Huang, the executive he has acknowledged an affair with, was a spy. Trump said he believes Huang’s account and that he does not recall receiving a briefing that warned him about the relationship. He also said the FBI is looking into the matter, without saying what it is examining or when that began. Alice Huang, who is not related to Jensen Huang, is an executive at Megaspeed, a cloud-computing company. The claim that she worked for Chinese intelligence comes from unnamed U.S. officials, and no public finding or criminal charge has been reported. Earlier reporting, also citing unnamed officials, said Trump was briefed on the relationship about a year ago; no named official has confirmed that. Nvidia’s board opened an independent investigation Sunday, the same day Huang sent employees a letter admitting he had been unfaithful to his wife. The board said it has no reason to believe the company’s secrets or national security were compromised, and Huang said he supports the investigation and will cooperate fully. China’s government said the spying allegation is unsubstantiated. In Washington, Sen. Chris Coons, a Delaware Democrat, said he is pushing to investigate whether national security was compromised, and three House committee chairmen have asked Huang for a closed-door briefing on Nov. 18, the day after Nvidia reports third-quarter results. Nvidia shares fell sharply Monday, roughly 4% to 5% in the reports, which differ by the hour. Whether Huang will attend the briefing has not been disclosed.

Exxon Signs a Non-Binding Preliminary Agreement With Venezuela’s State Oil Company, a First Step Back After Nearly Two Decades

Exxon Mobil signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding in Venezuela on Monday (reports differ on whether the state oil company, PDVSA, or the government signed it) to assess upstream oil opportunities in the country. Upstream means exploration and production, finding and pumping crude, rather than refining or selling fuel. The memorandum is a first step: a joint venture or production-sharing contract, in which the company and the state divide the oil produced, would still have to be signed before Exxon could drill or pump there. Exxon has not said which projects or fields it is evaluating, and the text of the memorandum has not been published. The company said any future investment would require stable policies, clear regulations, strong investment protections and competitive returns. Venezuela’s interim president, Delcy Rodríguez, welcomed the signing and said she hoped it would lead to concrete agreements. Exxon left after the government of Hugo Chávez nationalized its oil projects in 2007, and a dispute over compensation between the company and Caracas remains unresolved.

Senate Votes 73-25 to Take Up a Bipartisan Bill to Speed Permits for Energy Projects and Data Centers

The Senate voted 73-25 on Monday to advance a bipartisan bill that would shorten the time it takes to win federal approval for energy projects, data centers and other infrastructure. One account described the vote as a procedural vote to formally take up the bill. It was the chamber’s first major action since the midterm elections. By one count, 51 Republicans, 21 Democrats and one independent voted yes. The bill targets permitting, the sequence of environmental and other federal reviews a project must clear before construction, and aims to speed it for projects from gas pipelines to clean energy and to ease the siting of interstate transmission lines, the high-voltage lines that carry power between regions. President Trump urged senators on Truth Social to vote for it, calling it a “BIG WIN for U.S. ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE.”

The bill divided Democrats. Key environmental groups oppose it, fearing it could speed data centers and other projects they object to, and several Democratic senators have said they will seek major changes through amendments. Senate Majority Leader John Thune urged Republicans to back it as a bipartisan win. The bill still needs amendment votes and a final Senate vote, expected in the coming days, and the House, which has not passed a version, would then have to act.

France Blames Iran’s Revolutionary Guard for the October Hacking of About 700 Supermarket Refrigeration Systems

France’s government said Monday that Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps hacked the refrigeration systems of about 700 retail sites across the country last month. Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot described the incident as an attack of “unprecedented scope and aggressiveness.” The targets were supermarket refrigerators connected to the internet, devices that most shoppers never think of as a way into a country’s systems.

France has vowed retaliation and says it will press for sanctions at the European level. Attribution of a cyberattack to a government is a political judgment that France has now made publicly; no court has ruled on it, and no Iranian response has been confirmed. The accusation comes while the United States and Iran remain locked in a war, which is the setting in which other governments are watching for attacks on civilian systems.
