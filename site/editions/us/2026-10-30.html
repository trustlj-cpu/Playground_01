Patrick Stewart, the stage actor who became Captain Picard and Professor X, dies at 86

The British actor died Wednesday at his home in Los Angeles after a battle with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia, his representatives said.

Sir Patrick Stewart, the classically trained British stage actor who became a global figure as Capt. Jean-Luc Picard in "Star Trek: The Next Generation" and as Professor Charles Xavier in the "X-Men" films, died Wednesday, Oct. 28, at his home in Los Angeles. He was 86. A spokesperson said he died after "a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer's Disease and Lewy Body Dementia," and the news reached the public Thursday through his official social media pages. Stewart built his reputation in the theater, working his way through the Shakespearean canon long before "Star Trek" put him on television in 1987. As Picard, a starship captain who settled crises by talking first, he played a leader who was patient, wry and exact, and the role made him a fixture of science-fiction fandom for decades. Raised by a violent father, he later became best known for playing kind, fatherly figures who held groups of outsiders together, from the bridge of the Enterprise to Xavier's school for young mutants.

Lewy body dementia is a brain disease in which clumps of an abnormal protein build up inside nerve cells. It affects thinking and memory as well as movement and alertness, and it often overlaps with Alzheimer's disease, which gradually destroys memory and reasoning. Tributes began within hours of the announcement. His longtime friend Sir Ian McKellen said Stewart "wore his heart on his sleeve, which made him a man to rely on, to admire and in my case, love." Hugh Jackman, Marvel Studios president Kevin Feige and his "Star Trek" co-star Jonathan Frakes were among the colleagues who paid public tribute. Stewart's later years were not spent in retirement: he kept working into his 80s, and in recent months he had said he would always be an actor.

Justice Department sends grand jury subpoenas to California election officials in a widening noncitizen voting probe

The Justice Department has issued grand jury subpoenas to local election officials and election workers in California, including in Los Angeles, as part of a criminal investigation into alleged voting by noncitizens, officials confirmed Thursday. A grand jury is a panel of citizens that hears evidence in secret and can approve criminal charges, so a subpoena from one signals a criminal inquiry rather than a civil dispute. The department says as many as 20,000 noncitizens may be on California's voter rolls, a figure that state officials and voting-rights lawyers have disputed; in a separate civil suit, some of the people named turned out not to be registered in the county being sued. The subpoenas arrive days before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Reporting this week counted at least 44 grand jury subpoenas to election officials nationwide, most of them issued in the past three months, and the department has charged 113 people in 20 states with illegal voting or registration over the seven weeks before Oct. 19. The department declined to confirm or deny its use of subpoenas, saying it "will enforce the law wherever the evidence leads." Studies have found noncitizen voting to be exceedingly rare, and no one has been convicted in the California cases brought so far.

Amazon falls about 7% and Intel more than 20% after earnings as worries over AI spending spread

Amazon shares fell about 7% in premarket trading Friday, and Intel dropped more than 20%, after both companies reported quarterly results Thursday that left investors uneasy about how much big technology firms are spending on artificial intelligence. Amazon posted record sales, up 12% from a year earlier, and its AWS cloud unit grew 39.3%, its fastest pace in about five years, with a backlog of signed but unfilled contracts reaching $578 billion. But Amazon's forecast for holiday-quarter sales came in below analysts' estimates, and its free cash flow, the cash left after paying for equipment and buildings, turned sharply negative because of capital spending on data centers. Net income edged down because the year-earlier quarter included a large gain on its stake in an AI developer. Intel's outlook disappointed too, as personal computer demand stayed weak and a blast at its factory in Kiryat Gat, Israel, this month, which killed two people and injured one, weighed on supply. Stocks in Tokyo, Seoul and China closed lower in response. The Nasdaq Composite had already fallen 1.38% on Thursday, to 26,184.73, before either report.

Alaska governor orders an investigation into Mary Peltola's Senate campaign over a second "Dan Sullivan"

Alaska Gov. Mike Dunleavy, a Republican, announced Thursday that he is ordering an investigation into Democrat Mary Peltola's U.S. Senate campaign after a newspaper report said people aligned with it helped recruit Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a man who shares a name with the Republican incumbent, Sen. Dan Sullivan. Critics call that kind of entry a spoiler candidate, a name placed on the ballot mainly to confuse voters and pull votes from a rival. Peltola said Thursday night she "knew nothing about" the other Dan Sullivan and is "not interested in tricking voters." The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee has denied involvement, and Peltola's former campaign manager, fired in August, told the newspaper that Peltola was not directly involved but was "definitely aware." The federal piece is moving faster: the Justice Department has begun a new wave of subpoenas in an ongoing criminal investigation into how the second Sullivan reached the ballot, and officials are weighing possible charges. Sen. Tim Scott has called for a Justice Department investigation of the campaign, and President Trump has called the episode voter fraud. Alaska's election is Nov. 3.

Brewers beat the Rays 4-1 in Game 5 to win the first World Series title in franchise history

The Milwaukee Brewers won the World Series on Wednesday night, beating the Tampa Bay Rays 4-1 in Game 5 at Tropicana Field in St. Petersburg, Florida, to take the series four games to one. It is the first championship for a franchise that began as the Seattle Pilots in 1969 and has played in Milwaukee since 1970, and it comes from the smallest market in the major leagues. Milwaukee had the best record in baseball this season and went up three games to none after a 10-1 win in Game 3 before the Rays staved off a sweep in Game 4. In the clincher, starter Jacob Misiorowski threw five and two-thirds scoreless innings. Catcher William Contreras was named series MVP after batting .381.

The win answers a long drought. The Brewers had reached the World Series once before, in 1982, and lost to St. Louis in seven games. Their title run now becomes the case that a low-payroll team built on pitching, speed and small-ball offense can win it all in the sport's current era.
