ULA’s Vulcan Rocket Fails on a Return-to-Flight Launch, Destroying 40 Amazon Internet Satellites

United Launch Alliance says the rocket suffered an anomaly and did not deliver the satellites to orbit; it has not said what went wrong.

A United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket failed in flight on Sunday afternoon, Nov. 15, after lifting off from Cape Canaveral, Florida, and the 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites it carried were lost. It is the first outright failure for United Launch Alliance, the joint venture of Boeing and Lockheed Martin, and it came on a mission billed as a return-to-flight mission, the first launch after a rocket has had problems that needed fixing. The company said the vehicle suffered an anomaly, the industry’s word for something going wrong that was not planned, and did not deliver the satellites to their planned orbit. It has not said what went wrong or which part of the rocket failed. A Vulcan has two main sections: a first stage with strap-on boosters that lifts it off the pad, and an upper stage that finishes the climb and releases the cargo. Until the company or investigators say which one failed, any explanation is a guess.

Amazon Leo is Amazon’s planned constellation of broadband satellites in low Earth orbit, a few hundred miles above the ground, built to sell internet service in competition with SpaceX’s Starlink. A network like that needs a large number of satellites in the sky before it can cover the world, so every launch matters, and losing 40 at once sets the build-out back by a full batch. The payload was destroyed with the rocket, so none of the satellites can be recovered or flown again. Amazon has not said how the loss changes its launch schedule or when it expects to widen service. What happens next depends on the investigation: United Launch Alliance has to find the cause, and the U.S. Space Force, which books Vulcan for some national-security missions, will be watching the results. No date has been given for the rocket’s next flight.

Texas U.S. Attorney Tells Sen. Warren to Preserve Records Over Planned Probes of Trump Associates and Sets a Nov. 23 Deadline

Aaron Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, sent Sen. Elizabeth Warren a letter on Sunday, Nov. 15, telling her to keep all records tied to the investigations she has said she wants to open into people and companies linked to President Donald Trump. The letter covers Warren, her Senate staff, consultants and campaign personnel, and asks her to confirm by Nov. 23 that she received it and is preserving the material. Reitz argued that a congressional investigation must serve a legitimate lawmaking purpose, and that singling out people because of their association with Trump could violate their constitutional rights and federal civil-rights laws. Warren has not been accused of a crime, and no court has found that any planned inquiry is unlawful. A preservation letter is a formal request to keep documents because they may be needed in a later legal matter. It is not a charge, though it warns of consequences if records are destroyed. Reitz sent similar letters last week to Democratic Sens. Richard Blumenthal and Elissa Slotkin, who had spoken publicly about investigating Trump associates after the Democratic midterm wins. Warren’s office had not responded publicly to the letter as of Sunday, and it is not known whether she will answer by the deadline.

Fall Survey Finds U.S. International Enrollment Down About 9%, With New and Graduate Students Hit Hardest

International enrollment at U.S. colleges fell about 9% this fall, according to a preliminary survey of more than 800 institutions by the Institute of International Education. The drop was steepest among newly arriving students, put at about 15% in most reports and as high as 18% in one, so the exact figure is unsettled. Graduate enrollment fell about 15%, against about 3% at the undergraduate level, and students working in the U.S. under Optional Practical Training, which lets graduates work after finishing a degree, fell about 6%. In the survey, 88% of responding colleges named the visa application process as an obstacle and 70% named travel restrictions.

A separate full-year count for 2025-26 shows a smaller decline, about 3%, because it measures a different period. International students often pay full tuition, so falling numbers show up in college budgets. In those full-year figures California’s enrollment fell at about twice the national rate, and New York edged ahead as the state with the most international students; a count built on federal visa records ranks the two states differently, so the order depends on the data used. Colleges say Trump administration visa restrictions and proposed fees for international students have added to the uncertainty.

Senator-Elect Colom Says Democrats Should Invest in Mississippi and Vows to Press Trump on His 2024 Promises

Scott Colom, the Democrat who defeated Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith in Mississippi, used Sunday interviews to argue that his party should put money and organizing into the state and the wider South. “We didn’t have a bunch of resources,” he said, adding that little campaign money was spent to win Mississippi compared with other states. The senator-elect would be the first Democrat Mississippi has elected to the Senate in more than four decades, and the win surprised many in his party. He said investing in the South could help Democrats build a durable majority.

On Trump, Colom said voters sent him to make sure the president follows through on commitments from his 2024 campaign, which he listed as lowering prices, ending wars and releasing the Epstein files, and he said Trump has not fully released them. He said he would work with the president while pressing him on those points. Colom also named health care and grocery prices as priorities and called for a ban on insider trading by members of Congress, their families and executive branch officials.

NTSB Says the Tanker That Hit a Docked New Orleans Riverboat Lost Steering Minutes Before the Crash

Federal investigators say the tanker that slammed into a docked riverboat in New Orleans on Friday suffered a steering failure shortly before impact. The National Transportation Safety Board said its preliminary review of the tanker’s voyage data recorder, the ship’s equivalent of a flight recorder, shows the collision came about two minutes after the steering problem began. The recorder also captured warning whistles and emergency orders from the bridge in the minutes before impact. The board has not said why the steering failed, and its findings are preliminary.

The riverboat capsized after the collision, but officials said everyone aboard was accounted for, an outcome described as a miracle. Investigators have recovered the recorder and are continuing their work. What caused the failure, and whether anything could have been done in the two minutes before impact, are the main open questions.
