Federal Appeals Court Declines to Block the Army From Detonating 68 Barrels of Unstable Lead Azide in Iowa, Hours After Trump Said He Would Defer to the Court

Reports say the Army will now go ahead, but no new detonation date has been confirmed and the president's hold has not been formally lifted.

A federal appeals court on Monday declined to block the Army from detonating a stockpile of unstable explosives at an ammunition plant in Iowa, and reports say the Army will now go ahead. The ruling came the same day President Trump said the blast should not happen until a legal challenge was decided and that he would defer to the appeals court. At issue are 68 barrels holding about 9,500 pounds of lead azide, an explosive compound used in detonators, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown. Lead azide is set off easily by shock or friction, which is why the Army treats the degraded barrels as dangerous to handle. The Army says a safety review found the material unstable and that it cannot be safely moved or treated any way other than being detonated where it sits; neutralizing it with chemicals or trucking it to another demolition site were rejected as too dangerous. Environmental groups sued last week to stop the detonation, and residents and lawmakers have said the plan was not communicated to them properly.

The blast is expected to throw up a plume of dust containing lead particles, and one account says it could reach as far as 100 miles. Lead is a toxic metal, and the main worry is dust settling on yards, cars and playground equipment. Officials have told nearby residents to expect loud noise and possible window damage, and to hose down outdoor surfaces, wipe children's play equipment and wash cars where dust lands. A firm new detonation date has not been confirmed, and the date has shifted in earlier reporting. It is also not clear whether Trump will formally lift his hold, or whether the groups that sued will appeal or ask the court for an injunction, an order that would halt the blast. Trump's earlier hold had already added confusion to a plan that state officials, residents and environmental groups were contesting. What matters next is how much warning residents get once a date is set, and what the Army and state officials say about air and soil testing afterward.

Trump Owned at Least $12 Million in Nvidia Stock at the End of 2025, His Annual Financial Disclosure Shows, One Report Says

President Trump owned at least $12 million in Nvidia stock at the end of 2025, according to his annual financial disclosure report, as reviewed by one news organization. The figure surfaced a day after Trump pushed Nvidia's board to reinstate Jensen Huang as chief executive, a move that sent the company's shares sharply higher and drew criticism from lawmakers of both parties. Top Democrats have raised corruption concerns about Trump's involvement. Such concerns turn on conflict of interest, a situation in which an official's personal financial stake could pull against public duty. The disclosure is a snapshot of a single date, so it does not show what Trump owns today or whether any holding changed after the year ended. Disclosure forms report assets in ranges, which is why the figure is a minimum rather than an exact value. No response from the White House to the report has been found, and no source quoted in it ties the stock holding to the decision on Huang.

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, Who Is Leaving Office, Has Dropped Multiple Lawsuits From Culture-War Fights, Including Cases Against the NCAA and a Muslim Civil-Rights Group, Reports Say

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, whose term is ending, has dropped a string of lawsuits that made him a national figure in culture-war fights. Reports say they include a suit accusing the NCAA of deceptive marketing over transgender athletes in women's sports, a suit against the Council on American-Islamic Relations, a case against the El Paso migrant shelter network Annunciation House, and cases against two doctors over transgender health care. Some of the dismissals were filed as notices of nonsuit, a plaintiff's own move to end a case.

How final the dismissals are differs from case to case. The state ended the Annunciation House case with prejudice, which means similar allegations cannot be filed again, while the suit against the Council on American-Islamic Relations was dropped without prejudice, which leaves room to refile. Paxton's office has not been quoted in the reporting reviewed explaining why the cases are being dropped, so the reasons are not established. Paxton is leaving office at the end of his term, and the cases were among the legal battles most closely tied to his time as attorney general.
