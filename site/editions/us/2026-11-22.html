Trump Says the FBI's Nationwide Crime Database Was Targeted in a Hack, a Day After Unnamed Sources Described an Apparent Breach

The president spoke on the record on Saturday about an intrusion that two unnamed sources described to reporters on Friday; what was taken has not been reported.

President Trump said on Saturday, Nov. 21, that the FBI's National Crime Information Center, the nationwide database police use to check names, vehicles and other records, was targeted in a hack. The day before, two people briefed on the matter, who were not named, said the bureau was investigating an apparent breach of the system. A breach means someone got into a computer system without permission, and the possibilities range from looking at records to copying or changing them. None of those outcomes has been confirmed. The center connects federal, state and local law enforcement, so an officer making a traffic stop or a dispatcher handling a missing-person call can pull up the same records no matter which agency entered them. When a patrol officer runs a license plate, for example, the system can show whether the car has been reported stolen. It holds entries such as wanted persons, stolen vehicles, missing persons and protection orders. The first reports of an apparent breach came on Friday evening, Nov. 20, and no named source has given a cause.

No official has been reported saying what was accessed, how long the intruders had a way in, or who is responsible. It is also not established whether any records were stolen, altered or deleted, or whether police can still run checks as usual. The only sourcing for the investigation itself is anonymous, and the president's statement, as reported, says the system was targeted without describing the result. The practical stakes are indirect but real: officers rely on the system to learn in seconds whether a driver has an open warrant or a car is stolen, so the accuracy of its records matters as much as who might have seen them. The users of the system include police departments, sheriff's offices and other criminal justice agencies, which is why a statement about its security reaches well beyond the FBI. Still to come are a statement from the FBI or the Justice Department on the scope, any notice to the state and local agencies that use the system, and any claim of responsibility.

Masked Immigration Officers Detain Two Passengers on a Jet Bridge at Seattle-Tacoma Airport, and Washington Officials Condemn It, Reports Say

Plainclothes immigration officers wearing masks detained two people as they boarded a flight at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport on Saturday, Nov. 21, according to reports. One report says at least one person was detained, so the exact number is not settled. The arrests took place on a crowded jet bridge, the enclosed walkway between the terminal and the aircraft, and one report says the flight was delayed. Washington's governor and airport officials criticized the arrests, and airport officials called the scene "distressing." The officers were from ICE, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, the Department of Homeland Security agency that arrests and deports people in the country without legal status. Who the two passengers are, what immigration or criminal case they face, and where they were taken have not been reported. One report quotes an attorney for a family detained at the airport saying they are "traumatized" and disputing claims made by ICE; the claims themselves are not spelled out in what has been reported. The Port of Seattle, which runs the airport, issued a statement about the enforcement actions. Next to watch are any federal account of why the arrests were made at a boarding gate, any court filing for the detained people, and whether Washington officials take formal action.

A Mathematician Who Works for Anthropic Says He Solved the Riemann hypothesis Using Claude, but the Claim Has Not Been Independently Confirmed

A mathematician who works for Anthropic says he has solved the Riemann hypothesis, one of the best-known unsolved problems in mathematics, with the help of the company's AI tool Claude. The hypothesis is a conjecture, a statement believed true but never proved, about the zeros of the zeta function, a formula tied to how prime numbers are spread out. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, and the Clay Mathematics Institute offers $1 million for a solution. The claim is the second in recent months that an AI company, or someone linked to one, says it has cracked a famed unsolved problem. In August the company published a partial result on the same problem and said its methods were unlikely to settle the full question.

A claim like this is not accepted until other mathematicians check it. That process, called peer review, usually takes months, and a new proof must survive expert scrutiny before it counts. One report says groups that maintain Lean, software that checks proofs step by step, have certified the work, but that has not been confirmed by named people. Another report says Elon Musk called the result "mind-blowing" while academics urged caution. The mathematician's full argument, who has examined it and whether the proof is complete are the open questions.

Trump Shares Michigan Democrat Abdul El-Sayed's 'Trumpcare' Universal Health Care Pitch on Truth Social, Reports Say

President Trump responded on Saturday, Nov. 21, to Michigan Democrat Abdul El-Sayed, a progressive who has been pushing for universal health care, after El-Sayed suggested it be called "Trumpcare." Trump reposted El-Sayed's proposal on Truth Social, his social media platform. Universal health care means a system in which everyone is covered, and the version El-Sayed has promoted is described in one report as Medicare for All, the government-run insurance idea Democrats have debated for years. A repost is not a policy: no bill, plan or cost estimate has been announced, and the president's post, as reported, does not say what he would support.

One report says El-Sayed answered with "Ready when you are," and says right-wing commentator Laura Loomer reacted by writing "We are not a serious country." A separate report says Trump shared the post after saying he would make a deal with Democrats, though what deal was not spelled out. How Republican leaders in Congress respond, whether Trump says more in his own words, and whether any bill is drafted are the open questions. For now the only confirmed event is a social media post.

Max Verstappen Wins the Las Vegas Grand Prix After Inheriting Pole Position When Kimi Antonelli Took a Penalty, With Lando Norris Second and Oscar Piastri Third

Max Verstappen won the Las Vegas Grand Prix on Saturday night, Nov. 21, driving for Red Bull in a race where the lead changed hands and he was under pressure before he pulled clear in the final lap. Lando Norris finished second and Oscar Piastri third. One report says it was Verstappen's third win in Las Vegas in four years. Ferrari's Charles Leclerc and Lewis Hamilton again clashed on the track. The race ran 50 laps, and Piastri rose from 17th to 12th by Lap 2.

Verstappen had started from pole position, the front spot on the starting grid, only because of a penalty to Kimi Antonelli. Antonelli, the newly crowned world champion, set the fastest time in qualifying but took a grid penalty and started sixth. Verstappen lined up on the front row beside Mercedes' George Russell. The reason for the penalty is not given in the reports. The next race on the calendar and the updated standings are the next things to watch.
