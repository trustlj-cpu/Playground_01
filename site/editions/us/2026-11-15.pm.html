Megaspeed Says Alice Huang Left in August 2025 and Denies Wrongdoing as Pressure on Lutnick Grows

The Singapore company says inspections found no illegal chip transfers, Singapore’s investigation continues, and Sen. Blumenthal says the Commerce secretary “must go.”

Jensen Huang, who resigned as Nvidia’s chief executive on Nov. 12, is the subject of an unconfirmed report, not attributed to a named source, that he has begun talking with allies about returning to the job. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress has run the company as interim chief executive since the board announced his departure, and a petition asking the board to reinstate him has passed 135,000 signatures. Elon Musk signed it and wrote that Nvidia will lose “critical deals” without Huang. The company is due to report quarterly results on Tuesday, Nov. 17, with Kress leading it. The resignation followed disclosures about Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang, a former executive at the Singapore firm Megaspeed, and questions about whether it created a national-security risk. Whether she had any intelligence ties has not been established publicly.

Megaspeed said Sunday that Alice Huang left the company in August 2025 and denied any wrongdoing; it says inspections by U.S. and Malaysian authorities and by Nvidia found no illegal chip transfers. Singapore’s Home Affairs ministry said on Oct. 31 that its investigation into Megaspeed is continuing. The scandal has also reached Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whose department issues export licenses for advanced chips: Sen. Richard Blumenthal says Lutnick “must go,” Rep. Crawford calls it premature to blame him for Nvidia’s chip sales, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has called for a shake-up of Nvidia’s leadership. Huang has agreed to testify to Congress in a closed-door session, but the date is not confirmed because reports of it conflict.

Duffy Asks Congress for $50 Billion to Rebuild Air Traffic Control as Democrats Say $12.5 Billion Already Approved Is Largely Unspent

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said Sunday that a router being replaced as the FAA switched from copper to fiber lines began flooding other routers with data, setting off Friday’s nationwide ground stop, which shut down air traffic for more than four hours. Investigators are examining two routers, and the routers’ ability to broadcast data has been turned off while they work. Duffy said there is no sign of a cyberattack or a foreign actor, could not say whether a faulty router or human error was to blame, and could not guarantee that a similar meltdown will not happen again. He said the timing of maintenance work will be reassessed. Delays and cancellations lingered into Sunday as the system recovered. Duffy is asking Congress for $50 billion to modernize the system, up from the $30 billion he requested in a letter on Thursday. President Trump, in his first comments since the outage, wrote on Truth Social that the Biden administration was an “FAA disaster” and that Congress must provide the money “FAST.” Democrats say money is not the obstacle: two House Democrats who oversee transportation spending say Congress approved $12.5 billion for air traffic control upgrades 15 months ago, of which the administration has obligated $4.3 billion and spent $1 billion. Duffy says the approved funds are all accounted for, will be deployed by the end of 2028, and are not enough.

Iran Launches Six Drones at a U.S. Air Base in Bahrain, Its Second Claimed Strike There in Three Days, and Bahrain Says It Intercepted Them

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said on Sunday that it fired six drones at an air-defense radar at Riffa Air Base in Bahrain, a base used by U.S. forces, calling it retaliation for hostile U.S. actions. Bahrain’s Defence Force said its air defenses intercepted and destroyed the drones before they could achieve their objectives, and Bahrain’s foreign ministry condemned the attack and held Iran fully responsible for the consequences. Bahrain has not confirmed that any radar was hit. Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Jordan and Egypt issued statements of solidarity with Bahrain.

It was the second strike Iran has claimed on a U.S. facility in Bahrain in three days: on Friday the Guard said it destroyed a U.S. early-warning radar there. The attacks come after months of relative quiet following a run of Iranian strikes on Bahrain, Kuwait and Jordan in June and July, when Tehran said it was answering U.S. strikes. Bahrain hosts the U.S. Navy’s Fifth Fleet, and the United States and Iran remain in a standoff over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which much of the world’s oil ships. The United States has not announced a response.

Van Hollen Says He Has a Path to Unseat Schumer as the Senate Democrats’ Leader, and Schumer Says He Has the Votes

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland said on Sunday that he has a “wide-open” path to replace Chuck Schumer as the Senate Democrats’ leader and that his challenge is gaining support, calling it a “bunch of nonsense” that he cannot win. Schumer has said he will run again and that he has the votes. The leader is chosen by secret ballot of the Democratic caucus, which includes the senators-elect, and a candidate needs a majority, so the contest turns on head counts that neither side has made public. Van Hollen has said the choice of the next leader should not be automatic, and Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed has said he would back him.

The fight follows Democratic wins in this month’s midterm elections and growing calls from the party’s progressive wing for new leadership. Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan has also been mentioned as a possible challenger. Van Hollen says his campaign is about more inclusion within the caucus and openness to change among his colleagues.

Sen. Cassidy Says RFK Jr. Is Covering “a Series of Failures” at HHS and Will Not Predict the Outcome of a Pending Committee Vote

Sen. Bill Cassidy, a Louisiana Republican and physician, said Sunday that Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is “covering a series of failures,” and called for accountability for higher health care costs, more measles deaths, the loss of the country’s measles-elimination status and what he described as no progress on processed food. Cassidy, who lost his bid for re-election and leaves the Senate early next year, has been at odds with Kennedy for months over vaccine policy. Asked how he would vote in a pending committee vote, he said, “I still have questions, and so I cannot tell you the outcome of that vote right now.”

Asked whether Congress should impeach Kennedy, Cassidy said, “You can impeach, I guess, over a ham sandwich, so they’ll have to decide what to do on that.” In February 2025 his vote was the pivotal one that moved Kennedy’s nomination out of committee, after Kennedy made commitments on vaccine policy.
