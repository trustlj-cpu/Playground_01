Senate Banking Chair Asks Huang and Lutnick to Testify; Reports Differ on Whether Huang Has Agreed

New development: the committee chair’s request is on record, and the attorney general says the question of whether Alice Huang was a spy is unresolved.

Sen. Tim Scott, the Republican who chairs the Senate Banking Committee, said Thursday that he has asked Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to appear before the panel, a day after Huang resigned as Nvidia’s chief executive. It is the first formal step toward public testimony in the affair, and no hearing date has been announced. The committee has jurisdiction over export controls, the rules that govern which advanced chips American companies may sell abroad, including to China. Accounts differ on Huang’s answer. One report says he has agreed to testify after senior lawmakers threatened a subpoena, a legal order that compels a witness to appear or hand over documents. Another says Republicans are still negotiating for him to come, and a third says GOP lawmakers have renewed their demand that he update Congress on the espionage allegations. Lutnick has agreed to testify, according to one report that other outlets had not matched by early Friday. Nvidia has not said why Huang left, and Colette Kress is running the company as interim chief executive.

Lutnick is in the frame because his department licenses those exports. Critics say Commerce moved too slowly to stop Nvidia chips from being diverted to China, and ask whether his friendship with Huang softened oversight; Lutnick’s response was not available. Sen. Jim Banks, an Indiana Republican, said Lutnick should testify, accused him of using a “loophole,” and said, “This news is as concerning as it gets.” On the espionage question, Attorney General Todd Blanche said Thursday that the Justice Department has not yet concluded whether Alice Huang, the woman Huang is reported to have had a relationship with, was a Chinese spy. That is the most precise official statement so far: the spy claim is an allegation, not a finding. Reports on the White House’s view of Lutnick conflict. One says President Trump is standing by him; another, citing unnamed sources, says aides are weighing his removal. Neither is confirmed. Still open: the hearing date and format, whether Huang appears under subpoena or voluntarily, and what Nvidia says about the departure.

Van Hollen Says He Will Challenge Schumer for the Top Senate Democratic Post

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland announced Thursday that he will run against Sen. Chuck Schumer of New York for leader of the Senate Democratic caucus, the group of Democratic senators who choose their own leader. He announced the bid on X, saying: “The American people called for change.” Van Hollen acknowledged that he is the underdog. Schumer, who holds the post now, says he has the votes to keep it, a claim he made himself that has not been tested. One account describes Van Hollen as the favorite of Democrats who want an alternative to Schumer. The contest is decided by secret ballot among the chamber’s Democratic senators, and one report puts the vote on Dec. 1, which would leave about three weeks for activists and colleagues to press their case. A secret ballot means a senator can pledge publicly to one candidate and vote for another, so announced support is only a guide. Still unknown: whether anyone else enters the race, how many senators commit in public, and whether Schumer’s count holds up when the ballots are cast.

Microsoft Outage Disrupts Outlook and Teams Worldwide; Company Says First Fixes Fell Short

A widespread outage knocked out Microsoft’s Outlook email, Teams and other Microsoft 365 services on Thursday. Reports began arriving from around the world shortly before 1 p.m. Eastern, with users unable to open Outlook on the web or in its apps, and nearly 130,000 users worldwide had reported problems by one running tally. Microsoft acknowledged the problem from its service-status account and said some of its servers were unexpectedly unavailable, which strained the remaining infrastructure. That is Microsoft’s own explanation; the company has not said why the servers failed.

Through the evening, Microsoft’s updates described a slow, step-by-step recovery: it was returning some affected servers to service, diverting traffic to alternate routes where possible, and noting partial recovery. Later, it said its fixes had not “provided the anticipated relief,” and that it was “aggressively pursuing all pathways to reduce impact.” Some users of Amazon and Google services also reported problems on Thursday, but those reports have not been tied to Microsoft’s outage. Still unknown: when service is fully restored, why the servers went down, and whether email sent during the disruption was delayed.

Murder Charge Against Richard Glossip Is Dismissed in 1997 Killing, His Attorney Says; Another Report Describes a Plea

A murder charge against Richard Glossip, a former Oklahoma death row prisoner who came close to execution several times, was dismissed Thursday, according to his attorney. Glossip had been awaiting a retrial in the 1997 killing of his former boss. Another report describes the outcome differently, saying he pleaded guilty to a lesser charge. The early accounts do not make clear whether the two fit together, what the terms of any plea are, or whether Glossip leaves custody.

The case has been through the nation’s highest court. In February 2025 the U.S. Supreme Court threw out his conviction and death sentence, ruling he was entitled to a new trial. Oklahoma’s attorney general, Gentner Drummond, said that June the state would retry Glossip for murder but would not seek the death penalty. A dismissal would end that plan and spare a third trial. What to watch: court filings that set out the terms, any statement from the attorney general’s office, and whether Glossip is released.

Cities Use Environmental-Review Lawsuits to Slow ICE Detention Centers; San Antonio and DOJ Discuss Pausing Its Suit

San Antonio is the latest city to try to stop an immigration detention center not by winning outright but by slowing it down. On Oct. 13 the city sued the Department of Homeland Security and Immigration and Customs Enforcement, arguing that federal officials must finish an environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act before work on a planned East Side facility continues. The city wants a judge to void the federal purchase of the property and the contract to convert a warehouse of more than 640,000 square feet into a center that could hold up to 1,500 people. The city’s attorney has described the suit as a delaying step.

Similar suits elsewhere are part of a wave of legal challenges that is slowing the administration’s push to build detention centers across the country. They do not automatically stop a project: two city council members argued the litigation would cost taxpayers without keeping the facility from opening. This week the city said it is in discussions with the Justice Department on an agreement to pause the lawsuit while environmental reviews are conducted. No agreement has been announced. Still open: whether a pause is signed, what the reviews find, and when the center might open.
