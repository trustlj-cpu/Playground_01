Prime Minister Takaichi and President Xi Only Shake Hands; Talks Expected to Be Skipped, About 30 Minutes With Putin

On the sidelines of the APEC leaders' meeting in Shenzhen, China, the leaders of Japan and China met face to face. No formal talks are expected to be set.

On the night of the 18th, ahead of the dinner at the leaders' meeting of APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation), which opened in Shenzhen, China, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi was greeted by President Xi Jinping and shook hands. It was the prime minister's first visit to China, and it is also said to be the first time the leaders of Japan and China have met in person since the prime minister's Diet answer on a Taiwan contingency. However, contact between the two leaders stopped at a handshake, and formal talks are expected to be skipped. It is also reported to be unusual for no talks to be set with a major country's leader. APEC is an annual framework in which countries and regions of the Asia-Pacific discuss liberalizing trade and investment, and each year leaders gather at the host's leaders' meeting. China is the chair this year.

At the same dinner, the prime minister also exchanged views with Russian President Putin for about 30 minutes. What was said has not been made clear at this point. Meanwhile, the first-ever three-way meeting of U.S. President Trump, President Xi and President Putin is scheduled for the 19th, and Japan is not part of that framework. If talks are skipped, it is still too early to judge whether the handshake will become the entrance to a resumption of dialogue between the two countries or merely show the current state of relations. A handshake is a gesture of greeting, and it is separate from talks in which policy is discussed. What the Japanese side conveyed to the Chinese side, and what it could not convey, will have to await later explanations.

Supreme Court Grand Bench Rules for the First Time That Provision Denying Prisoners the Vote Under Public Offices Election Act Is “Constitutional”

On the 18th, the Grand Bench of the Supreme Court gave its first ruling that a provision of the Public Offices Election Act that uniformly denies prisoners serving sentences the vote does not violate the Constitution, which guarantees the right to vote; in other words, that it is constitutional. The Grand Bench is the Supreme Court's weightiest panel, hearing cases with all 15 justices, and it handles important matters such as constitutional rulings. This conclusion came in a lawsuit contesting whether the provision violates the Constitution. The ruling was not unanimous, and opinion among the justices was divided. Some opinions urged caution about a uniform restriction, and the Supreme Court in effect called on the public to debate the matter. Even though the conclusion is that the provision is constitutional, room to review how the restriction is designed is left to the Diet. It is reported that some countries abroad abolished restrictions on prisoners' voting altogether after a ruling that they were unconstitutional. Whether the Diet will take up the provision from now on is not yet known.

Bill to Cut House of Representatives Seats by 31 Enters Substantive Deliberation; Three Sponsoring Parties Stress Its Merit, Other Opposition Parties Opposed or Wary

A bill to cut the House of Representatives' number of seats by 31 in proportional representation entered substantive deliberation in a special committee on the 18th. The three parties that submitted the bill, the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP), Ishin and the Democratic Party for the People, stressed the significance of the cut. Other opposition parties, by contrast, voiced views against making the cut in proportional representation alone, and arguments against the cut itself.

On the opposition side, there is criticism that the bill shifts the point at issue away from the slush-fund scandal. The number of seats is the total number of House of Representatives members, and proportional representation is a system that allocates seats according to the votes parties receive. Which parties lose seats looks different depending on one's position, and each party has released its own estimates. The timing of a vote has not been made clear.

Singer Hiromi Ohta Dies at 71; “Cotton Handkerchief” Was a Huge Hit

Singer Hiromi Ohta has died. Ohta was 71. The news was reported on the 18th. “Cotton Handkerchief,” released in 1975, became a huge hit and a song that stays in many people's memories along with Ohta's singing voice, and it was widely loved as a song about the exchanges between lovers far apart. It has also been reported that Ohta had disclosed breast cancer in 2019.

The date and time of death and the cause have not been confirmed at this point. The schedule for the funeral and so on has not been made clear either. Because it is a song that has been sung for so long, voices recalling those days are spreading in response to the news.
