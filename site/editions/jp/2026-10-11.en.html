Trump Announces Russia to Supply Over 300,000 Tonnes of Diesel Immediately; U.S. Temporarily Eases Sanctions on Russia, Zelensky Pushes Back

Buying fuel that has grown more expensive from a country that is still waging war. Ukraine and Europe pushed back at the U.S. president's decision, saying it would “fund the war.”

On the 9th, in a phone call with Russian President Putin, U.S. President Donald Trump announced that they had agreed that Russia would supply diesel to the U.S. and world markets. It will release more than 300,000 tonnes right away and several million tonnes more over the coming months, he said (Reuters, CBS, NBC). Russia has banned diesel exports since July, but Jiji Press reported that the lifting of the embargo would begin immediately. The Asahi Shimbun reported that the United States had temporarily eased its sanctions on Russia. Trump said, “I want to sincerely thank Putin” (Yomiuri Shimbun), and the background is said to be persistently high diesel prices and the Nov. 3 midterm elections (Mainichi Shimbun, The Japan Times). The U.S. average diesel price is above $6 a gallon, near a record high, and the thinning of crude passing through the Strait of Hormuz because of the war with Iran is also cited as a factor in the shortage (AP). Putin said it would “have a positive effect on the world economy,” the Mainichi Shimbun reported. An aide at the Kremlin said the call was “very friendly” and that neither side tried to hang up first (Reuters).

Ukrainian President Zelensky denounced the agreement as an “investment in the war” and criticized the easing of sanctions as “weakness” (Mainichi Shimbun, Jiji Press, Reuters). Zelensky said that attacks across Ukraine had killed 78 people over two days (Mainichi Shimbun). On the 10th, Zaporizhzhia was attacked and at least 12 people died (Reuters). The German government said it would keep its sanctions on Russia, and an EU spokesperson said the policy of reducing dependence on Russia would not change (Reuters, ANSA). Many are cautious about any effect in lowering prices. The U.S. daily The Washington Post pointed out that the volume is small compared with U.S. demand and runs counter to Ukraine's efforts to pressure Russia by attacking refineries, and Germany's FAZ also sees the impact as limited. WTI crude closed on Friday at $91.85 (+0.39%). The same day, delegations from the United States, Ukraine and Europe held talks in Miami, Florida (Reuters, Jiji Press). The Kremlin explained that Putin had been cautious about an early resumption of talks with Ukraine (Reuters). In the United States, criticism came from both Democrats and Republicans (NU.nl). The focus from here is when actual shipments begin and whether the Russian side confirms the volumes.

Eight Countries Across Japan, Europe and Canada “Strongly Oppose” ICC Sanctions; Foreign Minister Motegi Calls U.S. Secretary of State and President Akane in Quick Succession

On the 9th, the U.S. Treasury Department designated the International Criminal Court (ICC) itself as a sanctions target. In August it had targeted President Akane personally; this time it reached the organization itself (NHK). In response, the foreign ministers of eight countries, including Japan, Britain, France and Germany, issued a joint statement of “strong opposition” in the early hours of the 10th (Mainichi Shimbun, Jiji Press). The EU also touched on “room for dialogue with the United States” (Jiji Press). The EU said it is “ready to take any measures,” and UN Secretary-General Guterres also expressed deep concern about the court's future (Mainichi Shimbun, Jiji Press). ICC President Tomoko Akane said in a video that she “will not yield to intimidation.” Those praising the sanctions included Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu, while Nobel Peace Prize laureate Pillay said they are “unacceptable” (Mainichi Shimbun, BBC). The Mainichi Shimbun reports that Japan stepped further after criticism that it was “weak-kneed.” The same paper also reported that whether to dismantle the ICC has become a security “loyalty test” tied to the U.S. military, and Europe is troubled. Foreign Minister Toshimitsu Motegi conveyed Japan's position to Secretary of State Rubio on the night of the 10th and also consulted by phone with President Akane (NHK, Asahi Shimbun, Mainichi Shimbun). About 60,000 U.S. troops are stationed in Japan, and how to reconcile with the United States is the challenge, The Japan Times noted.

Hakurei Holder Tomoka Nishiyama Defends Her Title at the Hakurei Tournament for a Fifth Term; First Woman to Earn the Right to Become a Professional Shogi Player

Game 5 of the Hakurei Tournament, a women's shogi title match, was played on the 10th in Kanazawa, and Hakurei titleholder Tomoka Nishiyama (31) beat four-title holder Kana Fukuma to defend her title. It is her fifth term as titleholder overall, and under a rule the Japan Shogi Association introduced last year, she became the first woman to earn the right to become a professional shogi player (NHK, Jiji Press, Asahi Shimbun, Mainichi Shimbun). The Sankei Shimbun reported a fourth straight title, and the Asahi Shimbun wrote that it is already not rare for women's professionals to beat professionals. The Hakurei Tournament is the highest-ranking title for women's professionals, and the rule allows anyone who wins five terms overall to transfer to professional status. Ms. Nishiyama reached 3-dan in the Shoreikai, the school that trains professionals, then left in 2021 to become a women's professional player, and she also took on the best-of-five professional transfer exam but fell short at 2 wins to 3 losses (Akita Sakigake Shimpo). A professional is a player who competes in official games as a full member of the Association, while women's professionals belong to a separate system. She cleared that wall by another route. According to the Mainichi Shimbun, Ms. Nishiyama said she had been “trying not to think about” professional status.

However, she has not decided whether to use the right. At the press conference, Ms. Nishiyama said, “I can't give an answer now,” and that she wants to decide after calm discussion (Jiji Press, Yomiuri Shimbun, Asahi Shimbun). The Association's president commented that it is “meaningful,” and Akiko Takojima, the very first women's professional, also spoke of her joy (Mainichi Shimbun). We will wait for the Association's announcement on the procedure and timing if she exercises the right.

Danjiri Festival Float Overturns in Izumisano, Osaka: 1 Dead, 7–8 Injured

Shortly before 11 a.m. on the 10th in Izumisano, Osaka Prefecture, a float in the danjiri festival overturned at an intersection, leaving people trapped beneath it. A report to the fire department said “several people are injured,” and NHK initially reported that nine people, thought to be in their 20s to 60s, were taken to hospital. One man was later confirmed dead. The Mainichi Shimbun reported he was in his 50s, the Asahi Shimbun that he was 51. Another 7–8 people were seriously or lightly injured (Jiji Press, Mainichi Shimbun, Asahi Shimbun). The location was an intersection in Nagataki; the Asahi Shimbun mentioned the possibility that it failed to complete the turn, but the cause is still under investigation, and the organizers' explanation and any decision on whether to continue the festival have not yet been confirmed.

According to the Mainichi Shimbun, overturned danjiri were reported one after another across Osaka that day, while the festivals themselves continued in many places. A danjiri is a heavy wooden float that appears in towns in autumn, hauled at a run on ropes by many pullers. At corners, the weight makes the float tilt easily, the most dangerous moment. The condition of the seriously injured and the final tally of the injured have not yet been confirmed. While festivals continue, spectators too should stay away from corners and follow the instructions of staff.

Prime Minister Takaichi Skips Online G7 Leaders' Meeting; Opposition Voices Criticism: “Diplomatic Schedule Comes First”

It has come to light that Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi did not attend the online meeting of G7 leaders set by France, which holds the G7 presidency. The prime minister explained that “France set it up suddenly, right before the day,” but reports say this conflicts with France's announcement (d menu News). Kyodo News reported that the prime minister had no public engagements announced and pointed out that her presence may be fading. The Japan Times reports that, over the U.S. sanctions on the ICC, the prime minister is once again caught in the middle.

Opposition parties voiced a string of rebukes. Yuichiro Tamaki, leader of the Democratic Party for the People, said, “The diplomatic schedule comes first,” and also sounded the alarm over North Korea's new drones (d menu News). The leader of Sanseito also criticized the prime minister's absence (Jiji Press). The same day, the eight countries of Japan, Europe and Canada joined the statement opposing the ICC sanctions, and occasions that test Japan's diplomatic voice continue. At home, the ruling side is asking for a bill to cut the consumption tax on food to begin deliberation this month, and the opposition is consulting, including on a censure motion against Agriculture Minister Kazuo Yana (NHK). The prime minister is being asked for explanations on both diplomacy and the Diet. The prime minister explains that the tax cut would lighten the burden by about ¥36,000 per person (The Japan Times). The meeting's agenda and the details of the Foreign Ministry's view have not been confirmed.
