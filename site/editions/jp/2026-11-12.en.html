Supreme Court Grand Bench Finds Last July's Upper House Election in “Unconstitutional State”; Disparity of Up to 3.13 Times, Claim to Invalidate Election Rejected

First ruling since merged districts were introduced, and the first on an upper house election in about 12 years. The Diet is now pressed to revisit the electoral system.

The Supreme Court's Grand Bench ruled on the 11th that the July 2025 House of Councillors election, in which the disparity in the value of a vote had widened to as much as 3.13 times, was in an “unconstitutional state” contrary to equality in the value of votes. However, it rejected the claim seeking election invalidation, so the victories themselves remain valid. The ruling was a final appeal judgment handling 16 lawsuits together, and Chief Justice Yukihiko Imazaki presided. The Grand Bench expressed the view that “the imbalance in the value of votes has reached a level that cannot be overlooked.” The “vote-value disparity” is a ratio showing how much the weight of one vote differs between a district with fewer voters and one with more; at 3.13 times, one vote in a given district carries only about a third of the weight of a vote in another district.

An “unconstitutional state” is a ruling that the situation violates the equality the Constitution demands but has not yet exceeded the grace period the Diet has to correct it, so it stops short of the kind of unconstitutionality that would invalidate an election. It is the first such ruling on a House of Councillors election in about 12 years, and the first since 2016, when “merged districts” were introduced, combining neighboring prefectures such as Tottori and Shimane, and Tokushima and Kochi, into a single electoral district. At the high court stage, opinions had split, with 11 of the 16 cases finding an unconstitutional state and 5 finding the election constitutional. The 2023 Supreme Court ruling on the 2022 election also called correcting the disparity an “urgent task.” The parties differ sharply on how to correct it, and some point out that merged districts have their limits. The Diet's concrete response to the ruling has not yet been decided.

Government Studying Plan to Write Emergency Exception Into “No Introduction” Principle of Three Non-Nuclear Principles in Revising Security Documents, Officials Say; Not a Formal Decision

The government is studying a plan to write an emergency exception into the “no introduction” principle of the Three Non-Nuclear Principles in revising the three security-related documents (the three security documents), which it plans to revise within the year, multiple reports revealed on the 11th. The reports were attributed to senior administration officials, and the government has not made an official announcement. The Three Non-Nuclear Principles are the national policy of “not possessing, not producing and not allowing the introduction of nuclear weapons,” and the current National Security Strategy says it will “uphold” them. The plan is said to keep the principles themselves while writing into the document room for an exception allowing nuclear weapons to be brought in during an emergency. Behind it lies the reply that then Foreign Minister Katsuya Okada gave in the Diet in 2010 (the Okada reply). It held that in an emergency in which Japan's safety could not be protected without allowing a temporary port call by a ship carrying nuclear weapons, the government of the day would make a political judgment; successive governments have carried this position over, and Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has likewise declared that the position will be carried over. A senior government official reportedly said, “Is it right to keep calling it the Okada reply forever?” and indicated a wish to incorporate wording of the Takaichi government's own. The prime minister told the House of Representatives Budget Committee in October that there is “room for consideration” in the wording. In response, atomic bomb survivors have voiced criticism that the horror of nuclear weapons is not understood. The content of the document has not yet been settled.

LDP and Ishin Approve Bill to Cut 31 Proportional Seats in House of Representatives; Joint Submission With Democratic Party for the People Planned as Early as the 12th

On the 11th the LDP and Nippon Ishin no Kai held a ruling-party policy chiefs' meeting and approved a bill to cut 31 seats from the House of Representatives' proportional representation seats. The bill was put together by working-level officials of three parties, including the Democratic Party for the People, which is also reported to have approved it at a party meeting. The three parties will submit it jointly to the Diet as early as the 12th and aim for enactment in the current Diet session. On October 29 the secretaries-general of the three parties had formally agreed on the 31-seat cut. The bill includes changing the method of allocating proportional seats to the modified Sainte-Laguë method and merging the Chugoku and Shikoku proportional blocs.

Meanwhile, six opposition parties including Komeito have voiced opposition, saying the grounds for the cut are weak. Because the ruling bloc is a minority in the House of Councillors, support from opposition parties such as the Democratic Party for the People is essential for enactment. Concern has also surfaced that the cut could reduce the number of women lawmakers, and debate from a gender perspective has arisen. The bill will actually be submitted on the 12th or later, and the deliberation schedule has not been set.

Tokyo Metropolitan Government Offers New “2 Trillion Yen for Regions” Proposal on Reviewing Local Allocation Tax; Talks With Government Over Tax Imbalance

Over correcting the “tax imbalance,” which would send tax revenue concentrated in Tokyo to the regions, the Tokyo Metropolitan Government, led by Governor Yuriko Koike and others, presented a new proposal in talks with the national government on the 11th. It is reported to be a plan under which revising the local allocation tax mechanism would send 2 trillion yen to the regions. The metropolitan government has opposed the imbalance-correcting measures under consideration on the national side as “unreasonable measures.” At the talks on the 11th, Governor Koike asked of the national measures, “What kind of effect does it have?”

The local allocation tax is a mechanism by which the national government distributes funds to make up differences in resources, from municipalities with more tax revenue to those with less, and the Tokyo Metropolitan Government is classed as a non-recipient municipality, one that receives no allocation. On the national side, a review of the special corporate business tax and redistribution of fixed asset tax in Tokyo's 23 wards have reportedly been named as items under consideration. The government and ruling parties are said to have the fiscal 2027 tax reform in view. The basis for the 2 trillion yen figure and the national side's reaction have not been made clear.

Nissan Cuts Global Sales Forecast for This Fiscal Year to as Low as 3 Million Units, Keeps Operating Profit Forecast Unchanged

On the 11th, Nissan Motor lowered its global sales forecast for the fiscal year ending March 2027 from the previous 3.15 million units to 2.97–3 million units. It had also cut the forecast in August, from 3.3 million to 3.15 million, making this the second downward revision this term. In China, the overall market fell 22% in the first half and Nissan's retail sales fell 15%. Meanwhile, the United States, which accounts for about a third of Nissan's sales, rose 6%.

The full-year operating profit forecast was left unchanged at 200 billion yen. Operating profit is the profit earned from the core business; it was 80 billion yen for April–September, but only 2.1 billion yen for July–September alone. The first-half net result was a loss, and the size of the loss is reported to have narrowed. Management explained measures to rebuild sales.
