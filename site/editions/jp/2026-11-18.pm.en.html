Law Denying Prisoners the Vote Is “Constitutional,” Supreme Court Grand Bench Rules in First Decision

The Supreme Court's Grand Bench has issued its first ruling that the provision of the Public Offices Election Act denying the vote across the board to inmates serving sentences does not violate the Constitution.

On the 18th, the Grand Bench of the Supreme Court ruled that the provision of the Public Offices Election Act that denies the vote across the board to inmates serving sentences is “constitutional,” holding that it does not violate the Constitution, which guarantees the right to vote. It is the first time the Supreme Court has ruled in a case contesting whether this provision violates the Constitution. The Grand Bench is a court in which all 15 justices of the Supreme Court sit together, convened for weighty cases such as constitutional rulings and changes to past precedents. A ruling of constitutional means that the court recognizes a legal provision as not contrary to the Constitution.

At issue was the system under which no one can vote while serving a sentence in a prison or similar facility. With this ruling, the Supreme Court has treated the provision as valid. However, the detailed reasoning behind the conclusion that it is constitutional, and whether the justices were divided in their opinions, cannot be confirmed in current reports. Had the provision been struck down, the question would have been whether the Diet would revise the law, but that did not happen. Whether debate over the provision will continue in the Diet and elsewhere is also not yet known.

Bill to Cut 31 Lower House Seats Enters Substantive Deliberations; Three Sponsoring Parties Aim to Pass It in This Diet Session

A bill to cut the statutory number of seats in the House of Representatives by 31 in proportional representation has entered substantive deliberations in a special committee of the House. The three parties that submitted the bill, the Liberal Democratic Party, Ishin and the Democratic Party for the People, stressed its significance and aim to pass it in this Diet session. The statutory number of seats is the number of members fixed by law. Proportional representation is an electoral system that allocates seats according to the votes each party receives. Entering substantive deliberations means the stage at which the parties begin questioning and stating positions that go into the substance of a submitted bill.

In response, other opposition parties voiced opposition to cutting seats in proportional representation alone. The opposition is against the bill, and criticism that it is “a diversion from the slush fund problem” was reportedly also heard. When a vote on the bill will be held, and whether amendments will be made, cannot be confirmed at present.

Education Minister Seki Admits Holding Another ¥90,000-a-Ticket Party in March 2024 With Officials Invited

Minister of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology Seki has revealed that bio-related companies attended a political fundraising party held at a Tokyo hotel in March 2024 and received policy briefings from officials of the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. The fee was 90,000 yen per person. On the 17th, Seki had said that a different political fundraising party held in June 2024 raised no legal problem. The latest explanation extends to that other gathering.

Seki's explanation is reported to be that the 90,000 yen fee does not count as a donation. The opposition Constitutional Democratic Party took issue with the 90,000-yen party tickets and is reported to have hinted that it is ready to file a censure motion against Seki. A censure motion is a procedure by which the Diet expresses its intention to hold a minister responsible. Whether there were other similar gatherings, and how many companies took part, cannot be confirmed at present.
