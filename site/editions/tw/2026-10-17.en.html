Waiver of National Health Insurance premiums for people 65 and over passes third reading; funding is the biggest dispute

The Kuomintang and the Taiwan People's Party joined forces to amend the Senior Citizens Welfare Act so that the government subsidizes the full health insurance premiums of older people whose individual income tax rate is below 20%; Democratic Progressive Party lawmakers question the lack of any account of the funding.

On the 16th the Legislative Yuan passed on third reading an amendment to the Senior Citizens Welfare Act. At its core: for people aged 65 and over whose individual income tax rate is below 20%, the government subsidizes their National Health Insurance premiums, and they no longer have to pay them themselves. The amendment was pushed jointly by Kuomintang and Taiwan People's Party lawmakers, and went to a vote after cross-party negotiations broke down.

Opposition centers on where the money will come from. The Democratic Progressive Party caucus said the amendment gives no account of the funding and pointed to a gap of NT$38.8 billion; the figure comes from the Democratic Progressive Party caucus, and this issue found no independent official estimate from any other source, so readers should treat it as one side's claim. Another outlet's headline said the measure could make each person pay about NT$3,000 more a year; this is a report's estimate and headline wording, likewise with no official figure behind it, and the details remain to be clarified.

The Minister of Health and Welfare has also responded publicly, but the material obtained for this issue is only a headline, so the argument cannot be fully relayed. Another former vice minister of health and welfare, Wang Pi-sheng, said on the 16th that he had heard young people say they “no longer want to pay health insurance premiums either,” and called this the beginning of the system's collapse. This is a personal observation and commentary, not survey data, but it points to the most sensitive spot in the system's design: health insurance runs on mutual aid, and once the boundary of what is free is widened, will people's willingness to pay waver as well?

Several things to watch next: whether the Executive Yuan seeks reconsideration or earmarks a separate budget; whether the subsidy is paid from the central budget or absorbed by the National Health Insurance fund; and whether a follow-up amendment or a petition for a constitutional interpretation emerges. With the year-end local elections close at hand, both parties have put this “welfare card” into the campaign context, and the attacks and counterattacks are expected to continue.

AI and semiconductor stocks keep sliding; Taiwan stocks close Friday down a sharp 3.11%

The TAIEX closed down 3.11% on the 16th at 47,111.26. A foreign wire roundup (CNBC, relayed by the Liberty Times) shows that from Monday to Thursday this week, the world's 20 largest AI and semiconductor companies lost about 32 trillion in market value over four trading days (the original report does not state the currency in this issue; please refer to the original), with South Korea's Samsung and SK hit hardest. The causes were put down to worries about further U.S. rate hikes, an overheated AI investment boom, and progress in Chinese memory technology. Another report mentioned that the Taiwan index futures night session swung violently, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index plunged, and TSMC's ADR was at one point down more than 5%; this figure so far appears in a single source only and is for reference. In the U.S., the S&P 500 closed down 1.09% on the 16th; earlier, on the 15th, the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed up more than 500 points as investors turned to names outside AI. European stocks closed higher on the 15th because of their lower exposure to the tech sector, and also rose on the 16th after Nvidia and Federal Reserve Chair Warsh stepped forward to reassure markets.

Chiang Wan-an announces looser urban renewal threshold; consent for self-initiated consolidation cut to 50%

Taipei Mayor Chiang Wan-an said on the 16th that the city government will ease the thresholds for urban renewal: the first-stage consent needed for self-initiated consolidation falls from 75% to 50%, so once more than half of the households are willing, the city will step in to help with feasibility calculations; for large-area urban renewal projects, the base floor area ratio rises from 225% to 300%. This is what two outlets both reported.

The aim is to make it easier to start rebuilding in old neighborhoods stuck on high consent thresholds, but a higher floor area ratio also involves surrounding traffic, public facilities and existing residents' rights; when it will take effect and whether the self-government ordinance must be amended are not explained in this issue's material. With the year-end Taipei mayoral race entering its countdown, urban renewal has long been a housing issue voters care about, and how each camp responds is worth watching.

Tainan railway undergrounding opens today; surface tracks become history

The first phase of Tainan's railway undergrounding formally opened on the 17th. According to the Liberty Times, after the last southbound train left the old station at about 10 p.m. on the 16th, the construction team used the few late-night hours with trains halted to cut and seal off the power, cut and join the rails, shift the track, backfill ballast, tamp it and test the signals; the track at the north and south ends was switched over in a single night.

Mayor Chen Shih-kai said this is not the end point, meaning follow-up works and integration with the surrounding city remain to be handled. The project took 17 years to push through; once the line opens, the barrier that at-grade crossings posed in the urban area will gradually disappear, but this issue's material gives no complete follow-up timeline and does not touch on the cost.

Kuomintang Central Committee member launches petition to expel Jaw Shaw-kong; party headquarters: “not pleased to see it”

Kuomintang Central Committee member Chiang Yung-hsiung has launched a petition asking the party's Discipline Committee to expel former Broadcasting Corporation of China chairman Jaw Shaw-kong from the party, on the grounds that Jaw twice interviewed Puma Shen, the Democratic Progressive Party's Taipei mayoral candidate. Chen Yi-hsin, head of the Kuomintang's Culture and Communications Committee, responded that party headquarters is not pleased to see internal strife arise from this, and even less wants to stumble over its own feet at a critical moment in the campaign. Another report dug into Chiang Yung-hsiung's background, saying he has commemorated Mao Zedong and congratulated China on its National Day, and that he was elected to the Central Committee as a party delegate representing Taiwanese businesspeople in China; this is the account of a single outlet.

The same day, the Central Election Commission announced that, because a sentence has become final and probation has not been fully carried out, Wang Yu-min's director Ho Po-lun (何博倫) may not register as a candidate, as reported by several outlets. Separately, Australian Defence Force chief Hammond (韓蒙德) told Kyodo News in an interview that Australia will watch China's moves around Taiwan “extremely closely”; Palau President Surangel Whipps Jr., whose country has diplomatic ties with Taiwan, also told Politico that small nations must join hands to curb China's assertive actions in the South Pacific. South Korea's Ministry of National Defense, for its part, clarified that widening the operating area of its Gulf of Aden unit is not sending troops to the Strait of Hormuz.
