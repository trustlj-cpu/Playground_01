Stelco's U.S. owner says it will fight Ottawa in court over the Hamilton layoffs as a federal deadline runs out

Cleveland-Cliffs blames U.S. and Canadian tariffs. Ottawa has threatened to ask a court to enforce the job promises made when it bought Stelco.

The chief executive of Cleveland-Cliffs, the Ohio steelmaker that owns Stelco, said on Sunday that he is prepared to fight Canada in court over the planned layoffs at Stelco's Hamilton plant, CBC and Radio-Canada reported. Stelco has said it will idle its Hamilton operations indefinitely and concentrate production at its Lake Erie Works in Nanticoke, Ont. The wind-down began on Friday, October 9, and affects up to 500 workers, according to The Hub and other outlets earlier this month; the union has estimated that about 350 steelworkers will lose their jobs. Lourenco Goncalves, the chief executive, has blamed the loss of access to the U.S. market under President Donald Trump's tariffs and Canada's retaliatory tariffs, and has said the dispute will end up in court, according to the trade press and BNN Bloomberg. Earlier reporting quoted him as saying that until access to the U.S. market is restored there is "nothing we can do." CBC, The Globe and Mail and Radio-Canada all reported over the weekend that Stelco is proceeding with the layoffs despite Ottawa's threat of legal action. The idling does not close the company: Stelco keeps operating in Nanticoke.

Ottawa's argument rests on the 2024 takeover. Cleveland-Cliffs bought Stelco under the Investment Canada Act, the federal law that reviews foreign purchases of Canadian companies, and promised to keep all unionized jobs and most non-union ones. On October 5, Industry Minister Mélanie Joly gave the company five business days to present a plan to protect those jobs or face an application to a superior court, BNN Bloomberg, CTV News and CP24 reported. A judge could be asked for compliance orders, a forced sale or financial penalties. On Wednesday Ms. Joly said Ottawa had not yet received a response. We saw no reply since then and no court filing, and the exact last day depends on whether the Thanksgiving holiday counts as a business day, so we treat the deadline as falling this week. In 2009 Ottawa sued U.S. Steel over similar promises and dropped the case after the company agreed to keep both plants running, one outlet recalled. In DailyDrop's reading, three outcomes are possible: Cleveland-Cliffs presents a plan that Ottawa accepts, which would end the matter quietly; the two sides negotiate a sale or a revised commitment; or Ottawa files and the case moves to a court, where timelines are measured in months, not days. We saw no sign of any of the three as of early Monday. The Thanksgiving long weekend slows federal offices and courts, so a quick filing is unlikely before Tuesday. For Hamilton families this is a story about paycheques; for Ottawa it is a test of whether takeover pledges can be enforced when a trade war changes the economics.

Carney says the U.S.–Russia diesel deal hurts Ukraine's peace cause, but backs an "energy ceasefire"

Prime Minister Mark Carney told President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in a call on Sunday that the deal between Washington and Moscow on Russian diesel works against peace in Ukraine, according to the Prime Minister's Office as relayed by CTV News, Radio-Canada, La Presse and the Journal de Montréal. The Globe and Mail said he also applauded Mr. Trump's efforts toward a ceasefire, and La Presse and TVA Nouvelles reported that in a separate call with Mr. Trump he said he supports an energy ceasefire between Kyiv and Moscow. In Washington, several senators, including Thom Tillis, said the diesel plan may be illegal and urged Mr. Trump to reverse it, CTV News and Yahoo reported. We did not see the legal basis they cited or any White House reply.

The pairing is deliberate: Ottawa criticizes the sanctions waiver, not the peace effort. Canada has its own Russia sanctions, so watch whether Mr. Carney's words become action.

Trump announces a Russia–Ukraine "energy ceasefire". Russia then strikes Ukrainian power plants

President Donald Trump said on Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an "energy ceasefire," a halt to attacks on each other's power and fuel facilities, effective immediately, according to Al Jazeera, Euronews, CTV News, Radio-Canada, The Globe and Mail and others. He gave few details. Mr. Zelenskyy said Ukraine supports the idea and was awaiting details from Washington, Global News and Politico reported, and Reuters said Ukraine would stop striking Russian refineries if Russia stopped its energy attacks. Live coverage from French-language outlets noted that Moscow and Kyiv had not confirmed the deal.

Hours later, the National Post and others reported, Russian strikes hit Ukrainian power plants, and Mr. Zelenskyy said Russia had hit energy sites after the announcement. Reuters and the French press also reported that Mr. Zelenskyy said he would organize elections if Russia agrees to a 60-day total ceasefire; we did not see Russia's answer.

Riyadh's main airport is hit again a day after a deadly strike. Canada advises against travel to the airport

A passenger terminal at King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh was struck again on Sunday, a day after an attack that killed 12 people, Global News, CTV News and CBC reported. The Saudi-led coalition said a projectile hit the terminal; the National Post called it another Houthi strike. Saudi Arabia extended the airport's closure and moved Riyadh schools online, The Globe and Mail and Global News said. CTV News reported that Saudi authorities also said Dammam's King Fahd International Airport was attacked, with few details. Radio-Canada said the Houthis, the Iran-aligned Yemeni rebel movement, renewed threats against Saudi airports.

Global News reported that Canada advised against travel to the Riyadh airport. Saturday's toll is not settled: most outlets said 12 dead, while a French-language report said at least 13 and more than 300 injured. FinancialJuice relayed a Saudi civil aviation statement that one person died and 11 were wounded in Sunday's assault; that figure comes from a single outlet. A separate FinancialJuice headline said the Houthis claimed responsibility for an assault on the airport, and we could not tell from it which strike it meant.

The TSX is closed today for Thanksgiving. Friday: TSX +1.5%, Wall Street up, oil near US$92

The Toronto Stock Exchange, TSX Venture Exchange and the Montréal Exchange are closed on Monday for Thanksgiving and reopen on Tuesday, October 13, TMX Group said. Friday's S&P/TSX Composite close was 35,664.62, up 1.48%, according to DailyDrop's quote feed; The Canadian Press and Yahoo Finance reported a gain of more than 500 points that day. We could not confirm Friday's USD/CAD close from a second source, so that figure is left blank. U.S. stock markets are open on Monday, though the U.S. bond market is closed for Columbus Day. On Friday the S&P 500 rose 0.59% to 7,811.54, the Nasdaq 0.64% to 27,366.17 and the Dow 0.83% to 51,654.95. WTI crude ended at US$91.85 (+0.39%) and gold at US$4,216.3 (+1.43%).

Oil is the number to watch. The DailyDrop feed showed WTI near US$90.31, down about 1.7%, in early Monday trading; we have only this one source. Riyadh airport strikes and the Ukraine energy ceasefire both feed into crude, and Canadian energy shares will react when the TSX reopens Tuesday. The National Post also reported that Mr. Trump's tariffs and the trade war with Canada are driving up costs for U.S. manufacturers.
