Quebec's premier-designate says an independence vote is no early priority. Carney tells Quebec to focus on the economy

A day after the Parti Québécois won a minority, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon played down the referendum, Prime Minister Mark Carney called him, and the defeated premier quit as leader of her party.

The morning after, the tone from Quebec City was careful. Paul St-Pierre Plamondon told reporters that an independence vote will not be an early priority for his incoming minority government, according to the Globe and Mail and the Toronto Star. The PQ won 59 of the 127 seats in the National Assembly on Monday, five short of a majority, and he has said any referendum would wait until Donald Trump leaves the White House in January 2029. His government will need opposition votes to pass a budget, let alone to hold a vote on leaving Canada. The Liberals under Charles Milliard are now the official opposition.

Prime Minister Mark Carney spoke with the premier-designate on Tuesday morning. Afterwards he told reporters that Canada is at a crucial time and that Quebec and the rest of the country should focus on housing, growth, infrastructure and workers hit by American tariffs. "This is not a time to wait for something a few years down the road," he said, according to The Canadian Press and the Associated Press. His Quebec lieutenant, Joël Lightbound, put the constitutional point more bluntly: a mandate for separation has to go through a referendum, and Monday was an election, not a referendum. The Toronto Star reported that Mr. Carney also argued that his own national mandate is larger than the PQ's, and that his government remains optimistic about the high-speed rail link between Toronto and Quebec City despite the result.

The new government's early agenda will still test Ottawa. Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon promised during the campaign to break with the monarchy, and CTV News reported he has already taken a first step toward abolishing it in Quebec. Alberta separatists, meanwhile, told Global News and the Star that they see an ally in him. Mrs. Fréchette announced Tuesday that she is leaving the CAQ leadership. The Bloc Québécois leader said he is very proud of the PQ win, according to CBC. No party has said how it would vote on a confidence motion.

Ottawa gives Stelco's U.S. owner five days to explain 500 layoffs, or face possible legal action

Industry Minister Mélanie Joly has written to Stelco's president demanding a plan within five days to comply with the commitments Cleveland-Cliffs made when it bought the Hamilton steelmaker. The Globe and Mail, Global News, CTV News, CP24 and BNN Bloomberg all reported the ultimatum on Tuesday. Stelco announced on Sept. 28 that up to 500 workers in Hamilton and Nanticoke would lose work as American tariffs idled lines. The layoffs take effect Oct. 9 in Hamilton and Oct. 24 at Nanticoke. The United Steelworkers, which represents the plants, estimates 350 Hamilton workers are affected and calls the company's trade-war explanation disingenuous.

Ms. Joly says Canada's approval of the takeover depended on the new owner keeping union jobs and most non-union positions, and that those conditions do not lapse because market conditions changed. Cleveland-Cliffs chief executive Lourenço Goncalves says his company has every legal right to idle the plant and expects the dispute to end up in court. The company also says many of the affected workers can be absorbed at its Lake Erie Works in the United States, a claim the union disputes.

A Washington State man is charged with conspiring with the Tumbler Ridge school shooter. Prosecutors say he offered advice and about $100

Authorities in the United States announced on Tuesday that James Cody Bryant, 30, of the Bellingham area, was arrested and charged with conspiracy to murder persons in a foreign country, in connection with the Feb. 10 school shooting in Tumbler Ridge, B.C. The charge is an allegation. Acting U.S. Attorney Neil Floyd said Mr. Bryant was in repeated contact with the 18-year-old shooter, offering advice and sending money, a little over $100 according to Mr. Floyd, mainly for a lighter and shoes. An affidavit quotes Discord chats between the two, who prosecutors say met on Omegle years earlier. It alleges he gave tactical advice, agreed to broadcast the attack and watched video footage as it unfolded.

The complaint also says the two discussed sending weapons, which Mr. Bryant declined to do. The FBI credited the RCMP; an RCMP assistant commissioner said his actions "helped facilitate" the attack, according to CBC. B.C.'s attorney general reacted on Tuesday night. Reports differ on the toll and on the livestream platform discussed. The first court appearance was set for Seattle.

Emera agrees to buy Canadian Utilities in an all-stock deal worth $14.3 billion and create a $72-billion utility

Emera and Canadian Utilities, with their controlling shareholder ATCO, announced a merger that would create what they call a Canadian utility and energy-infrastructure powerhouse. Emera would acquire Canadian Utilities for about $14.3 billion in shares, and the combined company would have an enterprise value near $72 billion. Headlines varied on the dollar figure; those are the numbers in the company announcement and filing. Class A shareholders of Canadian Utilities, other than ATCO, would receive 0.755 Emera shares per share, and Class B holders 0.819. The exchange ratio for Class A shares is worth, at Emera's Monday close, about $51.57 per Class A share, roughly 0.7% above Canadian Utilities' $51.20 close.

ATCO would spin off its industrial-services business into a new public company, New ATCO, and ATCO shareholders would receive Emera shares plus one share of the spin-off. Emera holders would own about 60% of the combined company and Canadian Utilities holders about 40%. It would be led by Emera chief executive Scott Balfour. Closing is expected in the third or fourth quarter of 2027, and ATCO's controlling shareholder has signed a voting support agreement. ATCO shares were among the day's strongest on the TSX.

Foreign-influence watchdog contacts Alberta separatist groups 13 days before the referendum

Canada's foreign influence transparency commissioner said his office has contacted Alberta separatist groups, the Toronto Star, the Globe and Mail and CBC reported on Tuesday. The Globe's story concerns a group that has met State Department officials in Washington; its leader says the watchdog reached out. CBC reported the commissioner's view that past separatist meetings in the U.S. do not fall under the new foreign-influence registry. That registry has drawn just 30 registrations, Global News reported. A State Department spokesperson earlier called such contacts routine and said no commitments were made.

Ottawa has no formal role in the Oct. 19 vote, which is run by Elections Alberta under provincial law. Global News reported on Tuesday that thousands of mail-in ballots were invalid because of errors; we have not seen a second outlet or an Elections Alberta count and treat that figure as unconfirmed. Earlier this autumn the agency said about 7,200 special-ballot applications were held up by elector errors and that 313 packages went out without ballots, with replacements sent. More than 290,000 special ballots were requested, nearly five times the 2023 number.
