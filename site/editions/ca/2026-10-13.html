Politics · AlbertaPress ✓ 9 outlets · the prime minister's words come from his Thanksgiving message

A week before Alberta votes on leaving Canada, Carney tells Albertans the country is stronger together

His Thanksgiving message was aimed squarely at the province. Both campaigns are now in the final stretch, and advance voting is about to open.

Prime Minister Mark Carney used his Thanksgiving message on Monday to speak directly to Albertans, a week before they vote in the referendum on leaving Canada. CBC, CTV News, The Globe and Mail, the National Post, Al Jazeera, Radio-Canada and La Presse all carried it, and together they give its outline. He urged Albertans to choose prosperity, argued that Canada is stronger together than apart and, in Radio-Canada's French rendering, said that "Canada works." The National Post reported that the message came ahead of a visit he is expected to make to the province. Radio-Canada also reported that British Columbia Premier David Eby told listeners that his province and Alberta are "stronger together"; that is a single-outlet report. The Globe and Mail and Radio-Canada described the two campaigns as entering their final stretch, with speeches and rallies on both sides, and Global News ran a story headed "Advance voting opens as experts debate meaning of Alberta referendum results."

Two things are worth keeping apart. The first is what a prime minister's message can do. It is persuasion, not a rule: the outcome rests with Alberta voters, and the headlines we read do not say what would follow in law after a yes. For Quebec, Parliament's answer to that kind of question was the Clarity Act of 2000, passed after the 1995 referendum there. Nothing we read says whether it would apply to Alberta, and we will not guess. The second is that the vote is already echoing outside the province. The Globe and Mail reported that Quebec businesses are bracing for a renewed sovereignty debate, and La Presse published a piece on what it called Alberta's strange sovereigntist debate. This comes as the Parti Québécois, which has just won the Quebec election, prepares to govern; Le Devoir reported that its leader, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon (PSPP), and Christine Fréchette were to discuss the government transition on Tuesday. Our Monday edition reported that Premier Danielle Smith has ruled out a second vote if Albertans choose to stay, per CBC and Radio-Canada, and that former premier Jason Kenney warned low turnout could favour the separatists, per CTV News. In DailyDrop's reading, those two points explain the tone of Ottawa's message: if a second vote is off the table, a no has to be decisive, and turnout may matter more than persuasion at the margin. A Thanksgiving message is also a deliberate choice of platform, because it reaches people who are not following the campaign, which is exactly the audience a turnout argument needs. What we can say with confidence is that the campaign is in its last week, advance polls are opening, and Ottawa has chosen to make a public argument for staying. What we cannot yet say is how the vote is leaning: we saw no poll, and the experts Global News cites are still debating what a result would mean.

U.K. says Iran's Revolutionary Guards almost certainly directed a plot against a U.S.-run air base. Canada signs a joint statement

U.K. Prime Minister Burnham said on Monday that a security incident at RAF Fairford, an air base in England run by the United States, was almost certainly directed by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, according to Reuters, CTV News, Global News, Castanet and the National Post. Reuters' headline was a notch more cautious ("likely"). Canadian headlines called it a terror plot, an attack attempt and, in The Globe and Mail, an "apparent" plot. The headlines we read do not describe the plot itself, and we did not see Iran's answer.

Canada was among the governments that signed a joint statement on Iran on Monday, which the Government of Canada's news centre posted; CBC headlined it as Canada and U.K. allies vowing action, and Castanet as a denunciation of Iran. We saw only the statement's title, so we cannot say what action is promised or who else signed. On a separate track, Reuters reported that Iran will reply to the U.S. response on its Hormuz proposal once its review is done, and that President Trump said he does not believe China and Russia are helping Iran "at all." Both are single-outlet reports. Separately, the Gulf risk now showing up in oil prices comes from Houthi strikes on Riyadh's airport; the Houthis are Iran-aligned, but nothing we read links the two episodes.

Health · MontréalPress ✓ 10 outlets · the exposure dates are in the health notice, not in the headlines we read

Montreal public health warns of measles exposure at Trudeau airport and the Sainte-Justine hospital

Montreal public health said a person with contagious measles had been at Montréal-Trudeau International Airport, and that people may also have been exposed at the Sainte-Justine pediatric hospital, according to the Montreal Gazette, CBC, CTV News, CityNews Montreal, Global News and Radio-Canada. La Presse, TVA Nouvelles and the Journal de Montréal described it as a new case in the city, and Le Devoir said the same. The headlines we read do not give the dates, times or flights involved. Those details sit in the public-health notice, which is the document anyone who passed through either place should check.

Measles spreads through the air, and general public-health guidance is that the virus can hang in a room for up to two hours after an infected person has left. Symptoms usually start with fever, cough and red eyes, and the rash follows a few days later; they can show up anywhere from about a week to three weeks after exposure. Two doses of the vaccine give strong protection, and anyone unsure of their status can check their immunization record or call their clinic before visiting one, so as not to expose waiting rooms. DailyDrop's reading: the useful fact here is the notice, not the alarm. It names places and, in the full text, times, so that people who were there can judge their own risk. We saw no report of a second case linked to this one. Separately, TVA Nouvelles carried a story about an Ebola suspicion in Longueuil, which we cover under unverified claims.

Markets · AsiaPress ✓ 7 outlets · Monday's U.S. closing levels not confirmed by two sources

Chip stocks tumble across Asia, with Korea's Kospi down more than 5%, as the IMF flags AI and bond-yield risks

Asian markets sold off overnight as worries about the AI boom hit semiconductor shares. AP reported that South Korea's Kospi index fell by more than 5%. Reuters said Asian stocks dived on "AI jitters" ahead of U.S. bank earnings, and CNBC reported that SK Hynix and SoftBank shares plunged as Asian tech stocks tracked a chip sell-off on Wall Street. Investing.com listed Samsung and SK Hynix among the fallers, citing fears of Chinese competition, and Bloomberg tied the drop in chipmakers to a breakthrough by China's CXMT, a memory-chip maker; we did not see details of the advance. Reuters also reported that Nvidia's share of China's AI-chip market is slipping as Huawei closes the gap. We could not confirm Monday's U.S. closing levels from two sources. AP's early report on Monday had U.S. stocks near a record in mixed trading, before the chip sell-off that CNBC describes.

The International Monetary Fund supplied the backdrop. Reuters said it cited a possible AI slowdown and spiking bond yields as looming risks to global markets, while also saying AI investment is keeping world growth around 3%; CNBC said the fund expects the global economy to slow less than feared in 2026 because the AI boom offsets the hit from wars. Reuters' Morning Bid column was headed "The fragile AI trade," a Bank of America poll found investors more exposed to tech stocks than at any time since 2020, according to Bloomberg, and Reuters reported that Broadcom's chief executive says the biggest hurdle to AI-driven growth is capital, not memory chips. For Canadians, the TSX reopens today after the Thanksgiving closure and has to absorb all of this at once. U.S. banks are about to start reporting earnings, which will show how higher yields are treating lenders, Reuters and MarketWatch said.

World · Ukraine and energyPress ✓ 11 outlets · Moscow has not confirmed the truce in what we read; some reports call the drones' origin unknown

Kyiv says Russian strikes go on despite the energy truce, a ship sinks off Bulgaria, and the diesel deal draws fire in Washington

The Associated Press, Global News, La Presse and Radio-Canada reported that Kyiv says Russian strikes on its energy sites continue despite President Trump's announcement of an "energy ceasefire." Reuters said Ukraine accuses Russia of violating it on the day the European Union adopted nearly 1,600 new sanctions listings against Russia, and a cluster of reports said the EU has not yet seen Russia comply. The Kremlin rejected President Zelenskyy's idea of a 60-day ceasefire and said it wants an overall peace deal, Reuters reported. In Washington, Reuters said Mr. Trump defended the Russian diesel deal as lawmakers from both parties complained, and its sources said the U.S. hopes the deal can "soften" Moscow on peace talks. Russia says it could export up to 3.5 million tonnes of diesel a month by the end of the year, Reuters reported. Mr. Carney has criticized the deal while thanking Mr. Trump for his ceasefire efforts, The Globe and Mail reported.

Oil eased overnight as traders weighed that supply against Gulf risks, CNBC and Reuters said. Those risks include the Houthi strikes on Riyadh's airport: Reuters reported that airlines cancelled flights to Saudi Arabia and that the Saudi warning system stayed silent before the deadly attack, and the National Post reported a Saudi prince promising an "equally serious" response. Bloomberg's video desk said a wider conflict could push oil to US$120, a scenario rather than a forecast. Overnight, the Black Sea added another front. Reuters, CBC, the BBC and The Guardian reported that drones hit ships off Bulgaria's coast and at least one ship sank. The BBC said Bulgaria's prime minister summoned Russia's ambassador, while The Guardian described the drones as being of unknown origin. FinancialJuice relayed European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen calling the Black Sea "another front of escalation" and "unacceptable" (single outlet). NATO commander Grynkewich warned Russia that an attack would be "utterly futile," Reuters reported.
