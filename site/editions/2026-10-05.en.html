"North Korea planted the mine."
The military has its answer. What comes next is the problem.

Joint Chiefs release final findings on the DMZ blast. "Buried, not washed downstream." Seoul demands an apology and launches a mine-clearing operation the same afternoon.

The explosion that injured three soldiers in the western sector of the Demilitarized Zone on Sept. 21 was caused by a mine North Korea buried south of the Military Demarcation Line, the military concluded on Monday. The Joint Chiefs of Staff (합동참모본부) released its final findings and cited three pieces of evidence: the accounts of soldiers at the scene, the depth at which the recovered mine had been buried, and a National Forensic Service analysis showing that fragments taken from the wounded matched North Korean-made mines recovered from the Imjin River in the past.

The military demanded an apology from the North and, at 12:51 p.m. the same day, began an operation to clear North Korean mines south of the Military Demarcation Line. Days earlier, Pyongyang had denied responsibility and warned of retaliation. A mine that "washed downstream" and a mine that was "planted" are very different things. The first is an accident. The second is a violation of the Armistice Agreement.

So why does my bank balance look the same?

The Pacific is boiling. A 75% chance of the strongest El Niño on record

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) puts the odds at 75% that the El Niño of October through December will be the strongest since observations began in 1950. The chance of a "very strong" El Niño is above 90%. For this point in the year it is already the strongest on record, and the outlook has it lasting into early 2027. The forecast was issued on Sept. 10 and was picked up again in a big way by the American press this week.

El Niño is the remote control for the world's weather. Floods in the American South, drought in Australia and Southeast Asia, and for Korea, usually a warm and wet winter. Global commodity prices move first: grains, coffee, sugar, palm oil. The 75% is the probability that ocean temperatures break the record, not the probability of a disaster in Korea.
