After Jensen Huang’s resignation, Nvidia reaffirms its pledge to supply 260,000 GPUs to Korea

The Ministry of Science and ICT held a review meeting with Nvidia on the 13th, and Deputy Prime Minister Bae Kyung-hoon said supply and cooperation will continue as previously announced.

On the 12th (US local time), Nvidia announced the resignation of chief executive officer (CEO) Jensen Huang and the appointment of Colette Kress, chief financial officer (CFO), as interim CEO. The board has begun the process of finding a successor. Allegations tied to private life and to involvement with Chinese intelligence were raised over the background to the resignation, but these allegations are not facts confirmed by an investigation or official probe, and it is also unconfirmed whether the company gave them as the reason for the resignation. On the 13th the KOSPI rose as much as 3.03% intraday to 5,504.44 before closing down 0.98% at 5,290.53. Democratic members of the US Senate and House said on the 12th that they would investigate the approval process under the export controls on China-bound sales of Nvidia’s high-performance chips.

On the morning of the 13th, the Korean government held an issue-review meeting with Nvidia, chaired by the Second Vice Minister of the Ministry of Science and ICT. Deputy Prime Minister and Science and ICT Minister Bae Kyung-hoon said on Facebook that day that Nvidia had reaffirmed its plan to pursue the supply of 260,000 advanced GPUs (AI computing chips) and cooperation with Korean companies as previously announced. The 260,000 is not an actual chip count but a figure converted into the computing power of a chip called the Nvidia B200. Of the roughly 35,000 that the government is to secure directly, about 21,000 have arrived, and the remaining roughly 14,000 will arrive by early 2027. The task of linking Korean-made AI chips with the Nvidia platform, and efforts to hold Nvidia’s developer event GTC in Korea, also continue as before. Who the permanent successor CEO will be has not yet been announced.

World · Aviation✓ 4 outlets · detailed cause of the failure and number of cancelled flights unconfirmed

US flights briefly grounded nationwide… some Incheon–US flights cancelled, back to normal on the morning of the 14th

The US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) halted aircraft departures at all airports nationwide at around 4 p.m. on the 13th (US Eastern time; around 6 a.m. on the 14th, Korea time), citing an air traffic control communications failure. It was the first nationwide halt of departures since January 2023. The halt was lifted on the morning of the 14th, Korea time, and reports are conflicting on how long it took, at 4–5 hours. Reportedly, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy explained that the cause was switches and routers (data-routing equipment) freezing from overload and that communications and radar were not completely cut off. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there is currently no evidence of a cyberattack or the like. The fallout reached Korea too. Some US-bound flights due to leave Incheon Airport on the morning of the 14th were processed as cancelled, then the cancellations were reversed once the halt was lifted, and Korean Air and Asiana Airlines operations moved toward normal. Figures for cancelled and delayed flights have not been tallied, and delays continued within the US. How far US authorities will disclose the exact cause of the failure and measures to prevent a recurrence has not yet emerged.

‘Fabricated-prosecution special counsel law’: 44% in favor, 37% against, about 20% undecided… Han Dong-hoon’s return to the party opposed by 40%

In a Gallup Korea survey of 1,002 voters nationwide on the 10th–12th, 44% favored the so-called ‘fabricated-prosecution special counsel law’ passed by the National Assembly, 37% opposed it, and the remaining roughly 20% were undecided. Put to 100 people, that means 44 in favor, 37 against and about 20 putting off a judgment. On Han Dong-hoon’s return to the party, 29% were in favor and 40% opposed. The figures for presidential job approval and party support are from the same survey, as reported in yesterday’s evening edition.

The law is a matter on which the ruling and opposition parties clash, and the People Power Party is demanding that the president use the veto. Whether the president will use the veto has not yet been disclosed. Because it is a sample survey, it carries a margin of error, and these numbers are not what the whole public thinks. Methods and timing differ from pollster to pollster, so the figures are hard to compare directly with those of other pollsters.

China approves Eli Lilly’s first ‘oral obesity pill’… a once-daily tablet

China’s National Medical Products Administration approved Eli Lilly’s oral obesity treatment orforglipron. The approval came on the 12th (local time), and it was classified as a Class 1 new drug. Its Chinese brand name is ‘Mu You Da’. GLP-1 is a gut hormone that regulates appetite and blood sugar, and drugs of this class mimic the hormone to reduce weight. The drug is a pill taken once a day, with or without meals. It is for adults who are obese or overweight and have at least one weight-related condition, to be used alongside a calorie-restricted diet and exercise.

The GLP-1-class weight-management drugs approved in China until now were injectables, so the first approval of an oral drug drew market attention. Rival Novo Nordisk’s oral obesity drug is known to be under review in China. Approval and launch timing in Korea, and the sales price in China, have not been confirmed.

Society · Trial✓ 2 outlets · sentencing date and next hearing date unconfirmed

Yoon Suk Yeol’s first-instance trial for abuse of power opens… special counsel sees end of insurrection as impeachment vote, former president denies charges

The first hearing in the first-instance trial of former President Yoon Suk Yeol on charges of obstruction of the exercise of rights through abuse of authority was held on the 13th in Criminal Collegiate Division 38-1 of the Seoul Central District Court. The special counsel indicted Yoon in August, holding that right after the Dec. 3 emergency martial law, Yoon instructed the National Security Office and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to convey messages explaining the justification of martial law to the US, UK, Japan, the EU and others. Yoon’s side denied the charges in full, arguing that there was no specific instruction and that no order was ever given to convey the message to the UK, Japan and others. All of this is the charges as indicted and the defense’s claims, not facts found by the court.

The dispute turned to when the insurrection ended. The comprehensive special counsel regards the end of the insurrection acts not as the lifting of martial law but as the passage of the presidential impeachment motion. Yoon rebutted: “It is hard to understand how an insurrection could continue after the martial law situation was fully lifted.” The court noted that this trial is about whether abuse of authority is established, not about whether conveying the messages was an act of insurrection. The court said explaining the intent of martial law to allied countries appears somewhat unavoidable, and told the special counsel to identify the parts that went beyond routine diplomatic work and supplement the indictment.
