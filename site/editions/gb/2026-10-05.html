Burnham scraps the plan to curb jury trials. Judges would sit alone only in some fraud cases

The U-turn reverses a Starmer-era reform meant to cut the court backlog. Lawyers cheered, but they say a "severe threat" to jury trial remains because magistrates' sentencing powers would still double.

The government has dropped its plan to take juries out of many Crown Court trials. Announced over the weekend, the change reverses a reform drawn up under the previous prime minister, Sir Keir Starmer, and first set out by David Lammy as Justice Secretary. That plan would have kept juries for the most serious offences and for those either-way offences likely to bring a sentence of more than three years, and sent everything else to a single judge. Under the revised approach, judge-alone trials would apply only to some fraud and financial cases, the Ministry of Justice told the BBC. Justice Secretary and Lord Chancellor Alex Norris, who took the post when Andy Burnham became prime minister in July, said he had "changed course" after hearing the strength of feeling against the idea. Reports said Mr Burnham had come to power facing a possible rebellion of dozens of Labour backbenchers on the issue, and the barrister-MP Karl Turner, whose Labour whip was suspended after he criticised the plan, welcomed the reversal. It was not a surprise to close followers: the Law Gazette had reported that Mr Burnham was hinting at dumping the curbs, and City AM that he was under pressure from the legal profession to safeguard jury trials. Several outlets described the decision as another reversal of a Starmer-era policy. Mr Norris insisted that the court backlog, which stood at 80,829 cases at the end of June, would still start to fall "in this Parliament", though he would not put a figure on it. He argued that legislating for judge-alone trials was the hardest part of the package to deliver and would have slowed the rest of it. Over the next decade, he said, the queue would fall significantly. The campaign group Defend Our Juries called the reversal "a step in the right direction".

Lawyers welcomed it too, but not the whole package. The courts Bill still removes a defendant's right to choose a Crown Court trial in some cases and doubles magistrates' maximum sentencing power to two years. The Criminal Bar Association says that could move around 25,000 cases a year, among them assaults and sexual offences, to volunteer magistrates it calls "completely unequipped" to try them. Its chair, Andrew Thomas KC, said barristers were "delighted" but that a "severe threat" to trial by jury remains in the Bill. The Law Society called the new position "common-sense", yet warned that magistrates' courts also face record backlogs and need more capacity. The Bar Council's chair, Kirsty Brimelow KC, called it "a significant and welcome move". One number is still missing: ministers will set the sentencing limit only once the Bill is enacted, and that figure will decide how many cases leave the jury system.

Business · TelecomsPress ✓ 5 outlets · cost figures as stated by BT

BT buys TalkTalk out of administration for about £400 million, with ministers speeding the clearance

TalkTalk went into administration on Monday and was sold to BT the same day, in a rescue that protects about 2.5 million customers and roughly 900 jobs, according to the administrators and Reuters. BT puts the total cash cost in its 2027 financial year at about £400 million, covering the price and other cash effects, and says its financial outlook is unchanged. The figure includes payments that Openreach, BT's network arm, would otherwise have received. Chief executive Allison Kirkby said BT "stepped in" because of the risk to the country and to public services had the company collapsed. BT shares rose by roughly 1% to 2% in London, depending on the hour and the outlet. The deal is not final. Ofcom and the Competition and Markets Authority will review it over the coming weeks, and the two firms will run separately and keep competing until then. Ministers moved to speed up that process. Virgin Media called it "a stitch-up masked as a rescue deal in the public interest". BT already holds about 30% of the UK broadband market.

Gilt yields reach levels last seen in 1998 and the FTSE 100 has its worst week since April. Healey's Budget is 23 days away

The yield on the UK 30-year gilt rose above 6% on 1 October for the first time since 1998, City AM reported, and an FXStreet market note puts the crossing on the same day. It is what the government pays to borrow for three decades, and it set the tone for last week: the FTSE 100 fell 2.2%, its worst week since April, as bond yields surged around the world. London steadied on Monday. The index closed up 0.34% at 10,497.94, helped by energy and mining shares, and did better than Paris and Frankfurt. Sterling held just above $1.32 and sat near its strongest level of the year against the euro, FXStreet said, partly because French bond-market stress makes British assets look the safer European choice.

The pressure lands on Chancellor John Healey, who presents his Budget on 28 October and is also being pressed to find money for defence and for immediate help for families, City AM noted. Brit Brief argued that higher borrowing costs raise the risk of tax rises or spending cuts. The Bank of England decides on interest rates soon after the Budget. The UK calendar has no major data releases this week, only Bank speeches, so gilts are trading on what happens to bonds elsewhere.

Security · DefencePress ✓ 6 outlets · links to Iran are claims by officials, not findings

US bombers leave RAF Fairford after a suspected plot. Washington cites 'threats'; Rubio calls it routine

All 12 US B-1B Lancer bombers left the Gloucestershire base over the weekend, CNN and others reported. It was a week after three vans were stopped near the base on 27 September; police found petrol but no explosives. Six people have been arrested so far, GB News reports, the latest a 25-year-old British-Iranian dual national held in London on 1 October. Accounts differ on how many of the earlier arrests ended in bail. President Trump said "threats" prompted the move and that the people behind them "would be linked to Iran".

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said Iran "played a part" but has given no details. Tehran denies any plot and summoned the British ambassador in Tehran. Secretary of State Marco Rubio rejected a direct link to the Fairford case, calling the move a routine rotation of assets and saying he has confidence in base security. Channel 4 News pointed out the mixed messages, and Iran has said bases used to attack it are legitimate targets. Fairford has been central to the American air campaign.

World · Iran warPress ✓ 7 outlets · battlefield and negotiating claims come from the parties

Iran says Hormuz stays shut until Washington accepts its seven-day plan. Trump says a decision is coming, 'the easy way or the hard way'

Iran's plan would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within a week if the United States releases frozen Iranian funds, lifts oil sanctions and ends its naval blockade of Iranian ports. President Trump rejected it on 26 September. On Saturday he said "the only question" was whether it would be "the easy way or the hard way", without saying what he meant. On Sunday Iran's chief negotiator, who is also the parliament speaker, said the strait would stay closed until Washington accepts the plan.

Axios and CBS reported that senior officials met at Camp David on Friday, chaired by Vice President JD Vance, to discuss next steps. The two sides disagree even about who controls the waterway: Mr Trump claims "total control" of it. Reports say he keeps open the option of strikes after the US midterm elections in November. For British readers the war reaches home in two ways: through the price of oil, which feeds into the bond yields above, and through bases such as Fairford.
