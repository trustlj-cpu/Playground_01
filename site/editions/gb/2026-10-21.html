Covid inquiry finds Matt Hancock's care home 'protective ring' claim was unfounded, in a report citing more than 43,000 deaths

The UK Covid-19 Inquiry's Module 6 report, published on 20 October, calls the care sector 'something of an afterthought', according to the inquiry, the Guardian, BBC News and the Evening Standard.

The UK Covid-19 Inquiry published its Module 6 report on the care sector on Tuesday 20 October. The inquiry said in its news release that the claim that a 'protective ring' had been put around care homes from the start of the pandemic was unfounded, and the Evening Standard headlined its report that Matt Hancock's care home protective ring claim was 'unfounded'. LBC reports that Mr Hancock made the claim in May 2020. The inquiry described the care sector as 'something of an afterthought' and found that governments knew before the pandemic how vulnerable care home residents were, yet homes received too little protection at the time they needed it most. The Guardian reports that the inquiry's chair described the failure to advise care homes to isolate people discharged from hospital as significant, and its headline says government failures let Covid spread 'like wildfire' in care homes. The Daily Mail, the Mirror and the Independent each put the toll at 43,000 care home deaths in their headlines, and the inquiry's report refers to more than 43,000 recorded deaths involving Covid-19 between March 2020 and July 2022.

The report finds that in the early months of the pandemic many hospital patients were discharged to care homes without a Covid-19 test and that the sector struggled to obtain personal protective equipment, known as PPE. It traces these failures to an absence of proper planning for adult social care before the pandemic. Among its recommendations is mandatory end-of-life care training for health and social care workers, and it says it found no evidence of a national policy encouraging blanket use of do-not-attempt-cardiopulmonary-resuscitation orders, known as DNACPR notices. Before publication the Mirror reported that bereaved families wanted the report to show who was responsible for decisions that cost so many lives. At earlier inquiry hearings, openDemocracy and Caring Times reported, Mr Hancock accepted that the ring had not been unbroken and agreed that the claim was misleading, while arguing that he had meant the government tried to protect homes. The Daily Mail framed the findings as care home residents having been 'sacrificed' to protect the NHS.

UK to deploy RAF fighter jets to help defend Saudi Arabia from Houthi attacks, in a defensive role

The Ministry of Defence published an operational update on 20 October on UK military support to Saudi Arabia, and BBC News, the Guardian and the Evening Standard report that the UK will deploy fighter jets to help defend the kingdom from Houthi attacks. The Guardian's report names RAF Typhoon fighters, and Al Jazeera's headline also refers to Typhoon jets. Bloomberg reported that the RAF will fly missions to defend Saudi Arabia, a report relayed by Reuters, and the Associated Press said the UK is expanding its support to include 'defensive' air patrols. Reports say the aircraft will have a defensive role and will not carry out strikes against the Houthis, the Iran-backed group in Yemen. The new deployment follows earlier, smaller steps. In September ITV News and Al Jazeera reported that the UK would provide air-to-air refuelling from a single RAF Voyager based at RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus, in addition to the Sky Sabre air defence system already in Saudi Arabia. Reports on 20 October add an Atlas A400M transport aircraft and military advisers, and say the refuelling arrangement has been extended by 90 days. Saudi Arabia has postponed its Future Investment Initiative conference, nicknamed 'Davos in the Desert', to 2027 after the attacks, Reuters and Bloomberg report.

BBC confirms closure of BBC Three, BBC Scotland and Radio 4 Extra linear services as it seeks to save £500m

The BBC announced on 20 October that BBC Three, BBC Scotland and Radio 4 Extra will close as linear broadcast services as part of its drive to save £500m, BBC News, the Guardian and the Evening Standard report. The Guardian's headline says the BBC Three and BBC Scotland television channels are to be shut down next year, and BritBrief puts the closures in spring 2027. BBC Three and BBC Scotland will continue online, with BBC Scotland remaining a channel on iPlayer and some of its content also shown on BBC One and BBC Two in Scotland, and Radio 4 Extra's programmes will move to BBC Sounds, according to reports. Reporting Scotland: News at Seven will not move to iPlayer and will end when the linear channel closes, BritBrief says.

Director-general Matt Brittin said the confirmation will be hard for audiences and colleagues. In the summer he had said the push to save £500m 'may well mean' some services closing, and the BBC said in June that about 550 job losses were expected. BritBrief reports that a further 100 jobs will go at BBC News and 10 roles in Scotland, though reports differ on the overall number of jobs affected. Audio performers have criticised the loss of Radio 4 Extra in its current form, saying it will be a significant blow to the income of audio artists.

Chancellor John Healey rules out raising capital gains tax ahead of the 28 October Budget

Chancellor John Healey has rejected calls to raise capital gains tax in his first Budget on 28 October, BBC News, the Guardian, City AM and the Evening Standard report. BBC News says influential Labour figures had been pushing for capital gains tax to be equalised with income tax, while critics argued this could backfire. The Times, as relayed by Zawya, reported that he decided after being warned that a higher rate could reduce revenue if wealthy people left Britain or delayed selling assets. Capital gains tax is currently charged at 18% and 24% on most gains. City AM reports that investors welcomed the end of what it called damaging speculation.

The Daily Mail reports that Mr Healey is targeting banks instead, and Zawya's report of the Times says the main revenue-raising measure will be a levy on banks, with the proceeds funding a temporary package of cost-of-living support. Reuters, citing the Financial Times, reported that a smaller increase had been under consideration, so reports differ on whether any change is ruled out. The Treasury's position is that tax decisions are for the Chancellor to set out at fiscal events. Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, said in comments reported by the Guardian that his Budget would be worse than Rachel Reeves's if he did not cut spending.

Storm Austen, the first named storm of the season, brings a yellow wind warning and gusts of up to 70mph

The Met Office has named Storm Austen, the first named storm of the 2026/27 season, and BBC Weather reports that it will bring severe gales to the UK. The Guardian says the storm is forecast to hit southern UK. AOL and GB News report a yellow wind warning for parts of southern England and Wales, with reports differing slightly on timing, running from around midnight on Thursday until midday on Friday. Gusts of 50mph to 60mph are widely expected and exposed coasts could see more than 70mph, Your Weather reports. The Mirror says people in 38 areas were urged to prepare, and the Daily Mail forecasts an October half-term washout for drought-hit England.

Forecasters say the track is still being refined, so the areas affected could change. The Met Office, Met Éireann and the Dutch weather service KNMI jointly publish the names, and Austen was chosen to mark Met Éireann's 90th anniversary, honouring Austen Nagle, the first director of the Irish Meteorological Service. Storms are named when they could bring medium or high impacts, typically an amber or red warning. Bloomberg's headline says the storm will boost wind power in Europe as gas prices surge.
