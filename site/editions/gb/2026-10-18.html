Reform UK faces fresh racism allegations as Nigel Farage is accused of sitting on his hands

An undercover ITV sting, in which a councillor is shown saying he wants to 'machine gun' grooming gang perpetrators, has landed a day after another Reform councillor quit over racist remarks.

Reform UK ended the week defending its record on discipline after two separate episodes involving its local representatives. The Mirror reports that an undercover sting by ITV shows a Reform councillor saying he wants to 'machine gun' perpetrators of grooming gang crimes. The same report says Nigel Farage has been accused of 'sitting on his hands' in response to what it calls fresh allegations of racism within the party. The coverage reviewed does not name who made that accusation, and no response from Mr Farage is quoted in the material available to us. The second episode came a day earlier. The Guardian reported late on Friday that Alan Feeney, who sat on both Solihull and Birmingham councils, had resigned after being filmed making racist remarks. Mr Feeney was quoted as saying there was 'no excuse' for his comments. Neither report says whether the filming was part of the ITV operation, so the two cases should be read as separate until a newsroom says otherwise.

Why does this matter beyond the individuals? Reform has built much of its pitch on being a party of ordinary local people who can win council seats from the established parties. That strategy depends on many new, often first-time candidates, and each filmed remark gives opponents a ready example of vetting that critics say is too thin. The 'sitting on his hands' charge is aimed squarely at the leader: it suggests that the test is not whether individuals resign but whether the party acts before a camera crew forces the issue. What is known is limited. One councillor has resigned and the party has not, in the reports reviewed, announced a wider review of its candidates. The identity and current status of the councillor in the ITV footage are not given in the summary available, and the full ITV report was not among the material reviewed. There has been no police statement connected to the remarks in the coverage we have, and nothing here says any offence is alleged. Readers wanting certainty should wait for the broadcaster's full report and for a formal Reform response, since both the accusation and the party's answer are still developing and the details may be corrected or expanded.

Police take no further action against the wife of Downing Street's national security adviser over a Palestine Action post

Police have said there will be no further action against Sarah Helm, the author married to the UK national security adviser Jonathan Powell, over a social media post about Palestine Action. BBC reporting on Saturday evening says Ms Helm was responding to a discussion about the novelist Sally Rooney, who has voiced support for the group. The Guardian had reported on Friday that Mr Powell was under pressure over his wife's comment, which it described as backing Ms Rooney's support for a proscribed direct-action organisation. The case turns on a narrow point of law. Palestine Action is a proscribed organisation, and expressing support for a proscribed group can be an offence under terrorism legislation. Police decisions on whether a particular post crosses the line are made case by case, and the reports reviewed do not set out the force's reasoning beyond the decision itself. The text of the post is also not reproduced in the summaries available to us. What remains unclear is whether any further internal process will follow. The coverage reviewed does not say whether Downing Street has commented, nor whether Ms Helm has withdrawn or amended her post. Readers should treat the outcome as one decision by police on one post, rather than a general ruling on what may be said about Palestine Action.

Water customers could face an extra £40 a year if the sector is hit by a credit rating downgrade, an industry chief warns

Water customers face paying an extra £40 a year to their suppliers if the industry is hit by a fresh ratings downgrade, an industry chief has warned, Sky News reports. The sums matter because a lower credit rating raises the cost at which companies can borrow, and the money to repay it ultimately comes from bills. The report does not identify which agency would act, or when, in the summary we reviewed. The warning lands in a sector already under scrutiny for service. The Guardian carried a separate account of a south London bike shop hit by what its owner calls an unbearable stench from a Thames Water sewer. The two stories are not linked in the coverage, but together they illustrate the argument at the heart of the debate: customers pay more while experiencing the infrastructure as failing.

Readers should note that £40 is a warning about a possible outcome, not a bill increase that has been approved. Any change to bills would have to go through the regulator's price-setting process, and the reports reviewed give no decision date. We have not seen the full text of the industry chief's remarks.
