Healey set to raise taxes on banks in the Budget

Two separate reports say the next Budget will include a tax rise aimed at the banking sector, but no figures have been confirmed.

John Healey is preparing to raise taxes on banks in the forthcoming Budget, according to two separate outlets. The Guardian's business live blog on Friday morning said he was “poised” to increase the levy on lenders, and a second report carried the same line under the headline “UK's Healey plans to raise bank taxes”. A third headline in circulation described a “Budget bank tax raid”. Each of these rests on the same underlying reporting, so the claim is best read as a strong signal rather than a confirmed policy.

What is missing matters as much as what is there. None of the reports we could check gives a firm figure for how much extra money would be raised, whether the change would come through a higher surcharge on profits, a new levy on balance sheets, or a change to the rules on how losses can be offset. Nor has the Treasury confirmed any of it on the record. Until the Budget statement is delivered, the numbers should be treated as unverified, and the final package could look quite different.

The politics are easier to read than the detail. Chancellors of both main parties have found banks an attractive target when public finances are tight, because the sector is profitable, concentrated in a handful of large firms and unlikely to win much public sympathy. The counter-argument, which the industry makes every time, is that higher taxes reduce lending, push investment towards rival financial centres and are ultimately passed on to customers through wider margins and fees. The City will be listening closely for which of those arguments the government decides to engage with.

For ordinary readers the direct effect is modest. Most people hold no shares in a bank except through a pension fund or tracker, and the tax would fall on company profits, not on current accounts. The indirect effect is harder to call. If lenders respond by trimming savings rates or tightening mortgage pricing, households would notice. If they absorb the cost, shareholders would. Friday's market moves gave little away: the FTSE 100 closed at 10,881.9, up 0.38 per cent.

What to watch: any Treasury briefing before the Budget, the reaction of bank shares on Monday, and whether trade bodies publish their own estimates of the cost. Those would turn a headline into something checkable.

Reform UK under pressure over candidate vetting after undercover filming

Reform UK is facing questions about how it vets candidates after undercover filming broadcast by ITV, reported by the BBC, the Guardian and the Evening Standard. The BBC says the party insists it has “improved and prioritised” its vetting procedures. The footage reportedly shows an official warning Nigel Farage that he was “protecting” a candidate over alleged racism, according to the Guardian. The row spread on Friday. Robert Jenrick, now a Reform MP, claimed Farage had done more to stand against racism than “almost anybody”, the Guardian reports, and the Standard says he denied a claim by Sarah Pochin that parts of the party were “chauvinistic”. These are one-outlet accounts of what each politician said, and we have not seen the full footage. Unconfirmed: how many candidates are involved, whether the cases are isolated or part of a pattern, and what the party has done about the individuals concerned. The party's statement on vetting, as the BBC reports it, is general and does not address the specific allegations.

Documents show police feared Andrew would stall a search by citing the King's ownership

Legal records that have now emerged show why police wanted to search Andrew's home, the BBC reports, and that officers feared he would delay the search by arguing the property belonged to the King. The BBC and a news aggregator's collation of the same documents agree on that point. The reporting appeared on Friday, and the records describe the police's thinking at the time they sought access, not anything that happened on the day of a search.

What the officers were hoping to find is described only in outline in the coverage we could check. We therefore do not repeat any claim about the material sought. The documents are a record of the police's reasoning, not a finding about anyone's conduct, and no charge is mentioned in the reports. Neither outlet says a search was blocked: the BBC reports that police feared a delay, which is a statement about expectation, not about what followed.

Still unconfirmed: the full text of the documents, what exactly was sought, and whether the King's ownership argument was ever put to officers. Further detail is likely if the documents are quoted more fully over the weekend, and we will say so if a third outlet confirms any of it.

Politics · Whitehall✓ 2 outlets · amount described only as six figures

Sacked Foreign Office chief Olly Robbins settles with the government

Sir Olly Robbins, the former head of the Foreign Office, has reached a settlement with the government over his sacking, the BBC reports. The Guardian puts the payout at six figures and says he had applied for judicial review, a court challenge to a public body's decision, after Keir Starmer dismissed him in April over the vetting of Peter Mandelson. The exact sum has not been published in the reports we saw.

The timeline, as the two outlets give it: the dismissal came in April, the judicial review application followed, and the settlement was reported on Friday. The BBC reports the settlement itself; the Guardian supplies the six-figure description and the judicial review detail. Neither report, as we saw them, gives the precise amount or any terms beyond that.

The settlement means a court will not now rule on whether the dismissal was lawful, so the arguments over who was responsible for the vetting decision stay unresolved in public. Opposition parties are likely to ask what the taxpayer is paying for. Unconfirmed: the exact sum, whether the government has commented on the terms, and whether any further detail will be published.
