Barclays to cut 5,000 jobs, about 5% of its workforce, as it turns to AI to save costs

BBC News and Sky News report that the bank is using more artificial intelligence, on a day when it also reported a 12% rise in quarterly profits.

Barclays announced on Thursday 22 October that it is cutting 5,000 jobs, BBC News and Sky News report, with the bank saying it is using more artificial intelligence to save costs and drive efficiency. Sky News describes the cuts as 5% of the workforce, and the BBC says the greater use of AI is leading to a 5% reduction in staff. The Evening Standard reports that the bank put the figure at about 5% of all its employees, who totalled around 93,000 globally at the end of 2025, and its headline says the jobs will go this year. The Mirror reports that the bank said around 1,000 of the roles were axed during the first half of 2026 as part of a cost-cutting drive, with the remainder spread out over the second half. The Financial Times reports that the reductions were announced as third-quarter profits fell short of City expectations, and City AM reports that Bill Borden, Microsoft's global head of financial services, told it that AI would replace some jobs in Britain's banking sector.

The announcement came with the bank's results for the three months to the end of September. The Guardian reports that Barclays posted £2.3bn in pre-tax profit for July to September, with profits up 12%, and gave a further £500m to shareholders. City AM reports that the £2.3bn narrowly missed an internal consensus of £2.4bn, because the investment bank lagged behind forecasts even though the trading desks provided a boost. The Guardian adds that the lender is steeling itself for an increase in UK bank taxes in the Budget, and the Evening Standard's web address for its report refers to the Chancellor, John Healey, and the Budget. The Guardian's headline and City AM's headline therefore describe the same quarter differently, one as a jump in profits and the other as a miss against expectations.

Government urges households and businesses to prepare for emergencies as Streeting warns of state-sponsored attacks on infrastructure

Updated government advice published on 22 October encourages households to take a few simple steps to stay prepared if everyday services are temporarily disrupted, GOV.UK says. The Financial Times reports that ministers are urging households and businesses to prepare better, and to stock up on essential items, amid a growing risk of natural disasters, cyber attacks or state-sponsored terrorism. The Guardian reports that the Defence Secretary, Wes Streeting, said Britain should prepare for the threat of 'state-sponsored terrorism' by hostile powers targeting everyday infrastructure, and that he gave the warning as he opened a tank gun barrel factory in the West Midlands. GOV.UK's published text of Mr Streeting's speech at the opening of Telford Gun Hall says he spoke on British industry and sovereign production capacity. The Guardian's report on the opening says Germany's Rheinmetall has opened the Midlands factory, quotes the Defence Secretary as saying that Britain must manufacture at scale, and says that for the last 20 years Britain could not put a barrel on its tanks without buying from abroad. AOL.co.uk reports that Mr Streeting was blasted for 'scaremongering' over the alert, and its headline quotes a critic who said 'Everyone will panic buy!'.

Sir Keir Starmer could be summoned to give evidence to the grooming gangs inquiry, with hearings due in December

Sir Keir Starmer may be required to give evidence at the grooming gangs inquiry, with hearings due to begin in December, Sky News reports. The Evening Standard reports that the former prime minister is a 'core participant' in the national inquiry into child sexual exploitation scandals, and its headline says he could be called to give evidence. The Telegraph and Argus headlined its report that he could give evidence in the Bradford grooming gangs inquiry, and the Keighley News headlined that a former prime minister could give evidence in the Keighley inquiry.

The wording differs between outlets on how firm the prospect is. Sky News and the Evening Standard say he 'could' be summoned or called, while a headline on AOL.co.uk says he is 'to be summoned'. The Law Society Gazette reported in June 2025 that Sir Keir had ordered a statutory inquiry into grooming gangs.

Competition watchdog confirms Aldi and Lidl will face the same land agreement rules as other major supermarkets

The Competition and Markets Authority has confirmed that Aldi and Lidl will be covered by the same land agreement rules as the UK's other large supermarkets, GOV.UK reports in a release dated 22 October, which says the aim is to ensure customers get the best choice of where to shop. City AM reports that Aldi and Lidl are currently excluded from a law that bans the UK's biggest supermarket chains from engaging in so-called 'landblocking'. The Grocer headlined the decision as Aldi and Lidl facing the same land rules as other supermarkets.

City AM reports that supermarket groups including Waitrose and Morrisons have welcomed the competition watchdog's decision to crack down on Aldi and Lidl. City AM's headline calls it a crackdown, while the GOV.UK release describes the change as bringing the two discounters under the rules that apply to the larger chains.

Regulators propose to relax post-2008 rules holding City bosses to account, with regulators saying it will save businesses £2bn over 10 years

Regulators have unveiled plans to simplify the Senior Manager and Certification Regime, the Bank of England says in a release dated 22 October, adding that the number of financial services roles required to face enhanced checks from regulators will fall significantly under proposals that will support growth. The Guardian reports that the plans water down rules introduced after the financial crisis to hold City bosses accountable for wrongdoing, and the Financial Times headlined the change as the UK watering down 'fit and proper' rules set up to hold bankers to account.

The Guardian reports that regulators say removing 'unnecessary burdens' will save businesses about £2bn over 10 years. The Guardian's headline dates the rules to 2008, and describes the plans as a relaxation of the post-2008 regime. The Bank of England's release describes the same proposals as a simplification, while the Guardian's headline describes them as a relaxation.
