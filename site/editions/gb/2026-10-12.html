UK · PoliticsPress ✓ 5 outlets · the 'panic button' framing is the Mirror's alone; details come from advance briefing of the speech

Farage pledges to suspend the asylum system for a full parliament if Reform wins power. Every claim would be rejected and existing ones voided

Reform UK would also scrap asylum tribunals and legal routes, and put tariffs on countries that refuse to take people back. The Mirror links the move to a fall in the party's poll ratings; that reading is the paper's own.

Nigel Farage, the leader of Reform UK, will pledge that a Reform government would suspend the asylum system for a full parliamentary term, which is up to five years, the BBC, the Evening Standard, the Telegraph, the Independent and the Mirror reported late on Sunday. According to the BBC, every asylum application would be rejected and existing claims voided, with all claimants marked for deportation. The Standard said all asylum tribunals, legal refugee routes and resettlement schemes would be scrapped. The Times reported that Reform would also impose tariffs, which are taxes on imports, on countries that refuse to take back their citizens, a point the Mirror had trailed earlier in the day. Asylum is protection for people who flee persecution. At present the Home Office decides claims, and a refused applicant can appeal to an independent tribunal, so the plan would remove both steps for the length of the term. Resettlement schemes bring refugees to Britain directly from camps abroad after checks, so closing them would shut the safe and legal routes as well as the route of arriving and then claiming.

The Mirror, a paper hostile to Reform, called the move a 'panic button' after a fall in the party's poll ratings and continuing donor controversies. That is the paper's characterisation, not a finding from the other outlets, and none of them published poll numbers. No report said where deported claimants would be sent, how many detention places would be needed, or what the plan would cost. Britain is a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention, the treaty that says states must not send people back to a place where they face persecution, and to the European Convention on Human Rights, so a blanket suspension would almost certainly be challenged in the courts, most likely through judicial review, a procedure in which judges decide whether a government decision is lawful. The reports did not say how Reform would handle those treaties.

The pledge arrives as the argument over migration sharpens. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said on Sunday that he holds Mr Farage 'significantly responsible' for what he called a 'decade of division and drift', and accused him of telling 'lies' about migration during the Brexit campaign, the Mirror reported. A parliament lasts five years at most, so the pledge would cover a whole term. It is a campaign promise: Reform is in opposition, and the reports are based on advance briefing of the speech, so the wording may change when the text is published. The Telegraph summed up the plan as shutting down the asylum system. Expect the Home Office and the other parties to respond today.

UK · PoliticsPress ✓ 5 outlets · the Prime Minister gave no evidence in public

Burnham says it is 'clear' Russia interfered in the 2016 Brexit referendum. He does not say who gave him the answer

Prime Minister Andy Burnham said on Sunday that it is clear Russia interfered in the 2016 EU referendum, the Guardian, the BBC, Reuters, the Financial Times and the Evening Standard reported. The Guardian said he asked the question when he entered Downing Street and was given a 'clear answer', but he did not say who gave it or on what evidence. The BBC reported that he told voters they had not been 'sufficiently' informed about how foreign actors try to disrupt British democracy, and the FT said he called for European voters to be better informed. The Evening Standard quoted him saying people had not been informed enough about 'the extent to which people are seeking to disrupt the political process from overseas'. The Independent called him the first prime minister to confirm Russian interference in the vote. He did not say it changed the result: Leave won the referendum on 23 June 2016 with 52% of the vote. Whether Russia interfered has been disputed for a decade. A 2020 report by Parliament's Intelligence and Security Committee said the government of the day had not looked for evidence, and no inquiry has put a figure on any effect. Mr Burnham spoke as he sharpened his attack on Mr Farage, who campaigned for Brexit. None of the outlets reported that new documents had been released, so for now this is the Prime Minister's statement of his view, not published proof.

World · UkrainePress ✓ 7 outlets · Mr Trump's words come from his own post; Russia has not responded; the arms threat is the FT's report

Trump declares an 'energy ceasefire' between Russia and Ukraine. Zelenskiy says it is news to him, and a Kharkiv power plant is hit hours later

President Donald Trump wrote on Truth Social on Sunday afternoon that 'effective immediately' there is an energy ceasefire in the war between Russia and Ukraine and that 'both parties have agreed', the BBC, Reuters, the Guardian and the Evening Standard reported. President Volodymyr Zelenskiy said it was 'news to me', the Guardian reported, but that Ukraine would agree if Russia did; Reuters said Kyiv would stop striking Russian refineries if Moscow stops attacking its energy network. Russia has not commented, the BBC said. The Mirror and The Hill reported that a power plant in Kharkiv was struck, at least twice according to the Mirror, shortly after Mr Trump's post, and Reuters said Russian attacks killed six people in two Ukrainian cities.

The announcement follows Mr Trump's deal to take Russian diesel, which the Guardian said temporarily lifts sanctions on Russian fuel. Reuters reported that a prominent Republican senator said the deal may be illegal, and the FT said lawmakers from both parties argue it would finance the war and break US law. Reuters, citing the FT, said Washington has threatened to block arms for Ukraine over its refinery attacks; that is not confirmed by the US. On Saturday Mr Trump had said it was time for Ukraine to get 'a new president', and Zelenskiy told ABC he would organise elections if Russia agrees to a 60-day ceasefire, Reuters said. The Evening Standard said Mr Burnham's government backs an energy ceasefire.

Sport · Premier LeaguePress ✓ 6 outlets · scores from BBC Sport and the Guardian

Manchester City win 3-0 at Liverpool in their first match since being found guilty of breaking financial rules. Burnham promises to publish his City meetings

Manchester City beat Liverpool 3-0 at Anfield on Sunday, the BBC, the Guardian, Reuters and the Telegraph reported, to make it six wins from six and move three points clear at the top of the Premier League. Liverpool's Ryan Gravenberch was sent off, and manager Andoni Iraola said he did not like the refereeing; Jamie Carragher criticised the dismissal on Sky Sports. The BBC praised Jeremy Doku, and the Guardian said Rayan Cherki capped the win with a 'cracker'. The home crowd booed and chanted 'cheat' at City, who were found guilty last week of breaking the league's financial rules, a verdict they deny and are appealing. Manager Enzo Maresca said his players had 'dealt with the noise'.

Politics followed the football. Mr Burnham, a former mayor of Greater Manchester, said he will publish details of his meetings with the club, the BBC, the Evening Standard and the Guardian reported, after Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch challenged him to release 'everything' and his comments after the verdict drew criticism. Elsewhere, Crystal Palace beat Nottingham Forest, Hull and Everton drew, and Celtic won 2-0 at Motherwell to go top of the Scottish Premiership.

World · Middle EastPress ✓ 6 outlets · the Sunday casualty count and the Dammam and Aramco claims rest on single sources and are attributed

Houthis hit Riyadh airport for a second day running, and Saudi aviation officials say Dammam airport was also targeted. Britain warns against all but essential travel

A second attack hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh on Sunday, a day after a strike that Saudi authorities said killed 12 people and wounded more than 300, the BBC, Bloomberg, the Guardian and the Mirror reported. The Saudi-led coalition in Yemen said an unidentified projectile hit Terminal 4 and started a fire, and that air defences intercepted a missile nearby. FinancialJuice, quoting Saudi civil aviation, said one person died and 11 were wounded; that figure comes from one outlet. The Houthis, the Iran-backed armed movement that controls much of northern Yemen, claimed responsibility and warned airlines and travellers to avoid Saudi airports. They also said they struck Dammam airport and Aramco facilities in the Eastern Province, which Riyadh has not confirmed, though Saudi civil aviation said Dammam airport was targeted on Sunday evening. The coalition vowed to 'respond firmly', the Guardian said. The UK embassy cautioned British citizens against all but essential travel to Jeddah, Dammam and all Saudi airports except Medina, FinancialJuice reported, citing the embassy's post on X.

Business is reacting. The FT reported that BP and other oil chiefs pulled out of the World Petroleum Council's congress in Riyadh, while the Guardian said Canelo Álvarez will still fight in the city later this month. At sea, the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, which warns shipping of dangers, reclassified an incident involving a tanker near Sohar in Oman as an attack, FinancialJuice reported; India's shipping minister said all 11 Indian crew on a Marshall Islands-flagged tanker were safe. Bloomberg's Sunday-evening markets wrap said oil rose and the dollar edged higher. London reopens today after Friday's FTSE 100 close of 10,552.05, up 1.06%. On Friday the S&P 500 closed at 7,811.54 (+0.59%), WTI crude at $91.85 a barrel (+0.39%) and gold at $4,216.3 (+1.43%).
