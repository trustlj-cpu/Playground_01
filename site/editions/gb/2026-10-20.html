Burnham calls the Neet crisis a 'moral failure' and says benefits will become 'the backstop, not the starting point'

Personnel Today, BBC News, the Evening Standard and the Guardian report the prime minister's pledge of 'big changes' to welfare for the roughly 981,000 young people not in education, employment or training.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has described the number of young people not in education, employment or training, known as Neets, as an 'economic catastrophe and moral failure', Personnel Today reports. BBC News headlined its report that he called the youth employment and training crisis a 'moral failure', and the Evening Standard reported that he vowed to tackle it. Personnel Today says the words appeared in a column for The Times on Monday 19 October, in which he wrote that the government can invest in the success of young people or pay for failure through the benefits system, and that he chooses investment. It quotes him as saying: 'Benefits will become the backstop, not the starting point.' The outlet cites Office for National Statistics figures putting the number of 16 to 24 year olds classed as Neet at an estimated 981,000 between April and June, roughly one in eight people in that age group. The Guardian's politics live blog reports that he promised 'big changes' to the way the welfare system works to tackle the crisis.

Personnel Today reports that Mr Burnham criticised a system that offers an assessment, a payment and then goodbye, with too often no support, no offer of skills and no opportunity to get a job. He pointed to two years of Covid lockdowns, social media, an education system that pushes the 'university route' and job markets with few opportunities, and said reform must be matched by an 'expectation that young people will take up the opportunities made available to them'. He also argued, in the words Personnel Today quotes, that the state should provide a safety net but that there must be a 'fundamental change in the way the Government and society support young people'. Personnel Today reports that Alan Milburn, who is leading a government review of the Neet crisis, told the Guardian that his report would probably recommend cutting benefit spending to channel the money into supporting people back into work. The review has not yet published its recommendations; no date or cost for the changes has been reported.

Burnham outlines private parking rules giving drivers five minutes to decide and ten minutes to leave

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has set out new rules for private car parks that he says will end 'rip-off' charges, the Guardian, the Mirror and BBC News report. The Guardian reports that drivers would get five minutes to decide whether to park and ten minutes to leave after their session ends, and the Mirror says motorists in England, Scotland and Wales would be given ten minutes to leave without facing a penalty. The Mirror and BBC News report that charges would be dropped on appeal for honest mistakes, such as briefly stopping to check directions, medical emergencies, breakdowns or a broken ticket machine, and that for smaller slips, such as entering the wrong registration, the maximum charge on appeal would be £20. The coverage cites the RAC as saying private firms issued 16.9 million parking tickets in the last financial year, up 17% on the year before. The AA's Jack Cousens said drivers finally have something that could offer better protection against the worst private operators. The British Parking Association welcomed the changes but argued that many of the principles are already reflected in its voluntary code of practice. A GOV.UK release is headlined 'PM puts the brakes on rip-off parking charges and car scams'.

Cristiano Ronaldo is given a 30-day suspension and a €255 fine by the Portuguese FA over his walkout from the national camp

The Portuguese Football Federation's disciplinary council has suspended Cristiano Ronaldo for 30 days and fined him €255, about £215, over his walkout from the Portugal camp, the BBC, the Guardian and Reuters report. The Guardian reports that the council upheld the charge against the 41-year-old, and Reuters ties the penalty to his leaving the camp after a rift with coach Jorge Jesus. Sky Sports describes an explosive row with Mr Jesus. Al Jazeera reports that the forward left the camp in Copenhagen shortly before a Nations League game against Denmark, after accusing the coach of twice breaking his word about his role following a match against Norway in which he was an unused substitute.

Ronaldo had already been provisionally suspended after the walkout. The Guardian reports that federation president Pedro Proença has left the door open for a return, and reports say everyone at the federation wanted Ronaldo back once the matter was resolved. Ronaldo has apologised for failing to acknowledge supporters after the Norway match and for leaving his teammates, and said he would accept any punishment. Reports differ on when the ban starts: one says that if it is backdated to the provisional ruling he could play in Portugal's next match, against Denmark on 14 November, while another, citing Sky Sports, says it is retroactive to his walkout on 30 September.

Downing Street says Pete Hegseth was wrong to claim that most non-US Nato forces in Afghanistan did not fight

A Downing Street spokesperson has said US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth was wrong to claim that most non-US Nato forces in Afghanistan did not fight, the Guardian, the Mirror and The News International report. The Guardian reports that the spokesperson gave the answer when asked at the lobby briefing, emphasised the sacrifices of UK troops and reasserted 'unwavering' support for Ukraine. The Mirror and The News International put Mr Hegseth's remark as follows: when he was a captain in Afghanistan, the Kabul headquarters flew about 50 flags, only a few countries contributed, and by the end only one or two were in kinetic operations. They add that he joked that ISAF stood for 'I Saw Americans Fighting'.

The News International reports that more than 450 British personnel died in the conflict, alongside troops from over 30 other countries, and that Downing Street said diminishing their role is wrong. It adds that the criticism follows similar comments from Donald Trump earlier this year that Nato allies stayed back from the front line.

Unite warns that an Apache offshore strike on Wednesday 21 October could disrupt UK fuel supplies through the Forties pipeline

Unite says more than 160 Apache offshore workers will walk out for 24 hours from 06:00 on Wednesday 21 October in a dispute over pay and backdated wages, the Guardian, STV News and Energy Live News report. The action covers seven installations across the Forties and Beryl fields, including the Forties Charlie platform. Unite argues that stopping Charlie could reduce pressure enough to shut the Forties Pipeline System, which it says carries around 29% of UK oil and 30% of UK gas. The union says the workers rejected an offer ranging from a pay freeze to below-inflation rises. Energy Live News runs the story under the headline 'Apache strike threatens fuel supplies as pump prices hit crisis levels'.

Baird Maritime reports that Apache says it will use experienced contractors to keep production going and does not expect the action to affect other producers using the Forties pipeline, even if its five platforms there could not operate. Ineos, which owns the pipeline system, declined to speculate on the impact. Reports add that Acas talks were scheduled before the walkout and that a second 24-hour strike is planned for 4 November if the dispute is not resolved; Apache has said its final offer is fair.
