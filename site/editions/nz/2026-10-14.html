Greens price their economic plan at nearly $6 billion and promise 284,000 jobs in four years

The party says that total is 64,000 more than the Treasury already forecasts, which is the number the New Zealand Herald put in its headline. One economist told RNZ he has never changed his vote because of a costing.

The Green Party launched its economic plan on Tuesday 13 October, with co-leaders Marama Davidson and Chlöe Swarbrick presenting what 1News billed as the party’s vision for the economy. RNZ reported that the plan costs nearly $6 billion and that the Greens say it would create 284,000 jobs in four years, 64,000 more than the Treasury forecasts. The New Zealand Herald led with the smaller number, headlining a $6 billion package that promises 64,000 new jobs and describing a pledge to reignite innovation. The two headlines look like one claim seen from opposite ends. If the Greens’ arithmetic holds, the Treasury’s baseline, meaning its forecast of what happens with no change in policy, is about 220,000 jobs over the period, and the package adds the rest. That is our reading of the headlines, not something either outlet spelled out, and we have not seen the party’s costings. The party’s own announcement, listed beside the coverage, was titled as a jobs guarantee for young people, and a Stuff headline said the Greens would keep National’s tax break, but only for some people. The detail behind both was not available to us. Three questions follow from the headlines: how many of the jobs would be public-sector places, how much of the $6 billion would be new borrowing, and over what period it would be spent. The coverage we checked answers none of them.

The announcement came in the campaign’s third week, with the election on 7 November and National’s fiscal plan from Monday still being picked over. Whether any of it moves votes is a fair question. RNZ spoke to the economist Michael Reddell, who said he has never changed his mind about how to vote because of a fiscal plan or an accusation that the numbers do not add up. The Herald reported that the ASB, one of the big banks, looked at seven election cycles and found no discernible effect of an election on the economy. The backdrop is uneven: ANZ told RNZ that traffic data points to a patchy recovery, with strength in exports such as agriculture while households feel the pinch of higher fuel prices and rising interest rates. A jobs promise is aimed squarely at that pinch. DailyDrop has seen no Labour response to the plan in the coverage checked, and no Treasury comment on the 64,000 figure. Both are worth looking for today, along with whether independent economists think a $6 billion package can add that many jobs on top of the forecast.

Nation · Election 2026Press ✓ 4 outlets · figure is Labour’s

Labour says life costs a typical family $6000 more a year than when National took office, and makes the fight personal

Labour spent Tuesday on the cost of living. Stuff reported that the party claims it costs the typical family $6000 more a year to live than it did when the Government took office three years ago, and the Herald said Labour pointed to groceries, power bills and petrol. RNZ called it a counter-attack on National over costs, and Stuff described a highly personal campaign under the line “Cost of Luxon”, aimed at National’s tax attacks. The figure is Labour’s own, and none of the headlines we checked say how it was calculated. Christopher Luxon answered reporters in Auckland, in coverage the Herald and 1News ran live. National’s day was about young people: RNZ reported a list of commitments for its first 100 days in government meant to bring young New Zealanders home or persuade them to stay, and Stuff said the five steps include priority for work and visitor visas for the partners of citizens. RNZ also reported that National insists its superannuation stance has not changed. Jonathan Coleman, a former National health minister, told RNZ the party’s drug testing of beneficiaries is “punitive”, while 1News headlined that the plan has wide public backing. In Epsom, RNZ said Luxon told voters not to vote tactically, and the Herald that Paul Goldsmith says they no longer need to back ACT’s David Seymour.

Chip shares slide from Seoul to Wall Street as doubts about the AI boom spread, and the Kospi falls more than 5%

Doubts about spending on artificial intelligence hit chip shares across Asia on Tuesday 13 October. The Kospi, South Korea’s main share index, fell more than 5% on a semiconductor sell-off, AP reported, and Bloomberg said Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix extended a recent slide on worries about Chinese rivals and a stretched AI rally. Reuters reported Asian stocks diving ahead of the big US banks’ earnings, and Bloomberg described cracks spreading across the AI trade as memory chips tumbled. The trouble had started in the US: Bloomberg’s markets wrap said Asian shares were set to fall after a chipmaker sell-off there, and Reuters’ Morning Bid column called the AI trade fragile.

In early US trading on Tuesday, Reuters reported US chip stocks extending the slide on a report about China’s memory makers and jitters over AI spending. The first bank results were mixed: Goldman Sachs and Citi beat expectations, Wells Fargo’s profit rose, and JPMorgan posted a rare profit miss as dealmaking and bond trading lost momentum (Reuters). Behind the moves sit an IMF warning of risks from a correction in AI-linked markets, alongside war and debt, and a Bank of America fund manager survey, relayed by FinancialJuice, that found investors the most heavily weighted to tech since August 2020 and named a disorderly rise in bond yields as the biggest tail risk, ahead of an AI bubble. DailyDrop could not confirm Tuesday’s NZX 50 close, so the ear shows a dash. New Zealanders feel this through KiwiSaver funds that hold global shares; 1News reported that Kiwis have been moving out of shares and into savings.

World · Middle EastPress ✓ 4 outlets · strike reports unverified

Saudi-led coalition tells civilians to leave an oil site in Sanaa as Riyadh vows the Houthis will face consequences

The war in Yemen widened on Tuesday 13 October. Reuters reported that the Saudi-led coalition ordered the evacuation of a Yemeni oil facility in Sanaa ahead of an operation, and FinancialJuice, relaying a coalition spokesperson, named the site as the Al-Sabaha Central Station. The Saudi Cabinet, chaired by the Crown Prince, said the Houthis would face “consequences” for their repeated attacks (FinancialJuice). The Houthis replied that any attack on Sanaa would meet a harsh response in Riyadh, and told staff and civilians to keep away from Aramco facilities, which they called legitimate targets. Bloomberg reported that Saudi Arabia intercepted more ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and Khamis Mushait.

Questions about Saudi air defences continue. Reuters reported that the kingdom’s warning system stayed silent before the deadly airport attacks, and AP said Saudi officials are investigating how the defences failed. Washington has told travellers to reconsider trips to Saudi Arabia (FinancialJuice), and Qatar Airways has suspended flights to Riyadh and Dammam. Donald Trump said he had spoken with the Crown Prince, that the Houthis are behaving very badly, and that he might ask Turkey to join the response (FinancialJuice). The Houthi-run Health Ministry said two people died in an attack on an oil port on the Red Sea coast, a claim we have not confirmed. Oil is the link to New Zealand: Reuters reported crude edging lower as the prospect of Russian diesel offset Gulf supply risk, and that OPEC+ output rose in September, led by Saudi Arabia.

Nation · Foreign policyPress ✓ 3 outlets · Peters’ reasons not seen

Peters abstains on a UN pandemic vote against the wishes of most ministers, and Helen Clark calls it “humiliating”

Newsroom reported that Winston Peters, the foreign minister, overrode his Government colleagues on a United Nations vote on a declaration about pandemic prevention. A majority of ministers wanted New Zealand to vote in favour, but because no Cabinet decision was needed, Peters made what Newsroom called a captain’s call. RNZ reported New Zealand’s abstention, and quoted former prime minister Helen Clark calling it “humiliating” and asking “How low can NZ go?”. Stuff reported that Labour leader Chris Hipkins called the vote an “embarrassment to New Zealand” and that National says it does not agree with Peters’ call.

It reached the news because the decision sat within Peters’ power while going against the preference of most of his colleagues, and because National, the senior partner, said so aloud with weeks to go before the election. DailyDrop has not seen Peters’ reasons in the coverage checked, and the headlines do not give the date or the final tally of the vote. A separate foreign-policy item: RNZ reported that New Zealand was among 30 countries condemning what they called Iran’s violent attacks, and the group recognised the UK’s attribution to Iran of a suspected plot against RAF Fairford air base. The Herald also reported Peters’ office accusing the Greens of “megaphone diplomacy” over Israel.
