Haze stays unhealthy across Singapore as the S$10 treatment subsidy begins and NEA tracks the fires behind it

The 24-hour PSI was 106 to 148 at 2pm on Monday, in the unhealthy band in every region. Malaysia is closing schools in four more areas today.

Air quality stayed in the unhealthy range across Singapore on Monday. At 2pm the 24-hour Pollutant Standards Index (PSI) was between 106 and 148 in every region, The Online Citizen reported, which puts all of them in the unhealthy band of 101 to 200. CNA and the Straits Times also described the air as unhealthy, and AsiaOne said the 24-hour PSI is forecast to stay in that range. NEA, the National Environment Agency, uses the 24-hour PSI together with the one-hour PM2.5 reading, which measures fine particles small enough to reach deep into the lungs. DailyDrop has not seen the evening readings, so check NEA directly before you plan the day.

The first health measure took effect on Monday. Subsidised treatment for haze-related conditions began at Public Health Preparedness Clinics, the private GP clinics enrolled in the scheme, and at polyclinics, CNA reported, with eligible Singaporeans paying no more than S$10. Doctors told CNA that weekly flu activity is the highest since 2024 and that haze can worsen breathing problems, especially in older people, young children, pregnant women and people with heart or lung conditions. The Straits Times reported that public bus and rail operators are making N95 masks available to staff, that delivery riders can collect free N95 masks through their association, and that at least five races and running-club events have been cancelled. The Islamic Religious Council of Singapore (MUIS) said mosques stay open for daily and Friday prayers with precautions, and arrangements may change if the haze worsens (CNA, Straits Times).

Attention is turning to the source. NEA is tracking fire hotspots and where the smoke comes from, CNA reported. The Straits Times said the unhealthy readings are enough to trigger the Transboundary Haze Pollution Act, which allows legal action against companies that cause haze in Singapore even when they operate overseas. The last time NEA sent notices to firms in Indonesia was during the 2015 crisis, and investigations into four companies remain open, the Straits Times said. Indonesia’s President Prabowo Subianto ordered tougher action against the fires, the paper added, while foreign offers of help were not discussed. DailyDrop has not seen NEA name any company this time.

The smoke is also closing classrooms and runways across the Causeway. The Straits Times reported that schools in Johor, Melaka, Negeri Sembilan and Putrajaya will shut today, and that schools which stay open must monitor outdoor activity. Malaysia’s education minister, Fadhlina Sidek, defended taking closure decisions day by day after some parents asked for several days at a time, CNA said. The Straits Times counted 22 Malaysian areas with unhealthy readings on Monday morning, the worst being Kota Tinggi in Johor at 170 on Malaysia’s air pollutant index. Firefly has extended its Seletar–Subang flight cancellations to today (Straits Times). Tomorrow’s question is the NEA forecast, and whether the wind changes.

PM Wong says AI could break the “bottom rung” of the career ladder, as the IMF chief calls it a “slow-moving tsunami” for jobs

Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said on Monday that the government is worried artificial intelligence (AI) may break the “bottom rung” of the career ladder for young workers, CNA and the Straits Times reported. Speaking at the first DBS Asia Future Forward Conference, he said AI will replace routine work, but “we should be careful not to automate away the learning that comes from it”, CNA quoted him as saying. The Straits Times headlined his aim for a better society “enabled by AI, not an AI-driven one”.

Tharman Shanmugaratnam said governments must give the private sector incentives to hire fresh graduates as entry-level roles decline, and that tertiary education should move towards applied learning, including work-study programmes and internships, the Straits Times reported. Separately, the International Monetary Fund’s chief, Kristalina Georgieva, called AI a “slow-moving tsunami” for jobs and taxes, saying it is already hollowing out demand for workers by taking over routine tasks (Straits Times, Japan Times). At the same forum, the chief executives of DBS and TikTok said human judgment and intentional outcomes are vital in using AI, and TikTok’s Chew Shou Zi said there is a “non-zero chance” that AI could surpass humans in certain ways (Straits Times). The worry is practical: a first job is where most people learn how their trade works, and routine work is how they learn it. DailyDrop has not seen what, if anything, the government will change in training rules.

Riyadh airport stays shut after a fresh Houthi barrage; Malaysia tells pilgrims to put off Umrah trips

Saudi Arabia’s civil aviation authority extended the suspension of operations at King Khalid International Airport on Monday without saying when flights might resume, CNA reported. Reuters, via the Straits Times, said about 60 per cent of scheduled flights in Riyadh were expected to be cancelled, citing the analytics firm Cirium, and that Saudi Arabia faced a new barrage of incoming fire. Bloomberg reported that the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted missiles and drones overnight. The Saturday strike killed 12 people and wounded 309, according to Saudi authorities (CNA, Straits Times); the BBC put the toll at 13.

The effects spread. Malaysia urged its citizens to postpone Umrah trips and Indonesia called for vigilance (CNA, Straits Times), Delta delayed its planned Atlanta–Saudi service to March (CNBC, Bloomberg), and Reuters reported that top oil executives were dropped from an energy event in Riyadh. Saudi Arabia’s energy minister said the attacks test infrastructure and vowed a response (Straits Times), while China, Turkey, Pakistan and Egypt condemned them (CNA, Straits Times). Iran offered to mediate between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia (Straits Times, New York Times). The boxer Saul “Canelo” Alvarez will still fight in Riyadh on 31 October (Straits Times, Al Jazeera).

World · EnergyPress ✓ 7 outlets · claims attributed to each side

Kremlin rejects Zelenskiy’s 60-day ceasefire idea; Kyiv says Russia broke the “energy ceasefire” at Kharkiv

The Kremlin on Monday rejected a 60-day ceasefire proposed by Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelenskiy to allow presidential elections, saying it wants an overall peace deal instead, Reuters reported via the Straits Times. Mr Zelenskiy accused Russia of violating the “energy ceasefire” announced by Donald Trump on Sunday, after a strike on a thermal power plant in Kharkiv, and urged the United States to make Moscow stick to any agreement (Reuters, Straits Times; AP). The Hill and ABC also reported the strike. Mr Zelenskiy said on Telegram that Ukraine did not hit any Russian energy facilities overnight.

Europe is cautious. The EU’s foreign-policy chief, Kaja Kallas, said the bloc will welcome the truce “when it holds” (CNA), and EU ministers adopted nearly 1,600 new sanctions listings on Russia (Reuters). CNA reported that oil fell on hopes the truce would lift supplies; WTI settled Friday at US$91.85. DailyDrop has not seen a Kremlin statement on the energy truce itself.

Americans Robert Hall and Michael Woodford win the Nobel economics prize for work on stabilising economies

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences said on Monday that Robert Hall and Michael Woodford, both Americans, won the 2026 Nobel prize in economics “for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation”, Reuters and the Straits Times reported. The Japan Times noted it was the last Nobel prize announced this year, and Livemint put the award at 12 million Swedish crowns, about US$1.2 million. The Economist described the pair as “New Keynesians”, economists who build on the idea that governments and central banks can smooth booms and slumps.

In plain terms, the prize is about how households and firms decide what to spend and what to charge, and how that shapes the way interest rates and government spending work. Le Monde said their studies of consumption and price setting gave public policy new tools, and The Hindu said the research also shows that policy works better when governments explain why they are acting. DailyDrop has not seen individual details of either laureate’s work beyond these summaries.
