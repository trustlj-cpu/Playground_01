Sheinbaum asks the US for proof of the “shots” at the border. The SRE admits Querétaro did give notice of the ambassador’s visit

Ambassador Ronald Johnson reported shots fired at the Coast Guard; the president says there is no evidence. In the afternoon, the clash moved to a police academy in Querétaro.

The week began with an open falling-out between the National Palace and the US embassy. In the early hours, Proceso reported that Mexico was deploying troops to the border while Ambassador Ronald Johnson reported “shots” fired at members of the US Coast Guard. At her morning press conference, Claudia Sheinbaum replied that there is no evidence of those shots and asked Washington to present proof, according to El Universal, El Financiero and El Economista. A Morena deputy from Tamaulipas joined the demand. Milenio reported at night that Johnson contradicted the Mexican version of the deployment in Tamaulipas. As of this edition’s close, no authority had made public any proof of the shots.

The second front was Querétaro. Sheinbaum asked Governor Mauricio Kuri and the ambassador for explanations over Johnson’s presence at an event at a police academy, a friction El Financiero called a “clash.” Kuri replied that his government notified the Foreign Ministry a week earlier. The ministry (SRE) acknowledged it: Querétaro did give notice of the visit but, according to the agency, left out the academy as a topic, and any security cooperation project with the US must be presented to the National Security Council (Proceso, La Jornada, Milenio). El Universal reported that federal officials were in fact present at the event, and La Jornada, that the Interior Ministry (Gobernación) is gathering information. In parallel, Interior Secretary Rosa Icela Rodríguez appeared behind closed doors before the Senate’s Jucopo; there she guaranteed security in the 2027 elections and ruled out US intervention (El Universal, Proceso).

Both episodes share the same underlying question: who speaks for Mexico on security with Washington. For the federal government, cooperation with US agencies must go through national institutions and not through direct agreements between a state and the embassy. La Jornada reported that Querétaro told the SRE the visit would not involve direct security cooperation, and the PAN in the Senate argued that notice was given on time. On the Interior Ministry side, Proceso reported that Rodríguez rejected before senators the claim that organized crime controls 30% of the territory, a figure that circulates in the public debate and that she did not accept. Milenio picked up her call on the parties to choose candidates “who walk the right path.” El Universal compiled a recap of the clashes between Sheinbaum and Johnson in recent months, all around security and sovereignty. The tone matters because the bilateral agenda is loaded: the same day it emerged that the USMCA (T-MEC) review is postponed to 2027. A tense relationship on security complicates the trade negotiation, and vice versa.

Flávio Bolsonaro wins the first round in Brazil. Sheinbaum urges waiting; the peso and Brazilian stocks rise

Right-wing candidate Flávio Bolsonaro finished first in Sunday’s Brazilian presidential election and will face Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in the runoff, according to Expansión and Milenio. Proceso reported that the right also took the majority of Congress and of the governorships. Donald Trump called the result a “great victory” (La Jornada, El Economista). Sheinbaum said Mexico has no reason for concern and asked to wait for the runoff (El Universal, Proceso). Markets reacted on Monday: the Bovespa index jumped 7.7% to a record close, according to reports from Rio Times and Daily Maverick, and the Mexican peso strengthened along with other currencies in the region (Proceso, La Jornada). Lula acknowledged that he had expected to win in the first round, according to El Financiero; Milenio reported that he resumed campaigning and called for “stripping bare” his rival’s proposals. For Mexico, the result matters in two ways. The first is financial: investors’ enthusiasm for Brazil pulled the whole region along. The second is political: with Bolsonaro, Brazil would align with Washington and leave Mexico with fewer allies on the Latin American left. Proceso cited analysts who see the alliance between Pemex and Petrobras at risk if the right wins the runoff.

IMF raises its Mexico forecast to 1.5%. It urges a faster fiscal adjustment

The International Monetary Fund raised its growth forecast for the Mexican economy in 2026 to 1.5%, El Economista reported. The institution sees an improvement in GDP, but asked the government to speed up the reduction of the fiscal deficit, according to El Financiero; El Economista summed it up as a warning of “fiscal surgery.” The same paper expected public debt to grow more than revenue and the economy in 2027.

Banxico moved to calm sentiment. Expansión reported that the central bank ruled out alarm over the peso’s recent depreciation and highlighted the IMF’s backing; Deputy Governor Omar Mejía said there is confidence in Mexico and its currency (La Jornada). Markets went along: the S&P/BMV IPC rose 0.69% to 64,975.08 points and the peso strengthened about 0.4%, to around 18.08 per dollar, according to Rio Times; El Economista attributed the improvement to smaller bets on the Federal Reserve. According to Rio Times, last week the peso had touched its weakest level since November 2025, so the rebound is, for now, a partial recovery. The domestic picture remains mixed: El Economista reported that private consumption began the second half “in neutral,” while El Financiero reported record car sales of 1,144,000 units through September. Growth of 1.5% is better than expected months ago, but is still low for creating enough jobs.

Plane the Nayarit governor often used crashes. The Attorney General’s Office investigates a possible attack

An aircraft frequently used by Nayarit Governor Miguel Ángel Navarro crashed in Ixtlán del Río, El Universal and Milenio reported. The state Attorney General’s Office is investigating whether it was an attempted attack, according to El Financiero, Expansión and Proceso. La Jornada reported that the pilot and co-pilot died and that the aircraft was allegedly shot down.

Navarro attributed threats against him to the recovery of 750 hectares and said he prefers to be “dead and brave than alive and a coward,” according to Proceso. Milenio published claims about a criminal group that allegedly had the governor’s flight schedules and tail numbers. No authority has confirmed who shot down the aircraft, or whether it was shot down. The case adds to a string of attacks on local officials: the same Monday, El Financiero reported a second attack on the home of a deputy in El Salto, Jalisco, and Proceso reported a shooting attack on a former director of FideIMSS in Mexico City. The expert examination of the aircraft will be what distinguishes an accident from an attack.

TradePress ✓ 4 outlets · positions attributed to each government

US closes the door on extending the USMCA this year. The review moves to 2027

The United States will not accept a 16-year extension of the USMCA in 2026 and is taking the treaty’s review to 2027, Proceso reported. La Jornada reported that talks on rules of origin and labor issues were postponed, and El Financiero warned in its analysis that the review “will take a long time.” Economy Secretary Marcelo Ebrard anticipated a new trade system, according to El Economista.

For businesses and workers, the delay prolongs uncertainty over tariffs and investment. Expansión reported that a US assessment sees modest progress in the Plan México and five shortfalls. The same outlet warned that restricting diesel shipments to Mexico would be a “shot in the foot” for US refineries, which depend on that market. The treaty provides for a joint review: if the three countries do not confirm its extension, the agreement moves to annual reviews. That scenario does not break trade, but it keeps open the question of the rules that will govern the auto industry, steel and energy. La Jornada also reported changes to the rules for importing textiles and apparel, another sensitive front for domestic industry.
