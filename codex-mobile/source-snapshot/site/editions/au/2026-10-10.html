Firmus scraps its $5 billion float as buyers balk, citing market volatility. Australia's biggest AI test goes private

The Nvidia-backed data-centre operator will look to private investors after buyers balked at the asking price. The ASX is shut today, and Wall Street's Friday session was still trading as we went to press.

Firmus, the Nvidia-backed operator of AI data centres, shelved its initial public offering on Friday, Reuters and iTnews reported. The sale, worth about $5 billion, would have been the second-largest in Australian history, but it had struggled to find buyers. According to the reports, Firmus said its board concluded that going ahead was not in the best interests of the company and its shareholders, and that it would turn to private investors while keeping other public and private options open. Channel NewsAsia said the failure dealt a blow to a share market that is already shrinking.

The price was the problem. Firmus had planned to sell shares at $11, which Reuters-based reports said gave it a valuation of $30.6 billion, almost three times the $10.5 billion it was worth in a fundraising in early August. iTnews gave the valuation in US dollars, so we have not confirmed the currency. The AFR said earlier this week that dilution clauses were driving price talks, and reports the same week put a cut price of $8.25 on the table. One portfolio manager quoted in the coverage said investors were being asked to pay a very large sum for results that depended on near-flawless execution. Firmus runs two leased data centres, in Melbourne and Singapore, and plans five more across Asia and the Pacific.

The timing was awkward. On Thursday the S&P 500 fell 0.47 per cent to 7,765.36 and the Nasdaq lost 1.25 per cent, on worries about AI spending and a 10-year yield of 5.231 per cent. Pimco's Ivascyn told the Financial Times that the yield could reach 6 per cent for the first time since 2000, according to Reuters; we have not seen a second outlet quote him. Wall Street was still trading on Friday as we went to press, so we have no final figures. Bloomberg and Reuters both said futures pointed to a rebound as oil slipped and fears of an AI bubble eased.

At home, the S&P/ASX 200 closed on Friday at 8,716.6, up 0.64 per cent, and the Australian dollar was near 69.7 US cents early on Saturday. The exchange is shut today and reopens on Monday. With oil near US$100 a barrel, inflation and the Reserve Bank's November 3 meeting remain the backdrop for any company asking investors for money.

For investors, the lesson is narrower than the headlines. A failed float is a verdict on a price, not proof that data centres will not be built. The next questions are who funds Firmus privately, at what valuation, and whether other AI-linked companies hoping to list in Australia will have to cut their asking prices too. A report that Firmus is eyeing a Nasdaq listing after the private round comes from one source, and the company has not said so.

Navi Pillay, the South African judge who helped define rape as genocide, wins the Nobel Peace Prize

The Norwegian Nobel Committee announced in Oslo on Friday that the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize would go to Navanethem 'Navi' Pillay, a South African jurist, for her work promoting peace and international law, Reuters, AP, the Herald and the Washington Post reported. AP framed the award as recognition of her work in international justice. She was a judge at the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, later sat at the International Criminal Court, and served as the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights. The committee singled out her time at the Rwanda tribunal, whose rulings helped establish that rape and sexual violence can amount to crimes against humanity and genocide.

Pillay also chaired the UN commission of inquiry that concluded Israel committed genocide in Gaza. Israel said the award legitimised anti-Israel hatred, according to RFI, BFM and G1, and Al Jazeera reported that the committee denied taking a position on that finding. Le Figaro and ANSA reported that Pillay joked that she might share the prize with Donald Trump if the applause continued. Kyodo reported that she urged what she called rogue states not to attack the court. The New York Times noted that she forged her career in South Africa fighting apartheid, and Hindustan Times reported that the International Criminal Court welcomed the award, saying justice is key to lasting peace. The prize will be presented in Oslo on December 10.

Three die in Houthi strikes on Riyadh airport, the deadliest so far. Oil eases after Trump says no Iran attack before the midterms

Saudi Arabia's civil aviation authority said three Saudis were killed in attacks on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh on Thursday, Reuters, AP, the Washington Post and Euronews reported. One of the dead was a Saudia captain, the airline said. The authority said the first strike hit airport facilities and the second hit an aircraft. The Houthis claimed the attacks, though the Saudi statement did not name them. Al Jazeera reported that the airport reopened on Friday, and Reuters said Lufthansa and Indian airlines had suspended flights to Riyadh. Saudi officials called it the deadliest attack in the kingdom since the Saudi-led offensive against the Houthis began last weekend. Yesterday we reported no casualties; that has changed.

Oil gave back some ground. Brent was down US$1.70, or 1.63 per cent, at US$102.58 a barrel at 1200 GMT on Friday, Reuters said, after settling 4 per cent higher on Thursday on attacks on tankers. President Donald Trump said on Thursday that Washington was having productive discussions with Iran and that no attack was planned before the November 3 midterms. Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araqchi said Tehran was reviewing the US response to its proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days, Tasnim reported. FinancialJuice relayed an Iranian Revolutionary Guard report that a large LPG tanker was hit and caught fire; we have not confirmed it from a second outlet. Reuters added that Washington imposed fresh sanctions on Iran's shadow fleet.

Chalmers delays the ATO credit card ban to mid-2027, and the government drops a disaster-funding change, both after backlash

The federal government backed down twice on Friday. Treasurer Jim Chalmers said it had stepped in to delay the Tax Office's ban on paying tax bills by credit card until June 30, 2027, after small businesses complained they needed card payments to manage cash flow, the Herald, The Age, AAP and the Canberra Times reported. The ban follows the Reserve Bank's decision to outlaw card surcharges, and Tax Office commissioner Rob Heferen has said merchant fees would cost the office almost $200 million a year, according to AAP. Chalmers said the cost of the delay would be settled in the mid-year budget update in December, and the government signalled the extra funding would not run past next year. One report gave July 2027; June 30 is the date most outlets used. Opposition Leader Angus Taylor said Labor should scrap the ban rather than delay it, the ABC reported.

The second reversal involved disaster recovery funding. After a letter signed by all Queensland mayors and peak industry groups warned that the change would leave communities to cover a shortfall, Chalmers confirmed the government would keep its current cost-sharing arrangements, the ABC, the Herald and The Age reported. Herald columnists called the credit card retreat unedifying and said Chalmers had to swallow his pride. For households nothing changes today. For small businesses the delay buys a year, and the open question is what fee arrangement replaces it.

Maddinson's 33-ball duck on his Test return leaves Australia reeling on day one in Durban

Nic Maddinson, recalled for his first Test in almost a decade because of an injury to Cameron Green, was bowled by Marco Jansen for a duck after 33 balls on the first morning at Kingsmead, the Herald, The Age, Yahoo Sports and Code Sports reported. Code's Daniel Cherny described it as the longest duck by an Australian opener in Test history where balls faced are recorded. The Herald said he had earlier survived a South African blunder. Travis Head fell for 9, and the ABC and Fox Sports described the visitors as reeling after Steve Smith and Marnus Labuschagne were dismissed within four balls either side of lunch. We did not have the close-of-play score.

The selection has drawn questions. Maddinson was picked ahead of Cooper Connolly, and all eleven Australians were 30 or older, according to reports, while captain Pat Cummins said at the toss that the batter was settled in his game. The Herald asked what the pick says about the state of Australian cricket. Reports said Maddinson, who was treated for testicular cancer last year, had been out of first-class cricket for 579 days. Day two is today, and the Test runs to Tuesday.
